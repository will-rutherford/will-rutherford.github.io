S1 Question 1 — Gradient from directional derivatives [12 marks] · Ch 5

The rate of change of a given function \(f(x,y)\) at the point \(P_0=(1,2)\) in the direction towards \(P_1=(2,3)\) is \(2\sqrt2\). The rate of change of \(f(x,y)\) at the point \(P_0\) in the direction towards \(P_2=(1,0)\) is \(-3\).
6 marks(a) Determine \(\nabla f(P_0)\).
Worked solution
"Rate of change in the direction towards…" = directional derivative \(D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}\) (on the sheet). Two unknown components \((a,b)\) + two given rates → two linear equations.
Direction vectors (towards = head minus tail): $$\overrightarrow{P_0P_1}=(2,3)-(1,2)=(1,1),\qquad \overrightarrow{P_0P_2}=(1,0)-(1,2)=(0,-2).$$ Let \(\nabla f(1,2)=(a,b)\). $$D_{\mathbf u_1}f=(a,b)\cdot\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt2}=\frac{a+b}{\sqrt2}=2\sqrt2\;\Rightarrow\;a+b=4.\quad(1)$$ $$D_{\mathbf u_2}f=(a,b)\cdot\frac{(0,-2)}{2}=-b=-3\;\Rightarrow\;b=3.\quad(2)$$ From (1), \(a=1\).
\(\nabla f(P_0)=(1,3)\).
Official scheme: 2 marks for each directional-derivative equation, 2 marks for solving & the correct answer. Write each equation with the unit vector visible — that is where the first 4 marks live.
Forgetting to normalise. \((a,b)\cdot(1,1)=2\sqrt2\) gives \(a+b=2\sqrt2\) and everything downstream is wrong. Always divide by \(\|\mathbf u\|\) — here \(\sqrt2\) and \(2\).
3 marks(b) Determine the rate of change of \(f\) at \(P_0\) towards the origin \((0,0)\).
Worked solution
Same formula, new direction: \(\mathbf u=(0,0)-(1,2)=(-1,-2)\), \(\|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt5\).
$$D_{\mathbf u}f(P_0)=(1,3)\cdot\frac{(-1,-2)}{\sqrt5}=\frac{-1-6}{\sqrt5}=-\frac{7}{\sqrt5}.$$
\(D_{\mathbf u}f(P_0)=-\dfrac{7}{\sqrt5}=-\dfrac{7\sqrt5}{5}\) (so \(f\) decreases towards the origin).
Using \((1,2)\) (the position of \(P_0\)) as the direction. "Towards the origin" from \(P_0\) is \(O-P_0=(-1,-2)\). The scheme gives 1 mark just for the correct \(\mathbf u\).
3 marks(c) Determine the direction(s) where \(f\) does not change.
Worked solution
\(D_{\mathbf u}f=0\iff \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=0\iff \mathbf u\perp\nabla f\). In 2-D, \((p,q)\perp(-q,p)\).
\(f\) does not change in directions perpendicular to \(\nabla f(P_0)=(1,3)\): check \((1,3)\cdot(-3,1)=-3+3=0\).
Directions \(\pm(-3,1)\), i.e. \((-3,1)\) or \((3,-1)\) (unit: \(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(-3,1)\)).
Scheme: 1 mark for stating "direction of no change is perpendicular to grad f", 2 marks for both directions. Say the sentence, then give both signs.

S1 Question 2 — Limit, continuity, partial from the definition [12 marks] · Ch 4

Let \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) be given by $$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}0, & xy\ne0\\ 1, & xy=0\end{cases}.$$ (The paper writes \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\); it means \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\).)
5 marks(a) Determine \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\), if it exists. If the limit does not exist, explain why.
Worked solution
Piecewise function that is 1 on the axes and 0 elsewhere → two-path test: one path along an axis, one path off the axes.
Path 1: \(x=0\) (\(y\ne0\)). Then \(xy=0\cdot y=0\), so \(f(0,y)=1\). Hence \(f\to L_1=1\) along this path.
Path 2: \(y=x\) (\(x\ne0\)). Then \(xy=x^2\ne0\), so \(f(x,x)=0\). Hence \(f\to L_2=0\) along this path.
Since \(L_1\ne L_2\), the limit does not exist.
\(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) does not exist (DNE).
Scheme: 2 marks per path, 1 for the conclusion. Each path needs three things: the path equation, the value of \(f\) with the reason ("since \(xy=0\)"), and the limit along it.
2 marks(b) Determine whether \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\).
Worked solution
Continuity at a point = the limit exists and equals the function value.
\(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\) iff \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)=f(0,0)\). By (a) the limit does not exist, so this fails.
\(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).
Scheme: 1 mark for writing the definition of continuity, 1 for the conclusion. Don't skip the definition line — it's half the marks.
5 marks(c) Use the definition of the partial derivative to determine whether \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) exists at \((0,0)\).
Worked solution
"Use the definition" = write the difference quotient \(\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(0+h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}\). Rules of differentiation get zero marks here.
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0+h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}.$$ Along the \(x\)-axis \(xy=h\cdot0=0\), so \(f(h,0)=1\); also \(f(0,0)=1\): $$=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1-1}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}0=0.$$
\(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(0,0)\) exists and equals \(0\).
Scheme: 2 marks for the definition, 3 for working + answer. The lesson the examiner is testing: a function can have partial derivatives at a point without being continuous there — partials only look along the axes.
Writing \(f(h,0)=0\) because "\(h\ne0\)". The condition is on the product \(xy\), and \(h\cdot 0=0\), so \(f(h,0)=1\).

S1 Question 3 — Contour of a saddle; critical point [12 marks] · Ch 3 · Ch 6

Consider the surface given by $$f(x,y)=4y^2-x^2+2x.$$
8 marks(a) Sketch the contour which passes through \((x,y)=(1,1)\). Label any \(x\)-intercepts, \(y\)-intercepts and asymptotes of the contour.
Worked solution
Contour through a point: find the level \(c=f(1,1)\), then complete the square to get a conic in standard form. \(+y^2\) and \(-x^2\) with opposite signs → hyperbola.
Level: \(f(1,1)=4-1+2=5\), so the contour is \(4y^2-x^2+2x=5\).
Complete the square in \(x\): $$4y^2-(x^2-2x)=5\;\Rightarrow\;4y^2-\big((x-1)^2-1\big)=5\;\Rightarrow\;4y^2-(x-1)^2=4$$ $$\Rightarrow\;\frac{y^2}{1^2}-\frac{(x-1)^2}{2^2}=1.$$ Hyperbola centred at \((1,0)\) with \(a=2\) (in \(x\)), \(b=1\) (in \(y\)); the \(y^2\) term is positive, so it opens up and down, vertices \((1,\pm1)\).
Asymptotes: \(y=\pm\dfrac{b}{a}(x-1)=\pm\tfrac12(x-1)\).
\(x\)-intercepts (\(y=0\)): \(-\dfrac{(x-1)^2}{4}=1\) has no real solutions → none.
\(y\)-intercepts (\(x=0\)): \(4y^2=5\Rightarrow y=\pm\dfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\).
Contour \(\dfrac{y^2}{1}-\dfrac{(x-1)^2}{4}=1\): hyperbola, centre \((1,0)\), opening up/down; asymptotes \(y=\pm\tfrac12(x-1)\); no \(x\)-intercepts; \(y\)-intercepts \(\pm\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\).
Scheme: 3 marks working + standard form, 1 asymptotes, 1 "no \(x\)-intercepts", 1 \(y\)-intercepts, 2 sketch — "must label intercepts and asymptotes". Draw the asymptotes as dashed lines through the centre and write their equations on the sketch.
Dropping the \(+1\) when completing the square: \(-(x^2-2x)=-(x-1)^2+1\). Getting the sign wrong moves the right-hand side to 6 or gives an ellipse-looking equation.
4 marks(b) Find and classify the critical point(s) of \(f\).
Worked solution
Critical points: \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\). Classify with \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\) (sheet).
$$\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)=(-2x+2,\;8y)=(0,0)\;\Rightarrow\;x=1,\;y=0.$$ $$D=\begin{vmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}-2&0\\0&8\end{vmatrix}=-16\lt0.$$
One critical point \((1,0)\) with \(f(1,0)=1\); \(D=-16\lt0\) ⇒ saddle point.
The official answer also accepts a geometric argument: \(z-1=4y^2-(x-1)^2\) is a hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle) centred at \((1,0,1)\). Consistent with (a): the level curves of a saddle are hyperbolas.

S1 Question 4 — Chain rule with a tree diagram [12 marks] · Ch 5

Suppose that \(w=x^2+y^2-z^2\) and $$x=r\sin\phi\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\phi\sin\theta,\qquad z=r\cos\phi.$$
8 marks(a) With the aid of a tree diagram, use the chain rule to evaluate \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial r}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}\).
Worked solution
\(w\) depends on \(x,y,z\); each of those depends on \((r,\phi,\theta)\). For \(\partial/\partial r\) follow every branch ending in \(r\); for \(\partial/\partial\theta\) only the branches ending in \(\theta\) — \(z\) has no \(\theta\) branch.
w / | \ x y z / | \ / | \ / \ r φ θ r φ θ r φ $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial r} =2x\sin\phi\cos\theta+2y\sin\phi\sin\theta-2z\cos\phi$$ $$=2\big(r\sin^2\!\phi\cos^2\!\theta+r\sin^2\!\phi\sin^2\!\theta-r\cos^2\!\phi\big)=2r\big(\sin^2\!\phi-\cos^2\!\phi\big)=-2r\cos2\phi.$$ $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial \theta}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} =2x(-r\sin\phi\sin\theta)+2y(r\sin\phi\cos\theta)$$ $$=2r^2\big(-\sin^2\!\phi\sin\theta\cos\theta+\sin^2\!\phi\sin\theta\cos\theta\big)=0.$$
\(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial r}=-2r\cos2\phi\) (equivalently \(2r(\sin^2\phi-\cos^2\phi)\)), \(\qquad\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}=0\).
Scheme: 2 marks tree diagram; for each derivative 1 mark for the chain-rule formula and 2 for working + answer. Always substitute \(x,y,z\) back in terms of \(r,\phi,\theta\) — an answer left with \(x\)'s in it is incomplete.
Adding a \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial z}\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial\theta}\) term "for symmetry". It is zero, but writing a non-zero \(\partial z/\partial\theta\) is a common slip — \(z=r\cos\phi\) has no \(\theta\).
4 marks(b) Check your answers in (a) by first expressing \(w\) directly in terms of \(r\), \(\phi\) and \(\theta\), and then evaluating the partial derivatives.
Worked solution $$w=r^2\sin^2\!\phi\cos^2\!\theta+r^2\sin^2\!\phi\sin^2\!\theta-r^2\cos^2\!\phi=r^2\sin^2\!\phi-r^2\cos^2\!\phi=-r^2\cos2\phi.$$ $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}=-2r\cos2\phi,\qquad \frac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}=0\ \ (\text{no }\theta\text{ in }w).$$
Both agree with (a). ✓
Scheme: 2 marks for simplifying \(w\), 1 each for the derivatives. Geometric sanity check: \(x^2+y^2\) is distance² from the \(z\)-axis, which doesn't depend on the angle \(\theta\) around that axis.

S1 Question 5 — Global extrema on a closed square [13 marks] · Ch 6

In the plane, let \(S\) be the closed square region bounded by the lines \(x=0,\ x=1,\ y=0,\ y=1\). Consider the function $$f(x,y)=4x^2-3y^2+2xy.$$
7 marks(a) Determine the maximum and minimum values of \(f\) on the boundary of \(S\).
Worked solution
Closed bounded region → check each edge as a one-variable problem on a closed interval: compare stationary points inside the interval and the endpoints (corners).
(i) \(x=0,\ y\in[0,1]\): \(f(0,y)=-3y^2\). Max \(0\) at \((0,0)\); min \(-3\) at \((0,1)\).
(ii) \(x=1,\ y\in[0,1]\): \(g(y)=f(1,y)=4-3y^2+2y\), \(g'(y)=-6y+2=0\Rightarrow y=\tfrac13\in[0,1]\). \(g(0)=4,\ g(1)=3,\ g\!\left(\tfrac13\right)=4-\tfrac13+\tfrac23=\tfrac{13}{3}\). Max \(\tfrac{13}{3}\) at \((1,\tfrac13)\); min \(3\) at \((1,1)\).
(iii) \(y=0,\ x\in[0,1]\): \(f(x,0)=4x^2\). Max \(4\) at \((1,0)\); min \(0\) at \((0,0)\).
(iv) \(y=1,\ x\in[0,1]\): \(h(x)=4x^2-3+2x\), \(h'(x)=8x+2=0\Rightarrow x=-\tfrac14\notin[0,1]\) (reject). \(h(0)=-3,\ h(1)=3\). Max \(3\) at \((1,1)\); min \(-3\) at \((0,1)\).
On the boundary of \(S\): max \(f=\tfrac{13}{3}\) at \((1,\tfrac13)\); min \(f=-3\) at \((0,1)\).
Scheme: 1 + 2 + 1 + 2 marks for the four edges, 1 for the boxed conclusion. Draw the square and number the edges first — markers follow your structure.
Keeping \(x=-\tfrac14\) on edge (iv). A stationary point outside the edge's interval is not a candidate — say "reject, not in \([0,1]\)".
4 marks(b) Determine the critical point(s) of \(f\) in the interior of \(S\).
Worked solution $$\nabla f=(8x+2y,\;-6y+2x)=(0,0)\;\Rightarrow\;4x+y=0,\quad x-3y=0.$$ From the first, \(y=-4x\); substituting, \(x+12x=0\Rightarrow x=0,\ y=0\).
The only critical point is \((0,0)\), which lies on the boundary, not in the interior. So \(f\) has no critical points in the interior of \(S\).
Scheme: 1 mark \(\nabla f\), 2 marks solving, 1 mark for saying \((0,0)\) is not in the interior.
2 marks(c) Determine the global maximum and minimum values of \(f\) on \(S\).
Worked solution By the Extreme Value Theorem (\(f\) continuous on the closed, bounded set \(S\)), \(f\) attains a global max and min on \(S\), each at an interior critical point or on the boundary. There are no interior critical points, so they are the boundary values from (a).
Global max \(=\tfrac{13}{3}\) at \((1,\tfrac13)\); global min \(=-3\) at \((0,1)\).
Scheme: 1 mark for stating the EVT, 1 for the values. Name the theorem explicitly.

S1 Question 6 — Lagrange multipliers on the sphere [12 marks] · Ch 6

12 marks Epsilon, our pet ant, walks on the unit sphere centred at \((0,0,0)\). The temperature at a point \((x,y,z)\) on the sphere is \(T(x,y,z)=400xyz^2\). Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the highest and lowest temperatures encountered by Epsilon.
Worked solution
Extremise \(T\) subject to \(g=x^2+y^2+z^2-1=0\). The sphere is closed and bounded, so the max and min exist and occur among the Lagrange points.
Formulation: max/min \(T=400xyz^2\) subject to \(g(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-1=0\).
Lagrange equations \(\nabla T=\lambda\nabla g\): $$400yz^2=2\lambda x\ (1),\qquad 400xz^2=2\lambda y\ (2),\qquad 800xyz=2\lambda z\ (3).$$ Special cases: if \(\lambda=0\) then at least one of \(x,y,z\) is \(0\), and if any of \(x,y,z=0\) then \(T=0\). These give \(T=0\) only.
Main case \(\lambda\ne0,\ x,y,z\ne0\): (2) ÷ (1): \(\dfrac{x}{y}=\dfrac{y}{x}\Rightarrow x^2=y^2\). (3) ÷ (2): \(\dfrac{2y}{z}=\dfrac{z}{y}\Rightarrow z^2=2y^2\). Constraint: \(y^2+y^2+2y^2=1\Rightarrow y^2=\tfrac14\), so \(y=\pm\tfrac12,\ x=\pm\tfrac12,\ z=\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\). $$T=400\,xy\,z^2=400\,xy\cdot\tfrac12=200\,xy,\qquad xy=\pm\tfrac14.$$
Highest temperature \(=400\left(\tfrac12\right)\left(\tfrac12\right)\left(\tfrac12\right)=50\) (when \(x,y\) have the same sign); lowest \(=-50\) (when \(x,y\) have opposite signs). \(z=\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\) either way.
Scheme: 2 formulation, 2 Lagrange equations, 2 for considering the special cases \(\lambda=0\) / a coordinate \(=0\), 4 for the working, 1 each for max and min. The special-case sentence is worth 2 marks — it's also what justifies dividing the equations.
Dividing (1) by (2) without saying \(x,y,z,\lambda\ne0\). Dividing by something that could be zero loses the special-case marks and can lose solutions.

S1 Question 7 — Two flying insects [13 marks] · Ch 7

The velocities of Atom Ant and Billy Bug at time \(t\ge0\) are given respectively by $$\mathbf v_A=6\sin(3t)\,\mathbf i+6\cos(3t)\,\mathbf j+0\,\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf v_B=\cos(t)\,\mathbf i-\sin(t)\,\mathbf j+0\,\mathbf k.$$ Atom Ant and Billy Bug are initially at \((-2,0,2)\) and \((0,1,-1)\) respectively. You may assume Atom Ant and Billy Bug can fly.
4 marks(a) Determine the position vectors of Atom Ant and Billy Bug at time \(t\).
Worked solution
Position = integral of velocity, one constant per component, fixed by the initial position.
$$\mathbf r_A(t)=\left(-2\cos3t+a_1,\;2\sin3t+a_2,\;a_3\right),\quad \mathbf r_A(0)=(-2+a_1,\,a_2,\,a_3)=(-2,0,2)\Rightarrow a_1=a_2=0,\ a_3=2.$$ $$\mathbf r_B(t)=\left(\sin t+b_1,\;\cos t+b_2,\;b_3\right),\quad \mathbf r_B(0)=(b_1,\,1+b_2,\,b_3)=(0,1,-1)\Rightarrow b_1=b_2=0,\ b_3=-1.$$
\(\mathbf r_A(t)=(-2\cos3t,\;2\sin3t,\;2)\), \(\qquad\mathbf r_B(t)=(\sin t,\;\cos t,\;-1)\).
Setting the constant equal to the initial coordinate. \(\int 6\sin3t\,dt=-2\cos3t\), which is \(-2\) at \(t=0\) — so \(a_1=0\), not \(-2\). Always evaluate at \(t=0\) and solve.
7 marks(b) Show that at time \(t\), the distance between Atom Ant and Billy Bug is \(\sqrt{14-4\sin(2t)}\) (hint: \(\sin(x-y)=\sin x\cos y-\cos x\sin y\)). Determine when Atom Ant is 4 metres away from Billy Bug for the first time.
Worked solution $$\|\mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B\|=\big\|(-2\cos3t-\sin t,\;2\sin3t-\cos t,\;3)\big\|$$ $$=\sqrt{(2\cos3t+\sin t)^2+(2\sin3t-\cos t)^2+9}$$ $$=\sqrt{4\cos^2 3t+4\cos3t\sin t+\sin^2 t+4\sin^2 3t-4\sin3t\cos t+\cos^2 t+9}$$ $$=\sqrt{4+1+9+4(\cos3t\sin t-\sin3t\cos t)}=\sqrt{14-4\sin(3t-t)}=\sqrt{14-4\sin2t}.$$ Distance 4: \(\sqrt{14-4\sin2t}=4\Rightarrow 14-4\sin2t=16\Rightarrow\sin2t=-\tfrac12\). For \(t\ge0\), \(2t\ge0\); the first non-negative angle with sine \(-\tfrac12\) is \(2t=\tfrac{7\pi}{6}\).
Distance \(=\sqrt{14-4\sin2t}\) ✓; first time at distance 4: \(t=\dfrac{7\pi}{12}\).
Scheme: 1 mark for \(\|\mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B\|\) set up, 3 marks for the simplification, 1 for setting \(=4\), 2 for \(t\). Note \(\cos3t\sin t-\sin3t\cos t=-\sin(3t-t)\) — match it to the hint with the sign.
Taking \(2t=-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\) (the calculator-style principal value — negative time) or \(2t=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\) (where \(\sin\) is \(+\tfrac12\)). Sine is negative in quadrants III/IV; the first positive angle is \(\pi+\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\tfrac{7\pi}{6}\).
2 marks(c) Determine the position of Atom Ant when it is 4 metres away from Billy Bug for the first time.
Worked solution At \(t=\tfrac{7\pi}{12}\), \(3t=\tfrac{7\pi}{4}\): \(\cos\tfrac{7\pi}{4}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \sin\tfrac{7\pi}{4}=-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\). $$\mathbf r_A\!\left(\tfrac{7\pi}{12}\right)=\left(-2\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\;2\cdot\left(-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right),\;2\right)=(-\sqrt2,\,-\sqrt2,\,2).$$
Atom Ant is at \((-\sqrt2,\,-\sqrt2,\,2)\).

S1 Question 8 — Conservative field, potential, work [14 marks] · Ch 7

Let \(\mathbf F(x,y)=e^y\,\mathbf i+xe^y\,\mathbf j\) be a force field in \(\mathbb R^2\), and let \(C\) be the path parametrised by $$\mathbf r(t)=\left(\cos t,\ \tfrac12\sin t\right),\qquad t\in[0,\pi].$$
3 marks(a) Sketch the path \(C\). Indicate the starting point and the direction of travel.
Worked solution
Eliminate \(t\) with \(\sin^2t+\cos^2t=1\), then use the \(t\)-range to decide which part of the curve and which way.
\(x=\cos t,\ y=\tfrac12\sin t\Rightarrow \sin t=2y\), so \(4y^2+x^2=1\), i.e. \(\dfrac{x^2}{1^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{(1/2)^2}=1\): ellipse centred at \((0,0)\), \(a=1,\ b=\tfrac12\). For \(t\in[0,\pi]\), \(y=\tfrac12\sin t\ge0\): the upper half, from \((1,0)\) at \(t=0\) through \((0,\tfrac12)\) to \((-1,0)\) at \(t=\pi\) — anticlockwise.
Upper half of \(x^2+4y^2=1\), starting at \((1,0)\), travelling anticlockwise to \((-1,0)\).
Scheme: 1 mark for the equation, 2 for the path — "must indicate start point and direction of travel". An arrow and "t = 0" at \((1,0)\) are compulsory.
2 marks(b) Show that \(\mathbf F\) is a conservative field.
Worked solution \(P=e^y,\ Q=xe^y\): $$\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=e^y,\qquad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}=e^y.$$
Since \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\) on all of \(\mathbb R^2\), \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
3 marks(c) Determine a potential function for \(\mathbf F\).
Worked solution Want \(\nabla f=\mathbf F\): \(f_x=e^y\ (1)\), \(f_y=xe^y\ (2)\). From (1): \(f(x,y)=xe^y+g(y)\). Then \(f_y=xe^y+g'(y)\); compare with (2): \(g'(y)=0\Rightarrow g(y)=K\). Take \(K=0\).
\(f(x,y)=xe^y\) (any \(xe^y+K\) works).
Scheme: 1 mark for writing the two equations, 2 for working + potential. Check: \(\nabla(xe^y)=(e^y,xe^y)\) ✓.
2 marks(d) Use the potential function in part (c) to determine the work done by \(\mathbf F\) on a particle as it moves along the path \(C\) from \(t=0\) to \(t=\pi\).
Worked solution Fundamental theorem for line integrals: \(W=f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\), with \(\mathbf r(0)=(1,0)\), \(\mathbf r(\pi)=(-1,0)\). $$W=\big[xe^y\big]_{(1,0)}^{(-1,0)}=(-1)e^0-(1)e^0=-2.$$
\(W=-2\).
(e) Let \(C_1\) be the straight line path from \((1,0)\) to \((-1,0)\).
2 marksi. Write a parametrisation for \(C_1\).
Worked solution
\(\mathbf r_1(t)=(1-t,\ 0),\ t\in[0,2]\).
Any correct one earns the marks, e.g. \((1-2t,0),\ t\in[0,1]\). State the \(t\)-interval — it's part of the parametrisation.
2 marksii. Use the formula \(W=\displaystyle\int_a^b\mathbf F(t)\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\) to determine the work done by \(\mathbf F\) on a particle as it moves along the path \(C_1\).
Worked solution \(\mathbf F(\mathbf r_1(t))=(e^0,\,(1-t)e^0)=(1,\,1-t)\), \(\mathbf r_1'(t)=(-1,0)\). $$W=\int_0^2(1,\,1-t)\cdot(-1,0)\,dt=\int_0^2-1\,dt=\big[-t\big]_0^2=-2.$$
\(W=-2\) — agrees with (d), as it must for a conservative field (path independence).

S2 Question 1 — Contours and a cross-section [12 marks] · Ch 3

Consider the surface $$z=\sqrt{x+y^2}-3.$$
6 marks(a) Sketch the contour curves for \(z=-2\), \(z=0\), and \(z=1\) on the same set of axes. Clearly label all intercepts with the coordinate axes for each contour.
Worked solution
Set \(z=c\), isolate the root, square. Squaring is only reversible when \(c+3\ge0\) — note the range of \(z\) first.
\(z+3=\sqrt{x+y^2}\ge0\), so \(z\ge-3\) (for \(z\lt-3\) there is no intersection with the surface). For \(z=c\ge-3\): $$(c+3)^2=x+y^2\iff x=(c+3)^2-y^2.$$ Each contour is a parabola opening in the negative \(x\)-direction, vertex \(((c+3)^2,0)\).
\(z\)ContourVertex / \(x\)-intercept\(y\)-intercepts
\(-2\)\(x=1-y^2\)\((1,0)\)\(y=\pm1\)
\(0\)\(x=9-y^2\)\((9,0)\)\(y=\pm3\)
\(1\)\(x=16-y^2\)\((16,0)\)\(y=\pm4\)
Three nested leftward parabolas \(x=1-y^2,\ x=9-y^2,\ x=16-y^2\), each labelled with its \(z\)-value and intercepts \((1,0),(0,\pm1)\); \((9,0),(0,\pm3)\); \((16,0),(0,\pm4)\).
Label each curve with its \(z\)-value — a contour diagram without level labels is just a picture of parabolas.
6 marks(b) Show that the cross-sections of the surface for \(y=k\) are parabolas. Sketch the cross-section for \(k=2\), indicating its vertex and intercepts.
Worked solution
Vertical cross-section: fix \(y=k\), get a curve in the \(xz\)-plane, rearrange into a known conic.
With \(y=k\): \(z=\sqrt{x+k^2}-3\Rightarrow (z+3)^2=x+k^2\), i.e. \(x=(z+3)^2-k^2\). For every \(k\) this is a parabola in the \(xz\)-plane with vertex \((x,z)=(-k^2,-3)\), opening in the \(+x\) direction. (Because \(z+3\ge0\), the surface contains the upper half, \(z\ge-3\).)
\(k=2\): \((z+3)^2=x+4\), vertex \((-4,-3)\). \(z\)-intercept (\(x=0\)): \((z+3)^2=4\Rightarrow z+3=2\Rightarrow z=-1\). \(x\)-intercept (\(z=0\)): \(9=x+4\Rightarrow x=5\).
Cross-sections \((z+3)^2=x+k^2\): parabolas, vertex \((-k^2,-3)\). For \(k=2\): \(z=-3+\sqrt{x+4}\), vertex \((-4,-3)\), intercepts \((0,-1)\) and \((5,0)\).
The official solution draws the whole parabola \((z+3)^2=x+4\) and lists \(z=-5\) as a second \(z\)-intercept. But \(z=\sqrt{\cdots}-3\ge-3\), so \(z=-5\) is not on the surface. Safest exam answer: show the parabola equation (that's what "show they are parabolas" wants), then sketch the branch \(z\ge-3\) and say the lower branch is excluded because the square root is non-negative. That can't lose a mark and shows you understand the domain.

S2 Question 2 — Tangent plane & linear approximation [12 marks] · Ch 4

Let \(f(x,y)=\ln(1+x^2y)\), with domain \(D=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:\ y\ge0\}\).
4 marks(a) Determine the partial derivatives \(f_x(x,y)\) and \(f_y(x,y)\).
Worked solution
Chain rule for \(\ln(\text{inside})\): derivative \(=\dfrac{\text{(inside)}'}{\text{inside}}\), holding the other variable constant.
$$f_x=\frac{1}{1+x^2y}\cdot2xy=\frac{2xy}{1+x^2y},\qquad f_y=\frac{1}{1+x^2y}\cdot x^2=\frac{x^2}{1+x^2y}.$$
\(f_x=\dfrac{2xy}{1+x^2y},\qquad f_y=\dfrac{x^2}{1+x^2y}\).
4 marks(b) Determine the equation of the tangent plane to the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) at the point \((-1,1)\).
Worked solution
Tangent plane = linear approximation \(P(x,y)\) from the sheet: \(z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)\).
$$f(-1,1)=\ln(1+1)=\ln2,\quad f_x(-1,1)=\frac{2(-1)(1)}{2}=-1,\quad f_y(-1,1)=\frac{1}{2}.$$
\(z=\ln2-(x+1)+\tfrac12(y-1)\).
Leave it in point-slope form — expanding invites arithmetic slips and earns nothing extra.
4 marks(c) Use the linear approximation for \(f(x,y)\) to estimate \(f(-0.95,0.95)\). Hint: You may use \(\ln2\approx0.6931\).
Worked solution Here \(x+1=0.05\), \(y-1=-0.05\): $$f(-0.95,0.95)\approx\ln2-0.05+\tfrac12(-0.05)=\ln2-0.075\approx0.6931-0.075=0.6181.$$
\(f(-0.95,0.95)\approx\ln2-0.075\approx0.6181\).
Sanity check (not needed in the exam): the true value is \(\ln(1+0.95^3)\approx0.6192\), so the estimate is off by about 0.001 — good, because \((-0.95,0.95)\) is close to \((-1,1)\).
Sign of \(x-a\): \(x-a=-0.95-(-1)=+0.05\). Getting \(-0.05\) here flips the first term and gives \(0.7181\).

S2 Question 3 — Chain-rule identity & greatest change [10 marks] · Ch 5

5 marks(a) Let \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) be a differentiable function, and let \(g:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be given by \(g(x,y)=f(ax+by)\), for some \(a,b\in\mathbb R\). Show that $$a\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\right)=b\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\right).$$
Worked solution
Abstract \(f\) of a linear combination → introduce \(u=ax+by\), so \(g=f(u)\), and chain-rule each partial.
Let \(u=ax+by\), so \(g(x,y)=f(u)\). Then $$\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=\frac{df}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=a\,f'(u)\quad(1),\qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}=\frac{df}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=b\,f'(u)\quad(2).$$ Therefore $$a\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\overset{(2)}{=}a\,b\,f'(u)=b\,\big(a\,f'(u)\big)\overset{(1)}{=}b\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}.\qquad\blacksquare$$
In a "show that", start from one side and end at the other, labelling which result you used at each step. Don't start by writing the identity you're proving.
5 marks(b) Determine the directions of greatest change of the function $$g(x,y)=e^{5x+y}\cos\!\big((5x+y)^2\big)$$ at any point in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane.
Worked solution
Greatest change is along \(\pm\nabla g\). Spot that \(g=f(5x+y)\) with \(f(u)=e^u\cos(u^2)\) — this is part (a) with \(a=5,\ b=1\). No need to differentiate the monster.
From (a) with \(a=5,\ b=1\): \(5\,g_y=g_x\). So $$\nabla g=(g_x,\,g_y)=(5g_y,\,g_y)=g_y\,(5,1),$$ where \(g_y=f'(u)=e^{u}\big(\cos(u^2)-2u\sin(u^2)\big)\), \(u=5x+y\). So at every point, \(\nabla g\) is parallel to \((5,1)\).
Directions of greatest change: \(\pm(5,1)\), unit \(\pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{26}}(5,1)\). Greatest increase is along \((5,1)\) where \(g_y\gt0\) and along \(-(5,1)\) where \(g_y\lt0\).
Reading "directions" as one direction. Greatest change is both greatest increase and greatest decrease: \(\pm\nabla g\). (Where \(g_y=0\) the gradient vanishes and no direction is preferred — worth one line if you want to be complete.)

S2 Question 4 — Continuity via a parabolic path [12 marks] · Ch 4

Let $$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^6}{(x^2-y)^2+x^6}, & \text{if }(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[2mm] 0, & \text{if }(x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}$$
7 marks(a) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? Justify your answer.
Worked solution
The \((x^2-y)\) in the denominator is a flag: along the parabola \(y=x^2\) that term vanishes. Two-path test with one straight path and the parabola.
Path 1: \(x=0\) (\(y\ne0\)): \(f(0,y)=\dfrac{0}{y^2+0}=0\), so \(L_1=0\).
Path 2: \(y=x^2\) (\(x\ne0\)): \(f(x,x^2)=\dfrac{x^6}{(x^2-x^2)^2+x^6}=\dfrac{x^6}{x^6}=1\), so \(L_2=1\).
Since \(L_1\ne L_2\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) does not exist, so it cannot equal \(f(0,0)=0\).
\(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).
Testing only straight lines. Along every line \(y=mx\), \(f=\dfrac{x^6}{x^2(x-m)^2+x^6}\to0\) (and along \(x=0\) too), so a student who tries only lines wrongly concludes the limit is 0. The \(x^2-y\) is telling you to try \(y=x^2\).
5 marks(b) Determine whether the partial derivatives \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) exist. Justify your answer.
Worked solution
At the gluing point of a piecewise function, partials come from the definition.
$$f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\dfrac{h^6}{h^4+h^6}-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h^6}{(1+h^2)h^5}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h}{1+h^2}=0.$$ $$f_y(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{0-0}{h}=0.$$
Both exist: \(f_x(0,0)=0\) and \(f_y(0,0)=0\) — even though \(f\) is not continuous there.
Same moral as S1 Q2: existence of partials ≠ continuity (≠ differentiability). Examiners love this pairing.

S2 Question 5 — Lagrange multipliers on a plane [12 marks] · Ch 6

12 marks Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the maximum value of the function $$f(x,y,z)=xyz^2$$ on the plane defined by \(x+y+z=20\).
Worked solution
One constraint \(g=x+y+z=20\), \(\nabla g=(1,1,1)\) → each component of \(\nabla f\) equals the same \(\lambda\). Equate pairs.
Set-up: maximise \(f=xyz^2\) subject to \(g(x,y,z)=x+y+z=20\). Solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\), \(g=20\): $$\nabla f=(yz^2,\;xz^2,\;2xyz),\qquad \nabla g=(1,1,1).$$ $$yz^2=\lambda\ (1),\qquad xz^2=\lambda\ (2),\qquad 2xyz=\lambda\ (3).$$ Special cases: if \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\) is \(0\) then \(f=0\), which is not the maximum (e.g. \(f(5,5,10)\gt0\)). So assume \(x,y,z\ne0\).
(1) = (2): \(yz^2=xz^2\Rightarrow y=x\) (since \(z\ne0\)).
(1) = (3): \(yz^2=2xyz\Rightarrow z=2x\) (dividing by \(yz\ne0\)).
Constraint: \(x+x+2x=20\Rightarrow x=5\), so \(y=5,\ z=10\). $$f(5,5,10)=5\cdot5\cdot10^2=2500.$$
Maximum value \(=2500\), at \((5,5,10)\).
Mark-earning structure (mirrors the S1 scheme): formulation → gradient equations → special cases → solve → evaluate → conclusion sentence.
Rigour footnote the paper glosses over: on the whole plane \(f\) is unbounded (take \(x=y=-N,\ z=20+2N\): \(f=N^2(20+2N)^2\to\infty\)). The intended answer 2500 is the maximum among Lagrange points, and is the true maximum on the part of the plane with \(x,y,z\ge0\). Give 2500; if you have time, add "(for \(x,y,z\ge0\))".

S2 Question 6 — Critical points of \((y^2+2x-3)e^{-2x}\) [12 marks] · Ch 6

12 marks Determine and classify all critical points of the function $$h(x,y)=(y^2+2x-3)e^{-2x}.$$
Worked solution
Product rule in \(x\); factor out \(e^{-2x}\ne0\) so the equations become polynomial. Classify with \(D\).
First partials: $$h_x=2e^{-2x}+(y^2+2x-3)(-2)e^{-2x}=e^{-2x}\big(-2y^2-4x+8\big),\qquad h_y=2y\,e^{-2x}.$$ Critical points: \(h_y=0\Rightarrow y=0\) (as \(e^{-2x}\ne0\)). Then \(h_x=e^{-2x}(-4x+8)=0\Rightarrow x=2\). Only critical point: \((2,0)\).
Second partials: $$h_{xx}=-2e^{-2x}(-2y^2-4x+8)+e^{-2x}(-4)=e^{-2x}(4y^2+8x-20),\quad h_{yy}=2e^{-2x},\quad h_{xy}=-4y\,e^{-2x}.$$ At \((2,0)\): \(h_{xx}=-4e^{-4},\ h_{yy}=2e^{-4},\ h_{xy}=0\). $$D(2,0)=h_{xx}h_{yy}-h_{xy}^2=(-4e^{-4})(2e^{-4})-0=-8e^{-8}\lt0.$$
One critical point, \((2,0)\), with \(h(2,0)=e^{-4}\); \(D=-8e^{-8}\lt0\) ⇒ saddle point.
Cancelling \(e^{-2x}\) without saying why. Write "since \(e^{-2x}\gt0\) for all \(x\)" — the "justify all answers" instruction applies to this step too.

S2 Question 7 — The cliff diver [12 marks] · Ch 7

A diver jumps from a cliff 40 metres above sea level, starting at position \((0,0,40)\). The diver pushes off horizontally with an initial velocity \(\vec v_0=(2,0,0)\), and the acceleration due to gravity is \(\vec a=(0,0,-9.8)\).
5 marks(a) Determine the position vector of the diver at time \(t\).
Worked solution
Integrate \(\mathbf a\to\mathbf v\) (constants from \(\mathbf v_0\)), then \(\mathbf v\to\mathbf r\) (constants from \(\mathbf r_0\)).
$$\mathbf v(t)=\int(0,0,-9.8)\,dt=(c_1,\,c_2,\,-9.8t+c_3),\quad \mathbf v(0)=(2,0,0)\Rightarrow \mathbf v(t)=(2,\,0,\,-9.8t).$$ $$\mathbf r(t)=\int\mathbf v\,dt=(2t+c_4,\,c_5,\,-4.9t^2+c_6),\quad \mathbf r(0)=(0,0,40)\Rightarrow c_4=c_5=0,\ c_6=40.$$
\(\mathbf r(t)=(2t,\;0,\;40-4.9t^2)\).
Show both integration stages and both sets of constants — they carry most of the 5 marks.
4 marks(b) Determine the time it takes for the diver to hit the water.
Worked solution Sea level is \(z=0\): \(40-4.9t^2=0\Rightarrow t^2=\dfrac{40}{4.9}=\dfrac{400}{49}\Rightarrow t=\dfrac{20}{7}\) (taking \(t\gt0\)).
\(t=\dfrac{20}{7}\approx2.86\) s.
No calculator: turn \(4.9\) into \(\tfrac{49}{10}\) and the square root comes out exactly. Reject the negative root in words.
3 marks(c) Determine the diver's position at the moment they hit the water.
Worked solution $$\mathbf r\!\left(\tfrac{20}{7}\right)=\left(\tfrac{40}{7},\;0,\;40-\tfrac{49}{10}\cdot\tfrac{400}{49}\right)=\left(\tfrac{40}{7},\,0,\,0\right).$$
\(\left(\tfrac{40}{7},\,0,\,0\right)\) — about 5.71 m out from the base of the cliff.

S2 Question 8 — Conservative field on a wild path [18 marks] · Ch 7

Consider the force field $$\mathbf F(x,y)=(2x\cos y,\;-x^2\sin y).$$
5 marks(a) Show that the force field \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
Worked solution
\(\mathbf F=(P,Q)\) on all of \(\mathbb R^2\) is conservative if \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\).
$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2x\cos y)=-2x\sin y,\qquad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-x^2\sin y)=-2x\sin y.$$
The cross-partials agree everywhere in \(\mathbb R^2\), so \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
For 5 marks, state the test in words, compute both partials, and write the conclusion — mention that \(\mathbb R^2\) (the domain) has no holes.
7 marks(b) Determine a potential function \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) such that \(\mathbf F=\nabla f\).
Worked solution Need \(f_x=2x\cos y\) and \(f_y=-x^2\sin y\). Integrate the first in \(x\): \(f(x,y)=x^2\cos y+\varphi(y)\). Differentiate in \(y\): \(f_y=-x^2\sin y+\varphi'(y)\). Match with \(-x^2\sin y\): \(\varphi'(y)=0\Rightarrow\varphi(y)=C\). Take \(C=0\).
\(f(x,y)=x^2\cos y\) (plus any constant).
Writing "+C" instead of "+\(\varphi(y)\)" after integrating in \(x\). The "constant" can depend on \(y\) — that's exactly what the second equation pins down.
6 marks(c) Calculate the work done by the force field \(\mathbf F\) when moving a particle along the path defined by $$x(t)=t\cos(\pi t),\qquad y(t)=2t^2\sin(\pi t),\qquad 0\le t\le3.$$
Worked solution
Conservative field + ugly path ⇒ never integrate along the path. Work = potential(end) − potential(start).
Endpoints: \(\mathbf r(0)=(0,0)\); \(\mathbf r(3)=(3\cos3\pi,\ 18\sin3\pi)=(-3,0)\). $$W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\mathbf r(3))-f(\mathbf r(0))=f(-3,0)-f(0,0)=(-3)^2\cos0-0=9.$$
\(W=9\).
Justify with one sentence: "Since \(\mathbf F=\nabla f\) is conservative, by the fundamental theorem for line integrals the work depends only on the endpoints." (Verified numerically: the brute-force integral along this path is also 9.)
\(\cos3\pi=-1\), not \(1\). An odd multiple of \(\pi\) — the endpoint is \((-3,0)\). Here \(x^2\) hides the sign, but get the endpoint right anyway.

What 2025 teaches

Patterns across both papers

  • No ODEs at all in 2025. Both papers are pure multivariable calculus. Still drill Ch 1–Ch 2 (older papers use them), but put most of your time into Ch 3–7.
  • Optimisation is about a quarter of each paper: a Lagrange question in both (S1 Q6, S2 Q5), plus classification by \(D\) (S1 Q3b, S2 Q6) and a closed-region global extremum (S1 Q5).
  • "Limits + partials from the definition" came up twice (S1 Q2, S2 Q4), both times with the same twist: the function is discontinuous but the partials exist. Know the two-path test and the \(h\)-limit definition cold.
  • Conservative field → potential → work came up twice (S1 Q8, S2 Q8: 32 marks together). The routine is always \(P_y=Q_x\) → integrate \(f_x\), fix \(\varphi(y)\) → \(f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\).
  • Integrate \(\mathbf a\to\mathbf v\to\mathbf r\) with initial conditions in both (S1 Q7, S2 Q7). Easy marks if you handle one constant per component.
  • Sketching is worth 8–12 marks per paper: complete the square → standard form → label every intercept and asymptote. Watch domain restrictions from square roots (S2 Q1).
  • Chain rule "show that" (S2 Q3a) and tree diagrams (S1 Q4): state the substitution \(u=ax+by\) or draw the tree; markers give explicit marks for it.

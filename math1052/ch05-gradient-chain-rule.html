§1 · Concepts in depth

Rusty on the single-variable chain rule \(\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x))=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\), implicit differentiation of \(x^2+y^2=25\), dot products or unit vectors? Read the Ground Floor first. And this chapter leans on partial derivatives from Chapter 4 — if \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) aren't automatic yet, drill those first.

The gradient vector \(\nabla f\) — all the partials in one arrow

For \(z=f(x,y)\), the gradient collects the two partial derivatives into a single vector (Summary p 5):

$$\nabla f(x,y)=\big(f_x(x,y),\,f_y(x,y)\big)=f_x\,\mathbf i+f_y\,\mathbf j,\qquad \nabla f(x,y,z)=\big(f_x,\,f_y,\,f_z\big)\ \text{in 3-D.}$$

Three facts to lock in straight away:

  • \(\nabla f\) lives in the domain — the \(xy\)-plane (the "map"), not on the surface. At each point \((a,b)\) you draw a 2-D arrow \(\nabla f(a,b)\). For \(f(x,y,z)\) the gradient is a 3-D arrow in \(xyz\)-space.
  • \(\nabla f\) is a function of position. \(\nabla f(x,y)\) is a formula; \(\nabla f(a,b)\) is a specific vector of numbers. Questions ask for one or the other — "Determine \(\nabla f(x,y)\)" (2024 S2 Q4a) wants the formula; "Determine \(\nabla f(P_0)\)" (2025 S1 Q1a) wants numbers.
  • Give it as a vector. \((-9,-3)\) or \(-9\mathbf i-3\mathbf j\). Never as a single number — that would be a rate, which is a different thing.

🧭 Directional derivative — the slope when you walk in any direction

\(f_x(a,b)\) is the slope of the surface if you walk due east (direction \(\mathbf i\)); \(f_y(a,b)\) is the slope walking due north (\(\mathbf j\)). But you can walk in any compass direction. The directional derivative in the direction of a unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf u}=(u_1,u_2)\) is

$$D_{\hat{\mathbf u}}f(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+hu_1,\,b+hu_2)-f(a,b)}{h}.$$

It measures "change in \(f\) per unit distance travelled in the \(xy\)-plane". That is why \(\hat{\mathbf u}\) must have length 1: if you used \(2\hat{\mathbf u}\) you'd travel two units per step and double the answer.

Why it collapses to a dot product. Walk along the line \(x=a+hu_1,\ y=b+hu_2\) and let \(g(h)=f(a+hu_1,b+hu_2)\). The directional derivative is just \(g'(0)\), and the chain rule (below) gives

$$g'(0)=f_x\,\frac{dx}{dh}+f_y\,\frac{dy}{dh}=f_x u_1+f_y u_2=\nabla f(a,b)\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}.$$

So the formula on the sheet, \(D_{\mathbf u}f(a,b)=\nabla f(a,b)\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}\), is not a new idea — it is the chain rule applied to a straight-line walk. Notation in papers varies: \(f_{\mathbf u}\), \(f_{\mathbf v}\), \(D_{\mathbf u}f\) all mean the same thing.

"Rate of change of \(f\) at \(P\) in the direction of \(\mathbf v\)", "slope in the direction of \(\mathbf v\)", "rate of change with respect to distance" — all mean directional derivative with the unit vector \(\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\). The answer is a number (a scalar).

🧭 The \(\cos\theta\) picture — why \(\nabla f\) points uphill

Write the dot product geometrically, with \(\theta\) the angle between \(\nabla f\) and your walking direction \(\hat{\mathbf u}\):

$$D_{\hat{\mathbf u}}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=\|\nabla f\|\,\|\hat{\mathbf u}\|\cos\theta=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta .$$

Everything in this chapter's "direction" questions falls out of this one line. \(\|\nabla f\|\) is fixed at the point; only \(\cos\theta\) changes as you turn, and \(\cos\theta\) ranges over \([-1,1]\):

Four exam consequences, each worth marks on its own:

  1. Direction of greatest increase \(=\nabla f(P)\); maximum rate \(=\|\nabla f(P)\|\).
  2. Direction of greatest decrease \(=-\nabla f(P)\); the rate there is \(-\|\nabla f(P)\|\) (i.e. \(f\) drops at \(\|\nabla f(P)\|\) per unit distance).
  3. Directions of no change: the vectors perpendicular to \(\nabla f(P)\). In 2-D, if \(\nabla f=(p,q)\) they are \(\pm(-q,p)\) — two opposite directions. In 3-D there is a whole plane of them.
  4. Reverse problems: if you are told \(D_{\mathbf v}f=-\|\nabla f\|\), then \(\cos\theta=-1\), so \(\nabla f\) points exactly opposite to \(\mathbf v\) (this is 2022 S1 Q6).

🧭 Why \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the level curves

This is the single most useful geometric fact in the chapter (Summary p 5: "\(\nabla f(a,b)\) is perpendicular to the contour line through \((a,b)\) and points in the direction of increasing \(f\)").

Proof in two lines. Let \(\mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t))\) trace out the contour \(f(x,y)=c\) through \((a,b)\). Along it \(f(x(t),y(t))=c\) for every \(t\). Differentiate with the chain rule:

$$f_x\frac{dx}{dt}+f_y\frac{dy}{dt}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=0 .$$

\(\mathbf r'(t)\) is the tangent vector to the contour, so \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the contour's tangent — i.e. normal to the level curve. Intuitively: walking along a contour keeps \(f\) constant, so the rate of change is 0, so by the \(\cos\theta\) picture you must be walking at \(90^\circ\) to \(\nabla f\).

Two corollaries the exam uses constantly:

  • Tangent line to a level curve \(F(x,y)=c\) at \((a,b)\): the normal is \(\nabla F(a,b)\), so the line is $$F_x(a,b)(x-a)+F_y(a,b)(y-b)=0.$$
  • Tangent plane to a level surface \(F(x,y,z)=c\) at \((a,b,c_0)\): exactly the same idea one dimension up, $$F_x(x-a)+F_y(y-b)+F_z(z-c_0)=0\quad(\text{partials at the point}).$$ A graph \(z=f(x,y)\) is the level surface \(F=f(x,y)-z=0\), whose normal is \((f_x,f_y,-1)\) — exactly the tangent-plane normal from Chapter 4 (this is Tutorial 8 Q5).

Contours bunched close together = large \(\|\nabla f\|\) = steep. Widely spaced contours = gentle slope. A marker loves the sentence "the gradient is perpendicular to the contour and points towards increasing \(f\)".

Steepest ascent on a real hill: slope vs angle

If \(h(x,y)\) is the height of a hill, \(\|\nabla h(a,b)\|\) is the steepest slope (rise per unit horizontal distance). The corresponding angle of climb above the horizontal is \(\arctan\|\nabla h(a,b)\|\) (Tutorial 8 Q4). The walking direction is still the 2-D vector \(\nabla h(a,b)\) in the map — you do not add a \(z\)-component.

🧭 The multivariable chain rule — why it adds up branches

Suppose \(z=f(x,y)\) and both \(x=x(t)\), \(y=y(t)\) depend on time. When \(t\) nudges by \(\Delta t\), \(x\) moves by \(\Delta x\approx x'(t)\Delta t\) and \(y\) by \(\Delta y\approx y'(t)\Delta t\). The linear approximation from Chapter 4 says

$$\Delta z\approx f_x\,\Delta x+f_y\,\Delta y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}.$$

Each term is one route by which \(t\) influences \(z\): "\(t\) changes \(x\), which changes \(z\)" plus "\(t\) changes \(y\), which changes \(z\)". The total effect is the sum of the effects along every route.

Tree diagrams — the marker-approved bookkeeping

The Summary says: draw a tree for \(z=f(\dots)\) with a branch for every intermediate variable, multiply the derivatives along each path, then add over all paths from the top variable down to the one you are differentiating with respect to. 2025 S1 Q4 gave 2 marks just for the tree.

Case 1 — one final variable (\(z=f(x,y)\), \(x(t),y(t)\))
z ∂z/∂x ∂z/∂y x y dx/dt dy/dt t t
$$\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$$
Case 2 — two final variables (\(z=f(x,y)\), \(x(s,t),y(s,t)\))
z ∂z/∂x ∂z/∂y x y ∂/∂s ∂/∂t ∂/∂s ∂/∂t s t s t
$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}$$ (follow only the paths ending in \(s\)).

Leibniz notation rule (markers check this): use a straight \(d\) when the thing on top depends on one variable only (\(\frac{dx}{dt}\) when \(x=x(t)\); \(\frac{dz}{dt}\) when ultimately \(z\) is a function of \(t\) alone), and a curly \(\partial\) when it depends on several (\(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\) because \(z\) depends on \(x\) and \(y\); \(\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}\) because \(x\) depends on \(s\) and \(t\)).

Three intermediate variables (e.g. \(w=f(x,y,z)\) with \(x,y,z\) functions of \(r,\varphi,\theta\) — 2025 S1 Q4) just means three branches and three terms per derivative: $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}.$$

Always finish by substituting back so the answer is in the final variables only (\(t\), or \(s,t\)), then evaluate at the given point if one is asked. An answer that still contains \(x\) and \(y\) when the question is about \(r\) and \(\theta\) is incomplete.

🧭 Functions of a single combined variable: \(g(x,y)=f(ax+by)\)

A favourite "show that" set-up: \(f\) is an unknown one-variable function and \(g\) feeds it a combination of \(x\) and \(y\). You can't differentiate \(f\) explicitly — you don't know it — so you name the inside \(u=ax+by\) and use the tree

g | dg/du = f'(u) u ∂u/∂x ∂u/∂y x y
$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=f'(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=a\,f'(u),\qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}=f'(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=b\,f'(u).$$

Consequences:

  • Identity: \(a\,g_y=ab\,f'(u)=b\,g_x\). This is 2025 S2 Q3(a) exactly.
  • Gradient direction: \(\nabla g=f'(u)\,(a,b)\). So \(\nabla g\) is always parallel to the fixed vector \((a,b)\): the greatest change is along \(\pm(a,b)\) at every point where \(f'(u)\ne0\) — \(+(a,b)\) for greatest increase where \(f'(u)\gt0\), \(-(a,b)\) where \(f'(u)\lt0\). This is 2025 S2 Q3(b).
  • Why geometrically: the level curves of \(g\) are the lines \(ax+by=\text{const}\) (constant \(u\) ⇒ constant \(g\)), and \(\nabla g\) must be normal to those lines — the normal to \(ax+by=k\) is \((a,b)\).

The same routine works for any inside: \(f(x^2-y^2)\) (2022 S1 Q4), \(f(x^2+y^2)\), \(f(y/x)\), \(x\,f(xy)\), \(f(x-ct)+g(x+ct)\)… Name the inside \(u\), compute \(\partial u/\partial x\), \(\partial u/\partial y\), multiply by \(f'(u)\).

🧭 Implicit differentiation — the chain rule on a level curve

A curve like \(y\ln x+xe^y-e^2=0\) (2024 S2 Q3) can't be solved for \(y\). But it is a level curve \(F(x,y)=0\). Along it, treat \(y=y(x)\) and differentiate \(F(x,y(x))=0\) with the chain rule:

$$F_x\frac{dx}{dx}+F_y\frac{dy}{dx}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}\quad(F_y\ne0).$$

That is the formula on the sheet, \(y'(a)=-F_x(a,b)/F_y(a,b)\). It's the same fact as "\(\nabla F\perp\) level curve": the tangent direction \((1,y')\) must be perpendicular to \((F_x,F_y)\).

Surfaces \(F(x,y,z)=0\) defining \(z=z(x,y)\) implicitly (NOT on the sheet — know it): hold \(y\) fixed and differentiate in \(x\), then vice versa:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=-\frac{F_x}{F_z},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}\qquad(F_z\ne0).$$
Tangent line to an implicit curve at \(x=a\) — the 12-mark routine (2024 S2 Q3 marking scheme)
  1. Find the point: substitute \(x=a\) into the curve and solve for \(y\). [2 marks] If there are several \(y\)-values, there are several tangent lines (2022 S1 Q5).
  2. Set up \(F(x,y)=0\) and write \(\frac{dy}{dx}=-F_x/F_y\). [2 marks]
  3. Compute \(F_x,F_y\) and evaluate at the point. [5 marks]
  4. Write the line \(y-b=m(x-a)\) and rearrange to the requested form. [3 marks]

§2 · Key methods & when to use them

The formula sheet prints the directional derivative, the two-variable chain rule and 2-D implicit differentiation. It does not print: "max rate \(=\|\nabla f\|\)", "no change \(\perp\nabla f\)", the tangent line/plane to a level set, the 3-D implicit formulas, or the partial-derivative (\(s,t\)) chain rule. Those are yours to carry in.

Gradient (know cold)

$$\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)\quad\text{or}\quad(f_x,f_y,f_z)$$
Any question containing "gradient", "\(\nabla f\)", "direction of greatest…", or as step 1 of a directional derivative.

Recipe: differentiate partially in each variable, write as a vector, then (if asked) substitute the point. Mini-example: \(f=x^2y+yz^3\Rightarrow\nabla f=(2xy,\ x^2+z^3,\ 3yz^2)\); at \((1,2,-1)\): \((4,0,6)\).

Directional derivative (on the sheet)

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\frac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}=\|\nabla f(P)\|\cos\theta$$
"Rate of change of \(f\) at \(P\) in the direction of \(\mathbf v\)", "slope in direction…", "rate of change with respect to distance … towards \(Q\)".
  1. Direction vector: given \(\mathbf v\), or \(\overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P\) for "towards \(Q\)" (towards the origin: \(-P\)).
  2. Normalise: \(\hat{\mathbf u}=\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\).
  3. \(\nabla f(P)\) (numbers).
  4. Dot product → a number. Leave surds exact (\(-21/\sqrt5\) is fine).

Mini-example: \(\nabla f(P)=(4,0,6)\), \(\mathbf v=(2,-1,2)\), \(\|\mathbf v\|=3\): \(D=(8+0+12)/3=20/3\).

Greatest increase / decrease / no change (know cold)

Tangent line / tangent plane to a level set (know cold)

$$\text{curve }F(x,y)=c:\ \ F_x(P)(x-a)+F_y(P)(y-b)=0\qquad\text{surface }F(x,y,z)=c:\ \ \nabla F(P)\cdot(x-a,\,y-b,\,z-c_0)=0$$
"Tangent line to the contour/level curve through…", "tangent plane to the surface \(x^2+\dots=k\)", "normal to the surface", "show that \(\nabla F\) is normal to…".

Normal line to the surface: \(\mathbf r(t)=P+t\,\nabla F(P)\). Mini-example: \(x^2+2y^2+3z^2=6\) at \((1,1,1)\): \(\nabla F=(2,4,6)\Rightarrow x+2y+3z=6\).

Chain rule — total derivative (on the sheet)

$$\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}\ \Big(+\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\frac{dw}{dt}\ \text{for a third variable}\Big)$$
One final variable (time \(t\)): "how fast is … changing", "rate of change experienced by the particle/bird", "determine \(dz/dt\) at \(t=\dots\)", related rates with given \(dx/dt\), \(dy/dt\).

Recipe: tree → partials of the top function → ordinary derivatives of each branch → multiply along paths, add → substitute the instant's values. Mini-example: \(V=\pi r^2h\), \(r=3\), \(h=4\), \(\dot r=\tfrac12\), \(\dot h=-1\): \(\dot V=2\pi rh\,\dot r+\pi r^2\dot h=12\pi-9\pi=3\pi\).

Chain rule — partial derivatives (know cold)

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial s},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}$$
Intermediate variables depend on two or more final variables: polar \((r,\theta)\), spherical \((r,\varphi,\theta)\), \(x=e^u\cos\theta\)…, "with the aid of a tree diagram".

"Check your answer by expressing \(w\) directly" (2025 S1 Q4b) = substitute first, then differentiate. Both routes must agree — a free self-check you should do even when not asked.

Combined-variable identities (know cold)

$$g=f(u),\ u=u(x,y):\qquad g_x=f'(u)\,u_x,\quad g_y=f'(u)\,u_y,\quad \nabla g=f'(u)\,\nabla u$$
"Let \(f\) be a differentiable function… show that…", \(f\) never given explicitly.

Mini-example: \(z=f(y/x)\): \(u_x=-y/x^2\), \(u_y=1/x\), so \(xz_x+yz_y=f'(u)\big(-\tfrac yx+\tfrac yx\big)=0\).

Implicit differentiation (2-D on the sheet; 3-D know cold)

$$F(x,y)=0\Rightarrow \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y};\qquad F(x,y,z)=0\Rightarrow \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=-\frac{F_x}{F_z},\ \ \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}$$
A relation you can't (or needn't) solve: "curve defined by the equation", "find \(y'(x)\)", "tangent line at \(x=a\)", "\(z\) defined implicitly by…".

Move everything to one side first so the equation reads \(F=0\) (or \(F=\) const). The minus sign in \(-F_x/F_y\) is the most-dropped symbol in this chapter.

Reverse-engineering \(\nabla f\) (know cold)

You're told the values of directional derivatives (and/or \(\|\nabla f\|\), or "max rate is … in direction …") and asked for \(\nabla f\) or constants in \(f\).
  1. Write \(\nabla f(P)=(a,b)\) (or \((a,b,c)\)).
  2. Each given rate gives one linear equation \((a,b)\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}_i=\text{value}\).
  3. "Max rate \(M\) in direction \(\mathbf d\)" means \(\nabla f(P)=M\,\mathbf d/\|\mathbf d\|\) directly.
  4. "\(D_{\mathbf v}f=-\|\nabla f\|\)" means \(\nabla f\) is anti-parallel to \(\mathbf v\).
  5. Solve; check by substituting back.

Example 1 — Gradient & directional derivative in 3-D

Q. Let \(f(x,y,z)=x^2y+yz^3\) and \(P=(1,2,-1)\). (a) Find \(\nabla f(P)\). [2] (b) Find the rate of change of \(f\) at \(P\) in the direction of \(\mathbf v=2\mathbf i-\mathbf j+2\mathbf k\). [3] (c) What is the maximum rate of change of \(f\) at \(P\), and in which direction does it occur? [2]

(a) \(f_x=2xy,\ f_y=x^2+z^3,\ f_z=3yz^2\). At \(P\): \(f_x=4,\ f_y=1+(-1)=0,\ f_z=3(2)(1)=6\). $$\nabla f(1,2,-1)=(4,\,0,\,6).$$

(b) \(\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{4+1+4}=3\), so \(\hat{\mathbf u}=\tfrac13(2,-1,2)\). $$D_{\mathbf v}f(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=\tfrac13\big(4\cdot2+0\cdot(-1)+6\cdot2\big)=\frac{20}{3}.$$

(c) Max rate \(=\|\nabla f(P)\|=\sqrt{16+0+36}=\sqrt{52}=2\sqrt{13}\), in the direction of \(\nabla f(P)=(4,0,6)\), i.e. of \((2,0,3)\).

Sanity check: \(20/3\approx6.67\lt2\sqrt{13}\approx7.21\). A directional derivative can never exceed \(\|\nabla f\|\) — if yours does, you forgot to normalise.

Example 2 — Steepest ascent, descent and level walking on a hill

Q. The height of a hill is \(h(x,y)=20-x^2-2y^2\). A hiker stands at \((2,1)\). (a) In which direction should she walk to climb most steeply, and what is the slope? (b) In which directions does her height not change? (c) What is the rate of change of height if she walks towards the point \((5,5)\)?

\(\nabla h=(-2x,\,-4y)\), so \(\nabla h(2,1)=(-4,-4)\).

(a) Steepest ascent is along \(\nabla h(2,1)=(-4,-4)\), i.e. the direction \((-1,-1)\) (towards the summit at the origin). The slope is \(\|\nabla h(2,1)\|=\sqrt{32}=4\sqrt2\).

(b) Zero rate ⇔ \(\hat{\mathbf u}\perp\nabla h(2,1)\). \((-4,-4)\cdot(u_1,u_2)=0\Rightarrow u_2=-u_1\): the directions \((1,-1)\) and \((-1,1)\) — along the contour through \((2,1)\).

(c) Direction \((5,5)-(2,1)=(3,4)\), \(\|\cdot\|=5\). $$D_{\mathbf u}h=(-4,-4)\cdot\tfrac15(3,4)=\frac{-12-16}{5}=-\frac{28}{5}.$$ Her height decreases at \(28/5\) per unit distance (she is walking away from the summit).

Example 3 — Reverse problem: recover \(\nabla f\) from two rates

Q. At \(P=(2,1)\), the rate of change of \(f\) in the direction towards \(Q=(5,5)\) is \(2\), and in the direction towards \(R=(0,1)\) is \(-2\). (a) Find \(\nabla f(P)\). (b) Find the rate of change of \(f\) at \(P\) towards the origin. (c) Find the direction(s) in which \(f\) does not change at \(P\).

(a) Let \(\nabla f(P)=(a,b)\). \(\overrightarrow{PQ}=(3,4)\), unit \(\tfrac15(3,4)\); \(\overrightarrow{PR}=(-2,0)\), unit \((-1,0)\). $$\tfrac15(3a+4b)=2\ \Rightarrow\ 3a+4b=10,\qquad -a=-2\ \Rightarrow\ a=2.$$ Then \(4b=4\Rightarrow b=1\). \(\boxed{\nabla f(P)=(2,1)}\). Check: \(\tfrac15(6+4)=2\) ✓, \(-2\) ✓.

(b) Towards the origin: \((0,0)-(2,1)=(-2,-1)\), unit \(\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(-2,-1)\). \(D=\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(-4-1)=-\tfrac5{\sqrt5}=-\sqrt5\).

(c) Perpendicular to \((2,1)\): \((1,-2)\) and \((-1,2)\).

This is exactly the shape of 2025 S1 Q1 (12 marks). The marking scheme gave 1 mark just for stating "the direction of no change is perpendicular to \(\nabla f\)" — write that sentence.

Example 4 — Tangent line to a level curve; tangent plane to a level surface

Q. (a) Find the tangent line to the curve \(x^2+xy+y^2=7\) at \((1,2)\). (b) Find the tangent plane and normal line to the ellipsoid \(x^2+2y^2+3z^2=6\) at \((1,1,1)\).

(a) Check the point: \(1+2+4=7\) ✓. The curve is the level curve \(F=7\) of \(F(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2\). \(\nabla F=(2x+y,\ x+2y)\), \(\nabla F(1,2)=(4,5)\), which is normal to the curve. Tangent line: $$4(x-1)+5(y-2)=0\iff 4x+5y=14.$$ (Same as implicit differentiation: \(y'=-4/5\).)

(b) Check: \(1+2+3=6\) ✓. \(F=x^2+2y^2+3z^2\), \(\nabla F=(2x,4y,6z)\), \(\nabla F(1,1,1)=(2,4,6)\propto(1,2,3)\). $$\text{Tangent plane: }(x-1)+2(y-1)+3(z-1)=0\iff x+2y+3z=6.$$ $$\text{Normal line: }\mathbf r(t)=(1,1,1)+t(1,2,3).$$

Example 5 — Related rates with the chain rule (cylinder)

Q. The radius of a cylinder is \(3\) cm and increasing at \(0.5\) cm/s, while its height is \(4\) cm and decreasing at \(1\) cm/s. How fast is the volume changing? Is it increasing or decreasing?

V ∂V/∂r ∂V/∂h r h dr/dt dh/dt t t

\(V=\pi r^2h\), so \(\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial r}=2\pi rh\), \(\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial h}=\pi r^2\). Chain rule: $$\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\frac{dr}{dt}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial h}\frac{dh}{dt}=2\pi(3)(4)(0.5)+\pi(9)(-1)=12\pi-9\pi=3\pi\ \text{cm}^3/\text{s}.$$ Since \(dV/dt=3\pi\gt0\), the volume is increasing at that instant.

"Decreasing at 1 cm/s" means \(dh/dt=-1\). Sign errors here cost the final mark on every related-rates question. Always give units and answer the "increasing or decreasing?" part in words (2022 S2 Q5(b): 1 mark).

Example 6 — Chain rule for partial derivatives (tree with two final variables)

Q. Let \(z=x^2y\) where \(x=s+t\), \(y=st\). Use the chain rule to find \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t}\) at \((s,t)=(1,2)\). Check by direct substitution.

z ∂z/∂x ∂z/∂y x y ∂x/∂s ∂x/∂t ∂y/∂s ∂y/∂t s t s t

\(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2xy,\ \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}=x^2;\quad \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial s}=1,\ \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial t}=1;\quad \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial s}=t,\ \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial t}=s.\)

At \((s,t)=(1,2)\): \(x=3,\ y=2\). $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=2xy\cdot1+x^2\cdot t=12+9\cdot2=30,\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=2xy\cdot1+x^2\cdot s=12+9=21.$$

Check: \(z=(s+t)^2st\). \(z_s=2(s+t)st+(s+t)^2t=12+18=30\) ✓; \(z_t=2(s+t)st+(s+t)^2s=12+9=21\) ✓.

Example 7 — "Show that" with an unknown function: \(z=f(y/x)\)

Q. Let \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) be differentiable and \(z=f(y/x)\) for \(x\ne0\). Show that \(x\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}+y\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}=0\).

Let \(u=\dfrac yx\), so \(z=f(u)\). Then \(\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}=-\dfrac{y}{x^2}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}=\dfrac1x\). By the chain rule, $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=\frac{dz}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=-\frac{y}{x^2}f'(u),\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=\frac{dz}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=\frac1x f'(u).$$ Hence $$x\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-\frac yx f'(u)+\frac yx f'(u)=0,$$ as required. \(\blacksquare\)

Note \(\frac{dz}{du}\) with a straight \(d\): \(z=f(u)\) depends on one variable \(u\). But \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\) is curly: \(u\) depends on \(x\) and \(y\). Finish with "as required" — a "show that" is marked on the logical chain, not on a final number.

Example 8 — Implicit differentiation and the tangent line at \(x=1\)

Q. Let \(C\) be the curve \(x^2y+y^3=10\). Find the equation of the tangent line to \(C\) at the point where \(x=1\).

Find the point. \(x=1\): \(y+y^3=10\Rightarrow y^3+y-10=0\Rightarrow(y-2)(y^2+2y+5)=0\). The quadratic has discriminant \(4-20\lt0\), so \(y=2\) is the only point: \((1,2)\).

Slope. Write \(F(x,y)=x^2y+y^3-10=0\). \(F_x=2xy\), \(F_y=x^2+3y^2\). At \((1,2)\): \(F_x=4\), \(F_y=13\). $$\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{(1,2)}=-\frac{F_x(1,2)}{F_y(1,2)}=-\frac{4}{13}.$$

Line. \(y-2=-\tfrac4{13}(x-1)\iff y=-\tfrac4{13}x+\tfrac{30}{13}\).

Example 9 — Implicit partial derivatives on a surface, and its tangent plane

Q. The equation \(xyz+z^3=10\) defines \(z\) implicitly as a function of \(x,y\) near \((1,1,2)\). Find \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\) there, and the tangent plane.

Check: \(2+8=10\) ✓. \(F=xyz+z^3-10\): \(F_x=yz=2,\ F_y=xz=2,\ F_z=xy+3z^2=1+12=13\). $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}=-\frac2{13},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}=-\frac2{13}.$$ Tangent plane (normal \(\nabla F=(2,2,13)\)): \(2(x-1)+2(y-1)+13(z-2)=0\iff 2x+2y+13z=30\).

Two routes, one answer: the plane \(z=2-\tfrac2{13}(x-1)-\tfrac2{13}(y-1)\) (the Chapter 4 linearisation with the implicit partials) rearranges to the same \(2x+2y+13z=30\).

Type A — Gradient, directional derivative & steepest direction for a given \(f\) ~10–15 marks

Instances: 2024 S2 Q4 (12 marks) · 2023 S2 Q3 (12) · 2024 S1 Q1 (12, 3-D temperature) · 2022 S2 Q3 (10) · 2021 S1 Q5 (10) · 2021 S2 Q7 (15) · Tutorial 8 Q1–4.

Looks like: "Consider \(f(x,y)=\dots\) (a) Determine \(\nabla f\). (b) Determine the directional derivative \(f_{\mathbf v}(P)\) where \(\mathbf v=\dots\) (c) Determine the direction of greatest increase/decrease." Often a temperature \(T\) and an ant/panda walking.

Routine:
  1. General \(\nabla f(x,y)\) — both partials, simplified. (1 mark for writing \(\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)\).)
  2. Evaluate at \(P\) — exact trig values, surds.
  3. Normalise the direction; dot product. (1 mark for the formula.)
  4. Greatest increase: \(\nabla f(P)\); decrease: \(-\nabla f(P)\); max rate \(\|\nabla f(P)\|\).

📄 Real exam question — 2024 S2 Q4 [12 marks]

Consider the function \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) given by \(f(x,y)=10+6\cos x\cos y+3\cos 2x+4\cos 4y.\)

  • (a) Determine \(\nabla f(x,y)\). [3 marks]
  • (b) Determine the directional derivative \(f_{\mathbf v}(\pi/4,\pi/4)\), where \(\mathbf v=2\mathbf i+\mathbf j\). [6 marks]
  • (c) Determine the direction of greatest increase of \(f\) at the point \((\pi/4,\pi/4)\). [3 marks]
Full worked solution — 2024 S2 Q4(a)–(c)

(a) \(\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)\) [1 mark for the formula], with $$f_x=-6\sin x\cos y-6\sin 2x,\qquad f_y=-6\cos x\sin y-16\sin 4y.$$ So \(\nabla f(x,y)=\big(-6\sin x\cos y-6\sin2x,\ -6\cos x\sin y-16\sin4y\big)\). [2 marks for correct working]

(b) \(f_{\mathbf v}(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}\) [1 mark], \(\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt5\). At \(P=(\pi/4,\pi/4)\): \(\sin\frac\pi4=\cos\frac\pi4=\frac{\sqrt2}2\), \(\sin\frac\pi2=1\), \(\sin\pi=0\): $$f_x=-6\cdot\tfrac12-6\cdot1=-9,\qquad f_y=-6\cdot\tfrac12-16\cdot0=-3\quad[3\text{ marks}]$$ $$f_{\mathbf v}(P)=(-9,-3)\cdot\frac{(2,1)}{\sqrt5}=\frac{-18-3}{\sqrt5}=-\frac{21}{\sqrt5}.\quad[2\text{ marks}]$$

(c) The direction of greatest increase is the direction of the gradient: \(\nabla f(\pi/4,\pi/4)=(-9,-3)\), equivalently \((-3,-1)\). [3 marks]

Marking scheme (2024 S2 MS): marks are split formula / partials / evaluation / final value. Even if a partial is wrong, writing \(f_{\mathbf v}=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\) and normalising correctly banks marks.

Type B — Reverse problems: find \(\nabla f\) (or constants) from rates ~10–14 marks

📄 Real exam question — 2025 S1 Q1 [12 marks]

The rate of change of a given function \(f(x,y)\) at the point \(P_0=(1,2)\) in the direction towards \(P_1=(2,3)\) is \(2\sqrt2\). The rate of change of \(f(x,y)\) at the point \(P_0\) in the direction towards \(P_2=(1,0)\) is \(-3\).

  • (a) Determine \(\nabla f(P_0)\). [6 marks]
  • (b) Determine the rate of change of \(f\) at \(P_0\) towards the origin \((0,0)\). [3 marks]
  • (c) Determine the direction(s) where \(f\) does not change. [3 marks]
Full worked solution — 2025 S1 Q1(a)–(c)

(a) \(\overrightarrow{P_0P_1}=(1,1)\), unit \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\); \(\overrightarrow{P_0P_2}=(0,-2)\), unit \((0,-1)\). Let \(\nabla f(P_0)=(a,b)\). $$(a,b)\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)=2\sqrt2\Rightarrow a+b=4;\qquad (a,b)\cdot(0,-1)=-3\Rightarrow b=3.$$ [2 marks each equation] So \(a=1\): \(\boxed{\nabla f(P_0)=(1,3)}\). [2 marks]

(b) Towards the origin: \(\mathbf u=(0,0)-(1,2)=(-1,-2)\) [1 mark], \(\|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt5\). $$f_{\mathbf u}(P_0)=(1,3)\cdot\frac{(-1,-2)}{\sqrt5}=\frac{-1-6}{\sqrt5}=-\frac7{\sqrt5}.\ [2]$$

(c) \(f\) does not change in directions perpendicular to \(\nabla f(P_0)=(1,3)\) [1 mark]: \((-3,1)\) or \((3,-1)\). [2 marks]

Use the vector from \(P_0\) to the target point, not the target point itself. Here "towards \(P_1=(2,3)\)" is the direction \((1,1)\), not \((2,3)\).

📄 Real exam question — 2025 S1 Q4 [12 marks]

Suppose that \(w=x^2+y^2-z^2\) and \(x=r\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ z=r\cos\varphi.\)

  • (a) With the aid of a tree diagram, use the chain rule to evaluate \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial r}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}\). [8 marks]
  • (b) Check your answers in (a) by first expressing \(w\) directly in terms of \(r,\varphi\) and \(\theta\), and then evaluating the partial derivatives. [4 marks]
Full worked solution — 2025 S1 Q4
w ∂w/∂x ∂w/∂y ∂w/∂z x y z / | \ / | \ / | \ r φ θ r φ θ r φ θ

[2 marks for the tree.] \(w_x=2x,\ w_y=2y,\ w_z=-2z\).

(a) $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}=2x\sin\varphi\cos\theta+2y\sin\varphi\sin\theta-2z\cos\varphi$$ $$=2r\big(\sin^2\varphi\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\varphi\sin^2\theta-\cos^2\varphi\big)=2r(\sin^2\varphi-\cos^2\varphi)=-2r\cos2\varphi.$$ $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}=2x(-r\sin\varphi\sin\theta)+2y(r\sin\varphi\cos\theta)+(-2z)(0)=2r^2\sin^2\varphi(-\cos\theta\sin\theta+\sin\theta\cos\theta)=0.$$

(b) Directly: \(w=r^2\sin^2\varphi(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)-r^2\cos^2\varphi=r^2(\sin^2\varphi-\cos^2\varphi)=-r^2\cos2\varphi\). So \(w_r=-2r\cos2\varphi\) ✓ and \(w_\theta=0\) ✓ (\(w\) has no \(\theta\) in it).

Note \(\partial z/\partial\theta=0\) — the \(z\)-branch still appears in the tree, it just contributes zero. Say so; don't silently drop it.

📄 Real exam question — 2023 S1 Q4 [12 marks]

Atom Ant is travelling on a path given by the parametric equations \(x_1=4\cos t,\ y_1=2\sin t\). Billy Bug is travelling on a path given by the parametric equations \(x_2=2\sin2t,\ y_2=3\cos2t\). Determine the rate at which the distance between Atom Ant and Billy Bug is changing at \(t=\pi\). Hint: The distance between Atom Ant and Billy Bug at time \(t\) is \(s=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\).

Full worked solution — 2023 S1 Q4

\(s\) depends on \(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2\), each a function of \(t\): $$\frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_1}\frac{dx_1}{dt}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial y_1}\frac{dy_1}{dt}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_2}\frac{dx_2}{dt}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial y_2}\frac{dy_2}{dt}=\frac{(x_2-x_1)(x_2'-x_1')+(y_2-y_1)(y_2'-y_1')}{s}.$$ At \(t=\pi\): \(x_1=-4,\ y_1=0,\ x_2=0,\ y_2=3\), so \(s=\sqrt{16+9}=5\).
Derivatives: \(x_1'=-4\sin t=0,\ y_1'=2\cos t=-2,\ x_2'=4\cos2t=4,\ y_2'=-6\sin2t=0\). $$\frac{ds}{dt}\Big|_{t=\pi}=\frac{(4)(4-0)+(3)(0-(-2))}{5}=\frac{16+6}{5}=\frac{22}{5}.$$ The distance is increasing at \(22/5\) units per unit time.

Type E — "Show that" identities for \(f(\text{combination})\), and directions for such \(g\) ~5–10 marks

Instances: 2025 S2 Q3 (10: the paper prints (a) 5 + (b) 5; the solutions file prints 7 + 3) · 2022 S1 Q4 (10) · 2021 S1 Q6(b) (5) · Tutorial 9 Q5, Q7.

Looks like: "Let \(f\) be a differentiable function… \(g(x,y)=f(ax+by)\). Show that \(a\,g_y=b\,g_x\)." / "\(z=f(x^2-y^2)\). Show that \(xz_y+yz_x=0\)."

📄 Real exam question — 2025 S2 Q3 [10 marks]

  • (a) Let \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) be a differentiable function, and let \(g:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be given by \(g(x,y)=f(ax+by)\), for some \(a,b\in\mathbb R\). Show that \(a\Big(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}\Big)=b\Big(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}\Big).\) [5 marks]
  • (b) Determine the directions of greatest change of the function \(g(x,y)=e^{5x+y}\cos\big((5x+y)^2\big)\) at any point in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane. [5 marks]
Full worked solution — 2025 S2 Q3(a)–(b)

(a) Set \(u=ax+by\), so \(g(x,y)=f(u)\). By the chain rule, $$\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=\frac{df}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=a\,f'(u),\qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}=\frac{df}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=b\,f'(u).$$ Therefore \(a\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}=ab\,f'(u)=b\big(a f'(u)\big)=b\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}\), as required. \(\blacksquare\)

(b) This \(g\) has the form of (a) with \(a=5,\ b=1\) and \(f(u)=e^u\cos(u^2)\). From (a) (or directly), \(g_x=5f'(u)\), \(g_y=f'(u)\), so $$\nabla g=f'(u)\,(5,1),\qquad f'(u)=e^{u}\big(\cos u^2-2u\sin u^2\big),\ u=5x+y.$$ The gradient is always parallel to \((5,1)\). Hence the directions of greatest change are \(\pm(5,1)\): greatest increase along \((5,1)\) where \(f'(u)\gt0\) and along \(-(5,1)\) where \(f'(u)\lt0\); greatest decrease is the opposite one. (Where \(f'(u)=0\), \(\nabla g=\mathbf 0\) and \(g\) has zero rate of change in every direction.)

The official solution uses (a) with \(a=5,b=1\): "\(5g_y=g_x\), so \(\nabla g=g_y(5,1)\) ⇒ directions \(\pm(5,1)\)". Recognising that (b) is (a) in disguise is the whole point — no need to differentiate \(\cos\big((5x+y)^2\big)\) at all to get the direction.

2022 S1 Q4 (worked answer)

\(z=f(u)\), \(u=x^2-y^2\): \(z_x=2x\,f'(u)\), \(z_y=-2y\,f'(u)\). So \(xz_y+yz_x=-2xy\,f'(u)+2xy\,f'(u)=0\), as required.

Type F — Implicit differentiation → tangent line ~10–12 marks

Instances: 2024 S2 Q3 (12) · 2022 S1 Q5 (10, two tangent lines) · 2021 S1 Q3 (10) · Tutorial 9 Q3–4. (Related: 2021 S2 Q2 (5) — tangent plane to the level surface \(x^4-y+z=0\) via its normal \((4x^3,-1,1)\); see Chapter 4.)

📄 Real exam question — 2024 S2 Q3 [12 marks]

Let \(C\) be the curve defined by the equation \(y\ln x+xe^y-e^2=0,\ \) and \(x\gt0.\) Show that the equation of the tangent line to the curve \(C\) at the point \(x=1\) is $$y=-\Big(1+\frac2{e^2}\Big)x+3+\frac2{e^2}.$$

Full worked solution — 2024 S2 Q3 (with the marking scheme)

Point [2 marks]: at \(x=1\), \(y\ln1+e^y-e^2=0\Rightarrow e^y=e^2\Rightarrow y=2\). The point is \((1,2)\).

Set-up [2 marks]: \(F(x,y)=y\ln x+xe^y-e^2=0\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}(1,2)=-\dfrac{F_x(1,2)}{F_y(1,2)}\).

Working [5 marks]: \(F_x=\dfrac yx+e^y\Rightarrow F_x(1,2)=2+e^2\); \(F_y=\ln x+xe^y\Rightarrow F_y(1,2)=e^2\). $$\frac{dy}{dx}(1,2)=-\frac{2+e^2}{e^2}=-\Big(1+\frac2{e^2}\Big).$$

Tangent line [3 marks]: \(y-2=-\Big(1+\dfrac2{e^2}\Big)(x-1)\Rightarrow y=-\Big(1+\dfrac2{e^2}\Big)x+1+\dfrac2{e^2}+2=-\Big(1+\dfrac2{e^2}\Big)x+3+\dfrac2{e^2}\), as required.

2022 S1 Q5 and 2021 S1 Q3 (worked answers)

2022 S1 Q5 \(y\cos x=x^2+y^2\) at \(x=0\): \(y=y^2\Rightarrow y=0\) or \(y=1\) — two points, two tangent lines. \(F=y\cos x-x^2-y^2\), \(y'=\dfrac{y\sin x+2x}{\cos x-2y}\), which is \(0\) at both \((0,0)\) and \((0,1)\). Tangent lines: \(y=0\) and \(y=1\).

2021 S1 Q3 \(e^{xy}+ye^y=2e\) at \(y=1\): \(e^x+e=2e\Rightarrow x=1\). \(F_x=ye^{xy}=e\), \(F_y=xe^{xy}+e^y+ye^y=3e\), so \(y'=-\tfrac13\): tangent \(y-1=-\tfrac13(x-1)\).

§6 · Traps & marks lost

Forgetting to normalise. \(\nabla f\cdot\mathbf v\) with \(\|\mathbf v\|\ne1\) gives the wrong number. The sheet writes \(\mathbf u/\|\mathbf u\|\) for a reason. Check: \(|D_{\mathbf u}f|\le\|\nabla f\|\) always.
"Towards \(Q\)" ≠ "in the direction of \(Q\)". Towards \(Q\) from \(P\) is \(Q-P\). Using \(Q\) as the direction vector is the most common error in 2025 S1 Q1-type questions.
Direction vs rate. "Direction" wants a vector; "rate" wants a number. Giving \(\|\nabla f\|\) when asked for a direction, or \(\nabla f\) when asked for the rate, scores zero for that part. 2024 S1 Q1 asks for both in (b) and (c) — keep them separate.
Steepest descent rate is negative. In the direction \(-\nabla f\), \(D_{\mathbf u}f=-\|\nabla f\|\). The 2022 S2 marking scheme writes "rate at which the temperature is dropping most rapidly is \(-\|\nabla T(1,1)\|=-\tfrac19\sqrt2\)". If you say "drops at the rate \(\tfrac{\sqrt2}9\)", the words carry the sign — be explicit either way.
Zero-change directions come in pairs. In 2-D there are two: \((-q,p)\) and \((q,-p)\). The 2025 S1 MS awarded 2 marks for "\((-3,1)\) or \((3,-1)\)". In 3-D, give the condition \(\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=0\) (a plane of directions), not a single vector.
"Directions of greatest change" of \(f(ax+by)\) — the sign flips. \(\nabla g=f'(u)(a,b)\); where \(f'(u)\lt0\) the gradient points along \(-(a,b)\). Say "\(\pm(a,b)\)" and explain which is increase — and that there's no preferred direction where \(\nabla g=\mathbf 0\).
Dropping a branch of the tree. With three intermediate variables you need three terms, even if one is zero (\(\partial z/\partial\theta=0\) in 2025 S1 Q4). Missing a whole branch usually loses the "correct working" marks for that derivative.
\(d\) vs \(\partial\). \(\frac{dz}{dt}\) only when \(z\) is ultimately a function of \(t\) alone; \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}\) when it depends on \(s\) and \(t\). Writing \(\frac{dz}{dx}\) for a two-variable \(z\) is marked as a notation error.
Not substituting back. "Find \(\partial w/\partial r\)" means in terms of \(r,\varphi,\theta\). Leaving \(2x\sin\varphi\cos\theta+\dots\) is unfinished; simplify with \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\), double angles, etc.
Sign of a "decreasing" rate. "Decreasing at 0.1 Ohm/s" ⇒ \(dR_2/dt=-0.1\) (Tutorial 9 Q6). Put the minus sign in before you multiply.
Implicit: forgetting to find \(y\) first. "Tangent line at \(x=1\)" gives only \(x\). The 2024 S2 MS awarded 2 marks for finding \(y=2\). If the equation gives several \(y\)-values, there are several tangent lines (2022 S1 Q5: \(y=0\) and \(y=1\)).
Implicit: the minus sign and the arrangement. \(y'=-F_x/F_y\) requires the equation written as \(F(x,y)=0\) (or \(=\) const). If you define \(F\) with the terms on the wrong sides, still fine — as long as all terms are on one side. Dropping the minus sign flips the tangent line.
Using \(\nabla f\) of the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) as the 3-D normal. \(\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)\) is a 2-D vector, normal to the contours in the \(xy\)-plane. The normal to the surface is \(\nabla F=(f_x,f_y,-1)\) with \(F=f(x,y)-z\).
Trusting a solution sheet blindly. The 2022 S1 Q6 official solution has a sign slip in the unit vector (see Type B). Always check your \(\nabla f\) against the given data before moving on — it takes 20 seconds.
What the markers reward (from the 2022 S2, 2024 S2 and 2025 S1 marking schemes): (1) the formula written before numbers, (2) correct general partials, (3) correct values at the point, (4) a final answer in the form asked (vector / number / line equation) with a sentence of interpretation ("the temperature is decreasing", "the capacity is decreasing at that instant", "\(f\) does not change in directions perpendicular to \(\nabla f\)").

§7 · Self-check

1. What is the maximum possible value of a directional derivative of \(f\) at \(P\), and in which direction?

\(\|\nabla f(P)\|\), attained in the direction of \(\nabla f(P)\) (because \(D_{\hat{\mathbf u}}f=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\le1\)).

2. \(\|\nabla f(P)\|=10\). What is the rate of change of \(f\) in a direction making \(60^\circ\) with \(\nabla f(P)\)?

\(10\cos60^\circ=5\).

3. \(\nabla f(P)=(3,-4)\). Give the directions of zero change.

\((4,3)\) and \((-4,-3)\) (both perpendicular to \((3,-4)\)).

4. Can the rate of change of \(f\) at \(P\) be \(7\) if \(\nabla f(P)=(4,5)\)?

No: \(\|\nabla f(P)\|=\sqrt{41}\approx6.4\lt7\), and no directional derivative exceeds \(\|\nabla f\|\).

5. Why is \(\nabla f\) perpendicular to the contour through \(P\)?

Along the contour \(f(\mathbf r(t))=c\); differentiating gives \(\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=0\), and \(\mathbf r'\) is tangent to the contour.

6. You are at \(P=(1,2)\) and asked for the rate "towards the origin". What direction vector do you use?

\((0,0)-(1,2)=(-1,-2)\), normalised to \(\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(-1,-2)\).

7. Write the chain rule for \(\partial z/\partial t\) when \(z=f(x,y)\), \(x=x(s,t)\), \(y=y(s,t)\).

\(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t}=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial t}\).

8. \(g(x,y)=f(2x-7y)\). Find \(\nabla g\) in terms of \(f'\), and the identity linking \(g_x\) and \(g_y\).

\(\nabla g=f'(2x-7y)\,(2,-7)\). Identity: \(2g_y=-7g_x\), i.e. \(7g_x+2g_y=0\).

9. State \(dy/dx\) for the curve \(F(x,y)=0\) and \(\partial z/\partial y\) for the surface \(F(x,y,z)=0\).

\(dy/dx=-F_x/F_y\); \(\partial z/\partial y=-F_y/F_z\).

10. Normal vector to the surface \(z=x^2+y^2\) at \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\)?

Write \(F=x^2+y^2-z\): \(\nabla F=(2x_0,2y_0,-1)\) (Tutorial 8 Q5).

11. The height of a hill has \(\|\nabla h(P)\|=1\). At what angle to the horizontal is the steepest climb?

\(\arctan1=\pi/4\) (45°).

12. "The radius is 5 cm and decreasing at 2 cm/s." What value do you substitute for \(dr/dt\)?

\(dr/dt=-2\) cm/s.

13. In a tree diagram for \(w=f(x,y,z)\), \(x,y,z\) functions of \((r,\theta)\), how many terms are in \(\partial w/\partial\theta\)?

Three — one per path from \(w\) down to \(\theta\) (through \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\)), even if some factors are 0.

14. \(D_{\mathbf v}f(P)=-\|\nabla f(P)\|\). What can you say about \(\nabla f(P)\)?

It points exactly opposite to \(\mathbf v\): \(\nabla f(P)=-\|\nabla f(P)\|\,\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\) (2022 S1 Q6).

Q. At \(P\), the rate of change of \(f\) in the direction \(\mathbf i\) is \(3\), and in the direction \(4\mathbf i+3\mathbf j\) it is \(0\). Find \(\nabla f(P)\) and the maximum rate of change at \(P\).

Solution

(a) \(\nabla f(P)=10\cdot\tfrac15(3,4)=(6,8)\). \(\nabla f=(2ax+by,\ bx)\), at \(P\): \((2a+2b,\ b)\). So \(b=8\), \(2a+16=6\Rightarrow a=-5\).

(b) \(\perp(6,8)\): \((4,-3)\) and \((-4,3)\).

8.4 · Tangent lines & planes to level sets via \(\nabla F\)

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — tangent line to a contour

Q. Let \(f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2\). Find the contour through \((2,1)\), the gradient there, and the tangent line to the contour at \((2,1)\).

\(f(2,1)=8\): the contour is the ellipse \(x^2+4y^2=8\). \(\nabla f=(2x,8y)\), \(\nabla f(2,1)=(4,8)\propto(1,2)\) — normal to the ellipse. Tangent line: \((x-2)+2(y-1)=0\iff x+2y=4\).

Worked B — tangent plane to a level surface

Q. Find the tangent plane to \(xy+yz+zx=11\) at \((1,2,3)\).

Check: \(2+6+3=11\) ✓. \(\nabla F=(y+z,\ x+z,\ x+y)\), \(\nabla F(1,2,3)=(5,4,3)\). Plane: \(5(x-1)+4(y-2)+3(z-3)=0\iff5x+4y+3z=22\).

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 3 marks Q1. Find the tangent line to the curve \(x^2+3xy-y^2=3\) at \((1,1)\).

Solution

Check \(1+3-1=3\) ✓. \(\nabla F=(2x+3y,\ 3x-2y)=(5,1)\). Line: \(5(x-1)+(y-1)=0\iff5x+y=6\).

STANDARD · 5 marks Q2. Find the tangent plane and normal line to the surface \(x^2+2y^2+z^2=7\) at \((1,-1,2)\).

Solution

Check \(1+2+4=7\) ✓. \(\nabla F=(2x,4y,2z)=(2,-4,4)\propto(1,-2,2)\). Plane: \((x-1)-2(y+1)+2(z-2)=0\iff x-2y+2z=7\). Normal line: \(\mathbf r(t)=(1,-1,2)+t(1,-2,2)\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 8 marks Q3. Find all points on the ellipsoid \(x^2+2y^2+3z^2=66\) at which the tangent plane is parallel to the plane \(x+y+z=0\), and give the equations of those tangent planes.

Solution

Parallel planes ⇔ parallel normals: \(\nabla F=(2x,4y,6z)=\lambda(1,1,1)\), so \(x=\tfrac\lambda2,\ y=\tfrac\lambda4,\ z=\tfrac\lambda6\). Substitute: $$\lambda^2\Big(\tfrac14+\tfrac18+\tfrac1{12}\Big)=\frac{11\lambda^2}{24}=66\Rightarrow\lambda^2=144\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm12.$$ Points \((6,3,2)\) and \((-6,-3,-2)\). Tangent planes: \(x+y+z=11\) and \(x+y+z=-11\).

8.5 · Chain rule with tree diagrams

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — three intermediate variables, one final variable

Q. Let \(w=xy+z^2\), \(x=\cos t\), \(y=\sin t\), \(z=t\). Find \(\dfrac{dw}{dt}\) and its value at \(t=\pi\).

w ∂w/∂x ∂w/∂y ∂w/∂z x y z | | | t t t

$$\frac{dw}{dt}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt}=y(-\sin t)+x\cos t+2z=\cos^2t-\sin^2t+2t=\cos2t+2t.$$ At \(t=\pi\): \(1+2\pi\). (Check: \(w=\tfrac12\sin2t+t^2\), \(w'=\cos2t+2t\) ✓.)

Worked B — two final variables

Q. Let \(z=\ln(x^2+y^2)\) with \(x=s+t\), \(y=s-t\). Find \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t}\) in terms of \(s,t\).

\(z_x=\dfrac{2x}{x^2+y^2}\), \(z_y=\dfrac{2y}{x^2+y^2}\); \(x_s=1,\ x_t=1,\ y_s=1,\ y_t=-1\); \(x^2+y^2=2s^2+2t^2\). $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=\frac{2x+2y}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{4s}{2(s^2+t^2)}=\frac{2s}{s^2+t^2},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=\frac{2x-2y}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{2t}{s^2+t^2}.$$

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 3 marks Q1. Let \(z=x^2y\), \(x=e^t\), \(y=t^2\). Use the chain rule to find \(\dfrac{dz}{dt}\), and evaluate it at \(t=1\).

Solution

\(\dfrac{dz}{dt}=2xy\,e^t+x^2\cdot2t=2t^2e^{2t}+2te^{2t}=2t(t+1)e^{2t}\). At \(t=1\): \(4e^2\).

STANDARD · 5 marks Q2. Let \(w=xyz\), \(x=s+t\), \(y=s-t\), \(z=st\). With a tree diagram, find \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial s}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial t}\) at \((s,t)=(2,1)\).

Solution

At \((2,1)\): \(x=3,\ y=1,\ z=2\); \(w_x=yz=2,\ w_y=xz=6,\ w_z=xy=3\). $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=2(1)+6(1)+3(t)=2+6+3=11,\qquad\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}=2(1)+6(-1)+3(s)=2-6+6=2.$$ Check: \(w=s^3t-st^3\): \(w_s=3s^2t-t^3=11\) ✓, \(w_t=s^3-3st^2=2\) ✓.

EXAM-LEVEL · 8 marks Q3. Let \(w=x^2+y^2-2z^2\) with \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ z=r\). (a) With the aid of a tree diagram, find \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial r}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}\). [6] (b) Check by writing \(w\) in terms of \(r,\theta\). [2]

Solution

(a) \(w_x=2x,\ w_y=2y,\ w_z=-4z\). $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}=2x\cos\theta+2y\sin\theta-4z\cdot1=2r(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)-4r=-2r.$$ $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial\theta}=2x(-r\sin\theta)+2y(r\cos\theta)-4z\cdot0=-2r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta+2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta=0.$$

(b) \(w=r^2-2r^2=-r^2\): \(w_r=-2r\) ✓, \(w_\theta=0\) ✓.

STRETCH · 6 marks Q4. Let \(f(x,y)\) have continuous partial derivatives and put \(x=u+v\), \(y=u-v\). Show that \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}=\Big(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big)^2-\Big(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Big)^2.\)

Solution

\(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}=f_x\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}+f_y\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}=f_x+f_y\); \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}=f_x\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}+f_y\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}=f_x-f_y\). Product: \((f_x+f_y)(f_x-f_y)=f_x^2-f_y^2\), as required.

8.6 · Related rates

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — a growing box

Q. A box has dimensions \(x=2,\ y=3,\ z=4\) cm at some instant, with \(x\) increasing at 1 cm/s, \(y\) decreasing at 1 cm/s and \(z\) increasing at 2 cm/s. How fast are (a) the volume and (b) the surface area changing?

(a) \(V=xyz\): \(\dot V=yz\,\dot x+xz\,\dot y+xy\,\dot z=12(1)+8(-1)+6(2)=16\) cm³/s (increasing).

(b) \(S=2(xy+yz+zx)\): \(\dot S=2\big[(y+z)\dot x+(x+z)\dot y+(x+y)\dot z\big]=2[7-6+10]=22\) cm²/s (increasing).

Worked B — two ships

Q. Ship A is 6 km east of a port, sailing east at 3 km/h. Ship B is 8 km north of the port, sailing north at 4 km/h. How fast is the distance between the ships changing?

\(D=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) with \(x=6,\ y=8,\ D=10\), \(\dot x=3,\ \dot y=4\). $$\frac{dD}{dt}=\frac{\partial D}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial D}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{x\dot x+y\dot y}{D}=\frac{18+32}{10}=5\ \text{km/h}.$$ The distance is increasing at 5 km/h.

✏️ Your turn

STANDARD · 5 marks Q1. A cone has radius \(r=3\) cm increasing at 2 cm/s and height \(h=6\) cm decreasing at 3 cm/s. Given \(V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h\), how fast is the volume changing? Is it increasing or decreasing?

Solution

\(\dot V=\tfrac23\pi rh\,\dot r+\tfrac13\pi r^2\dot h=\tfrac23\pi(18)(2)+\tfrac13\pi(9)(-3)=24\pi-9\pi=15\pi\) cm³/s — increasing.

STANDARD · 5 marks Q2. A rectangle has sides \(x=5\) cm (increasing at 2 cm/s) and \(y=12\) cm (decreasing at 1 cm/s). How fast are (a) its area and (b) its diagonal changing?

Solution

(a) \(\dot A=y\dot x+x\dot y=24-5=19\) cm²/s. (b) \(D=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=13\); \(\dot D=\dfrac{x\dot x+y\dot y}{D}=\dfrac{10-12}{13}=-\dfrac2{13}\) cm/s (decreasing).

EXAM-LEVEL · 8 marks Q3. Particle A moves on \(\mathbf r_A(t)=(3\cos t,\ 3\sin t)\) and particle B on \(\mathbf r_B(t)=(2t,\ 4+t^2)\). Determine the rate at which the distance between them is changing at \(t=0\). Are they approaching or separating?

Solution

At \(t=0\): A \(=(3,0)\), B \(=(0,4)\), \(s=5\). \(\mathbf r_A'=(-3\sin t,3\cos t)=(0,3)\), \(\mathbf r_B'=(2,2t)=(2,0)\). $$\frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{(x_B-x_A)(x_B'-x_A')+(y_B-y_A)(y_B'-y_A')}{s}=\frac{(-3)(2)+(4)(-3)}{5}=-\frac{18}{5}.$$ Negative ⇒ they are approaching, at \(18/5\) units per unit time.

8.7 · "Show that" identities with an unknown \(f\)

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — \(z=f(3x-2y)\): identity and directions of greatest change

Q. (a) Let \(f\) be differentiable and \(z=f(3x-2y)\). Show that \(2z_x+3z_y=0\). (b) Hence find the directions of greatest change of \(g(x,y)=(3x-2y)e^{3x-2y}\).

(a) \(u=3x-2y\): \(z_x=3f'(u)\), \(z_y=-2f'(u)\). So \(2z_x+3z_y=6f'(u)-6f'(u)=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

(b) \(g=F(u)\) with \(F(u)=ue^u\), \(F'(u)=(1+u)e^u\). \(\nabla g=(1+u)e^u\,(3,-2)\), always parallel to \((3,-2)\). Directions of greatest change: \(\pm(3,-2)\). Greatest increase along \((3,-2)\) where \(3x-2y\gt-1\), along \((-3,2)\) where \(3x-2y\lt-1\); on the line \(3x-2y=-1\), \(\nabla g=\mathbf 0\).

Worked B — a product with an unknown function

Q. Let \(z=x\,f(xy)\), \(f\) differentiable. Show that \(x\,z_x-y\,z_y=z\).

\(u=xy\). Product rule + chain rule: \(z_x=f(u)+x f'(u)\,y\); \(z_y=x f'(u)\,x=x^2f'(u)\). $$xz_x-yz_y=xf(u)+x^2yf'(u)-x^2yf'(u)=xf(u)=z,$$ as required.

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 3 marks Q1. Let \(z=f(x+4y)\) with \(f\) differentiable. Show that \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}=4\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\).

Solution

\(u=x+4y\): \(z_x=f'(u)\cdot1\), \(z_y=f'(u)\cdot4\). So \(z_y=4f'(u)=4z_x\).

STANDARD · 5 marks Q2. Let \(z=f(x^2+y^2)\). Show that \(y\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}-x\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}=0\), and explain what this says about \(\nabla z\) and the circles \(x^2+y^2=c\).

Solution

\(u=x^2+y^2\): \(z_x=2xf'(u)\), \(z_y=2yf'(u)\); \(yz_x-xz_y=2xyf'-2xyf'=0\). Meaning: \(\nabla z=2f'(u)(x,y)\) is radial, i.e. perpendicular to the circles \(x^2+y^2=c\), which are the level curves of \(z\). Equivalently the rate of change of \(z\) in the tangential direction \((-y,x)\) is zero.

EXAM-LEVEL · 5 marks Q3. Determine the directions of greatest change of \(g(x,y)=\ln\!\big(1+(2x-y)^2\big)\) at any point in the plane.

Solution

\(g=F(u)\), \(u=2x-y\), \(F(u)=\ln(1+u^2)\), \(F'(u)=\dfrac{2u}{1+u^2}\). \(\nabla g=\dfrac{2u}{1+u^2}(2,-1)\), always parallel to \((2,-1)\): directions of greatest change \(\pm(2,-1)\). Greatest increase along \((2,-1)\) where \(2x-y\gt0\), along \((-2,1)\) where \(2x-y\lt0\); on the line \(y=2x\), \(\nabla g=\mathbf0\) (no direction of change — these are minimum points of \(g\)).

STRETCH · 8 marks Q4. Let \(f,g\) be twice differentiable, \(c\) a constant, and \(z(x,t)=f(x-ct)+g(x+ct)\). Show that \(\dfrac{\partial^2z}{\partial t^2}=c^2\dfrac{\partial^2z}{\partial x^2}\) (the wave equation).

Solution

Let \(u=x-ct\), \(v=x+ct\). Then \(z_x=f'(u)+g'(v)\), \(z_{xx}=f''(u)+g''(v)\). \(z_t=-cf'(u)+cg'(v)\), \(z_{tt}=c^2f''(u)+c^2g''(v)=c^2z_{xx}\), as required.

8.8 · Implicit differentiation

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — two points, two tangent lines

Q. Find the equation(s) of the tangent line(s) to \(x^2+xy+y^2=3\) at \(x=1\).

\(x=1\): \(y^2+y-2=0\Rightarrow y=1\) or \(y=-2\). Two points: \((1,1)\), \((1,-2)\).
\(F=x^2+xy+y^2-3\): \(y'=-\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}\).
At \((1,1)\): \(y'=-\tfrac33=-1\) ⇒ \(y=-x+2\).
At \((1,-2)\): \(y'=-\tfrac{0}{-3}=0\) ⇒ \(y=-2\) (horizontal tangent).

Worked B — implicit partials on a surface

Q. The equation \(xz+yz^2+x^2y=5\) defines \(z=z(x,y)\) near \((1,2,1)\). Find \(z_x\), \(z_y\) there and the linear approximation to \(z\).

(At \((1,2)\): \(2z^2+z-3=0\Rightarrow z=1\) or \(-\tfrac32\); we're on the \(z=1\) branch.) \(F=xz+yz^2+x^2y-5\): \(F_x=z+2xy=5,\ F_y=z^2+x^2=2,\ F_z=x+2yz=5\). $$z_x=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}=-1,\qquad z_y=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}=-\frac25,\qquad z\approx1-(x-1)-\tfrac25(y-2).$$

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 3 marks Q1. Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) at \((1,0)\) for the curve \(xe^y+y=1\).

Solution

\(F=xe^y+y-1\): \(F_x=e^y=1\), \(F_y=xe^y+1=2\). \(y'=-\tfrac12\).

STANDARD · 6 marks Q2. Show that the curve \(y^3+xy-x^2=1\) passes through exactly one point with \(x=1\), and find the tangent line there.

Solution

\(x=1\): \(y^3+y-2=0=(y-1)(y^2+y+2)\); the quadratic has discriminant \(-7\lt0\), so the only point is \((1,1)\). \(F_x=y-2x=-1\), \(F_y=3y^2+x=4\): \(y'=\tfrac14\). Tangent: \(y-1=\tfrac14(x-1)\iff y=\tfrac14x+\tfrac34\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 8 marks Q3. For the curve \(x^2-xy+y^2=3\): (a) find \(dy/dx\); [2] (b) find all points where the tangent is horizontal; [3] (c) find all points where the tangent is vertical. [3]

Solution

(a) \(y'=-\dfrac{2x-y}{2y-x}\).

(b) Horizontal: \(F_x=0\Rightarrow y=2x\). Substitute: \(x^2-2x^2+4x^2=3\Rightarrow x=\pm1\): \((1,2)\), \((-1,-2)\) (there \(F_y=3\ne0\), resp. \(-3\)).

(c) Vertical: \(F_y=0\Rightarrow x=2y\): \(4y^2-2y^2+y^2=3\Rightarrow y=\pm1\): \((2,1)\), \((-2,-1)\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 6 marks Q4. The surface \(x^2y+yz^2+zx=7\) passes through \((2,1,1)\). Find \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\) there, and the tangent plane.

Solution

Check \(4+1+2=7\) ✓. \(F_x=2xy+z=5\), \(F_y=x^2+z^2=5\), \(F_z=2yz+x=4\). \(z_x=-\tfrac54\), \(z_y=-\tfrac54\). Plane: \(5(x-2)+5(y-1)+4(z-1)=0\iff5x+5y+4z=19\).

§1 · Concepts in depth

Rusty on one-variable limits, L'Hôpital, or the chain rule? Read the Ground Floor first. This chapter reuses the standard limits \(\lim_{u\to0}\frac{\sin u}{u}=1\), \(\lim_{u\to0}\frac{e^u-1}{u}=1\), \(\lim_{u\to0}\frac{1-\cos u}{u^2}=\tfrac12\) and \(\lim_{u\to 0}\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}=1\) all the time.

🧭 Why two-variable limits are harder than one-variable ones

In one variable, \(x\to a\) can only happen from two directions: the left and the right. If the left-hand and right-hand limits agree, the limit exists. That's all there is to check.

In two variables, \((x,y)\to(a,b)\) can happen from infinitely many directions, along infinitely many curves: along the \(x\)-axis, along a line \(y=mx\), along a parabola \(y=x^2\), spiralling in, and so on. The definition says:

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y)=L \iff f(x,y)\text{ gets arbitrarily close to }L\text{ whenever }(x,y)\text{ is close enough to }(a,b)\text{, by ANY route.}$$

Every path has to give the same \(L\). That one fact produces the whole chapter's logic:

  • To show a limit does NOT exist, you need only two paths that give different answers (a counter-example). This is easy: two-path test.
  • To show a limit DOES exist, checking paths is never enough. You'd need infinitely many. Instead you must bound \(|f-L|\) by something that goes to 0 however you approach, usually with polar coordinates + the squeeze theorem, or by rewriting in terms of \(u=x^2+y^2\) and using a one-variable limit.

The summary puts it in one line: "the limit does not exist if \(f(x,y)\) approaches two different limits \(L_1\neq L_2\) along two different paths \(C_1,C_2\in D\) (the domain of \(f\))" (Summary p 5).

Step zero: is it just continuous? Then substitute.

Polynomials, \(e^u\), \(\sin u\), \(\cos u\), \(\ln u\) (for \(u>0\)), \(\sqrt u\) (for \(u>0\)), and every sum, product, quotient (with nonzero denominator) and composition of these are continuous wherever they are defined. At such a point the limit is just the value:

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,2)}\big(x^2y+3y-1\big)=1\cdot2+6-1=7.$$

The whole difficulty of the chapter lives at points where substitution gives \(\tfrac00\). For exam questions that's nearly always \((0,0)\), the one point excluded from the domain or the point where a piecewise function switches formula.

Before anything else, try to cancel: \(\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x-y}=x+y\) for \(x\ne y\), so \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(2,2)}\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x-y}=4\). Likewise \(\dfrac{x^4-y^4}{x^2+y^2}=x^2-y^2\to0\). A factorisation can make the whole problem go away.

🧭 The two-path test: proof by counter-example

If the limit existed and equalled \(L\), then every path into \((a,b)\) would have to give \(L\). So if you find

$$\lim_{\text{along }C_1} f = L_1 \quad\text{and}\quad \lim_{\text{along }C_2} f = L_2 \quad\text{with } L_1\ne L_2,$$

the limit cannot exist. Along a path, \(f\) becomes a function of one variable, so each path limit is an ordinary Calc-I limit.

How to pick the paths (in this order)
  1. The axes. \(y=0\) (so \(f(x,0)\), \(x\to0\)) and \(x=0\) (so \(f(0,y)\), \(y\to0\)). Quick, and often enough for "nice" rational functions like \(\frac{x^2-3y^2}{2x^2+y^2}\) (gives \(\tfrac12\) vs \(-3\)).
  2. Straight lines \(y=mx\). If the answer depends on \(m\), you're done: two different \(m\) give two different limits. E.g. \(\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\) on \(y=mx\) gives \(\frac{m}{1+m^2}\): \(0\) for \(m=0\) and \(\tfrac12\) for \(m=1\).
  3. Curves that "balance the degrees". If all lines give the same value (often 0), look for a curve that makes the terms in the denominator the same size. For \(\frac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\): \(x^4\) and \(y^2\) balance when \(y=x^2\). Along it, \(\frac{x^4}{2x^4}=\tfrac12\ne0\).
  4. Curves that kill a bracket. If the denominator contains something like \((x^2-y)^2\), choose \(y=x^2\) to make it vanish. (2025 S2 Q4: \(\frac{x^6}{(x^2-y)^2+x^6}\) along \(y=x^2\) is identically 1.)

Degree-balancing rule of thumb. For \(\dfrac{x^a y^b}{x^{p}+y^{q}}\), try \(y=x^{p/q}\): it makes \(x^p\) and \(y^q\) equal. For \(\frac{x^3y}{2x^6+y^2}\) (2024 S1 Q2), \(p/q=6/2=3\), so try \(y=x^3\). For \(\frac{3x^4y}{x^8+y^2}\), try \(y=x^4\). If the numerator then has the same total power as the denominator, you get a nonzero constant.

A path must lie in the domain of \(f\) and must actually pass through the point. "Along \(y=x+1\)" doesn't approach \((0,0)\). And for a function whose domain excludes a curve (2023 S2 Q8 excludes \(y=-x^{3/5}\)), a path that runs along that curve isn't allowed. The summary's "\(C_1,C_2\in D\)" is there for a reason.

🧭 Why "all straight lines agree" proves nothing

Take \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\). On every line \(y=mx\):

$$f(x,mx)=\frac{mx^3}{x^4+m^2x^2}=\frac{mx}{x^2+m^2}\to0\quad(m\neq0),$$

and on both axes \(f=0\). So every straight line gives 0. But on the parabola \(y=x^2\), \(f=\frac{x^4}{2x^4}=\tfrac12\). The limit does not exist. The surface has a ridge of height \(\tfrac12\) that runs along the parabola into the origin, and every straight line crosses that ridge instead of following it.

This is exactly why exam questions like 2024 S1 Q2 ask you first for lines, then parabolas, then "does the limit exist?". The first two parts are a trap: they both give 0, and the answer is still "no".

🧭 Proving a limit EXISTS: polar coordinates + squeeze

Put \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\). Then \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is the distance to the origin, and \((x,y)\to(0,0)\iff r\to0\), whatever \(\theta\) does. The key identity is \(x^2+y^2=r^2\).

Recipe (limit at the origin)
  1. Guess \(L\) (usually the common value along the axes/lines).
  2. Substitute \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) and simplify. \(x^2+y^2\to r^2\).
  3. Bound \(|f-L|\le g(r)\) with \(g\) independent of \(\theta\), using \(|\cos\theta|\le1,\ |\sin\theta|\le1\) (and \(|\sin(\text{anything})|\le1\)).
  4. Since \(g(r)\to0\), the squeeze theorem gives \(f\to L\).

Example. \(\dfrac{x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}=\dfrac{r^3(\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta)}{r^2}=r(\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta)\), so \(\left|f\right|\le 2r\to0\). The limit is 0.

Radial functions shortcut. If \(f\) depends only on \(x^2+y^2\) (like \(\frac{\sin(x^2+y^2)}{x^2+y^2}\) or \(\frac{e^{x^2+y^2}-1}{x^2+y^2}\)), set \(u=x^2+y^2=r^2\to0^+\). You're left with a one-variable limit, which you finish with a standard limit or L'Hôpital. No \(\theta\) appears at all (2024 S2 Q2(b), 2023 S1 Q2).

Polar coordinates are not magic. "For each fixed \(\theta\), \(\lim_{r\to0}=0\)" only checks straight lines through the origin (a fixed \(\theta\) is a line). For \(\frac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\), every fixed \(\theta\) gives 0, yet the limit DNE. The polar argument is only valid when you have a bound \(|f-L|\le g(r)\) that holds for all \(\theta\) at once. If \(\theta\) survives in a denominator (e.g. \(\frac{\cos^2\theta\sin\theta}{r^2\cos^4\theta+\sin^2\theta}\)), you have no such bound. Go looking for a path.

If the polar form simplifies to something depending on \(\theta\) but not on \(r\) (e.g. \(\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}=\cos\theta\sin\theta\)), the limit does not exist: different directions give different constants. Back it up with two explicit paths.

Continuity: three conditions, one sentence

\(f\) is continuous at \((a,b)\) if

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y)=f(a,b).$$

Hidden inside are three checks: (i) \(f(a,b)\) is defined; (ii) the limit exists; (iii) they are equal. A continuity question at a piecewise point is therefore always:

  1. State the definition (a mark on the 2025 S1 marking scheme is literally "1 mark for definition of continuity").
  2. Decide whether the limit exists: two-path test (if no) or polar/squeeze (if yes).
  3. If it exists, compare with the given value \(f(a,b)\) (or \(c\)).
  4. Conclude in words: "Hence \(f\) is / is not continuous at \((0,0)\)."

Removable discontinuity (2023 S1 Q2): the limit exists but \(\ne f(a,b)\). You could "remove" it by redefining the single value \(f(a,b)\). "Find \(c\) so \(f\) is continuous" means "compute the limit and set \(c\) equal to it" (2021 S2 Q3). If the limit doesn't exist, no \(c\) works (2021 S1 Q2(b)).

🧭 Partial derivatives: slopes of cross-sections

Freeze \(y=b\) and you're left with a curve \(z=f(x,b)\): the vertical cross-section of the surface in the \(x\)-direction. Its slope at \(x=a\) is the partial derivative

$$f_x(a,b)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h},\qquad f_y(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h}.$$

In practice: differentiate as usual with respect to one variable, treating the other(s) as constants. For \(f=x^2\sin(3y)+ye^{2x}\): \(f_x=2x\sin 3y+2ye^{2x}\) (the \(\sin3y\) and \(y\) are constant coefficients), \(f_y=3x^2\cos3y+e^{2x}\). The same idea works for any number of variables: \(f_z\) means "only \(z\) moves".

When you MUST use the limit definition: at a point where \(f\) is defined by a separate formula (a piecewise point like \((0,0)\)). The rule-based derivative of the "\((x,y)\ne(0,0)\)" formula says nothing about the value at the origin, where \(f\) is defined differently. So you compute

$$f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h},$$

using the top formula for \(f(h,0)\) (since \((h,0)\ne(0,0)\)) and the given value for \(f(0,0)\). Only the axes matter here: \(f_x(0,0)\) only ever looks at points \((h,0)\), and \(f_y(0,0)\) only at \((0,h)\).

🧭 The big surprise: partials can exist where \(f\) isn't even continuous

Because \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) only probe the two axes, they are blind to everything else. In 2025 S2 Q4, \(f=\frac{x^6}{(x^2-y)^2+x^6}\) is identically 0 on the \(y\)-axis and equals \(\frac{h^2}{1+h^2}\) on the \(x\)-axis, so both partials exist (both are 0). But along \(y=x^2\), \(f\equiv1\), so \(f\) isn't continuous at the origin.

The logical hierarchy (memorise this direction of arrows):

$$f_x,\ f_y\ \text{exist and are continuous near }(a,b)\ \Longrightarrow\ f\ \text{differentiable (smooth) at }(a,b)\ \Longrightarrow\ f\ \text{continuous at }(a,b).$$

None of the arrows reverse, and "partials merely exist" implies nothing. The summary's "smoothness (differentiability)" means \(f,f_x,f_y\) are all continuous at \((a,b)\): then, zoomed in close enough, the surface looks like a plane, and the tangent plane is a good approximation.

The reverse can happen too: \(f\) can be continuous at a point while a partial fails to exist (e.g. \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) at the origin: a cone's tip, where \(\frac{|h|}{h}\) has no limit). And a function can be discontinuous with one partial existing and the other not (see Example 5).

Higher-order partials & Clairaut's theorem

Differentiate again: \(f_{xx}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\), \(f_{yy}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}\), and the mixed partials \(f_{xy}=(f_x)_y=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}\), \(f_{yx}=(f_y)_x=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}\). Subscripts are read left to right (differentiate by \(x\) first, then \(y\)). The \(\partial\) notation reads right to left.

Clairaut's theorem: if \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are continuous (true for every "formula" function you meet away from bad points), then \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\). Use it as a built-in error check: compute both. If they disagree, you've made an algebra slip.

These second partials feed the quadratic approximation (below) and the Hessian test in Ch 6.

🧭 Tangent plane = the two tangent lines, glued together

At \((a,b)\), the \(x\)-cross-section has tangent line with slope \(f_x(a,b)\), and the \(y\)-cross-section has slope \(f_y(a,b)\). There is exactly one plane containing both lines:

$$z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b).$$

It's the 2-D version of \(y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\). Rearranged as \(f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)-(z-f(a,b))=0\), it shows a normal vector \(\mathbf n=\langle f_x(a,b),\,f_y(a,b),\,-1\rangle\) (2020 S2 Q5(ii) asks for exactly this).

Two surfaces have a common tangent plane at a point if they have the same height there and the same \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) (2021 S1 Q1).

🧭 Linear approximation, small changes and error

Near \((a,b)\), a smooth surface is almost its tangent plane, so

$$f(x,y)\approx P(x,y)=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b).$$

(\(P\) is the formula sheet's name; the summary also calls it \(L\).) Writing \(\Delta x=x-a\), \(\Delta y=y-b\), \(\Delta z=f(x,y)-f(a,b)\), you get the small-change formula

$$\Delta z\approx f_x(a,b)\,\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\,\Delta y.$$

If \(x\) and \(y\) are measured with errors at most \(E_1,E_2\) (signs unknown), the worst case is when every term pushes the same way. That gives the maximum-error estimate (Summary p 5):

$$|E|\approx|f_x(a,b)|E_1+|f_y(a,b)|E_2.$$

Everything extends to 3 variables with an extra \(f_z(c)(z-c)\) term (2022 S2 Q1).

Relative / percentage error shortcut for products of powers: if \(f=Cx^py^q\), then \(\frac{|E|}{|f|}\approx |p|\frac{E_1}{x}+|q|\frac{E_2}{y}\). So 1% error in \(x\) and 2% in \(y\) give at most \(2(1\%)+3(2\%)=8\%\) in \(x^2y^3\).

Quadratic approximation: add the curvature

The tangent plane captures slope only. Adding the second-order Taylor terms captures curvature (formula sheet):

$$Q(x,y)=P(x,y)+\tfrac12 f_{xx}(a,b)(x-a)^2+f_{xy}(a,b)(x-a)(y-b)+\tfrac12 f_{yy}(a,b)(y-b)^2.$$

Note the mixed term has no \(\tfrac12\). That's because \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) each contribute \(\tfrac12\), and they're equal by Clairaut. \(Q\) is more accurate than \(P\) close to \((a,b)\): in 2022 S1 Q1, \(Q(1.1,1)=0.305\) vs the true \(0.305310\ldots\), while \(P\) gives \(0.3\).

§2 · Key methods & when to use them

The formula sheet prints \(P(x,y)\) and \(Q(x,y)\), and nothing else from this chapter. The limit definitions of \(f_x,f_y\), the definition of continuity, the two-path test and the polar method must be known cold. Marks are given for writing the definition down.

M1 · Direct substitution / cancel first

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y)=f(a,b)\quad\text{if } f \text{ is continuous at }(a,b).$$
The point isn't a "bad" point: no zero denominator, log of 0, etc. Or the \(\frac00\) disappears after factorising/cancelling.

Mini-example. \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,2)}(x^2y+3y-1)=7\) (polynomial, so substitute). Always say why: "\(f\) is a polynomial, hence continuous".

M2 · Two-path test (limit does NOT exist)

$$\lim_{C_1}f=L_1\ne L_2=\lim_{C_2}f\ \Longrightarrow\ \lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)\ \text{does not exist.}$$
Substitution gives \(\frac00\), and different directions seem to give different values. Classic signal: numerator and denominator of the same "weighted degree" (\(\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\), \(\frac{x^3y}{x^6+y^2}\), \(\frac{x^6}{(x^2-y)^2+x^6}\)).
Recipe: axes → lines \(y=mx\) → degree-balancing curve \(y=kx^n\) (or a curve that kills a bracket). Evaluate each as a 1-variable limit. State "\(L_1\ne L_2\), so the limit does not exist."

M3 · Polar coordinates + squeeze (limit DOES exist)

$$x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\quad |f(x,y)-L|\le g(r)\to0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \lim f=L.$$
The numerator has higher total degree than the denominator (\(\frac{x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}\), \(\frac{3xy^2}{x^2+y^2}\)), or there's a bounded oscillating factor (\(y\sin\frac1{x^2+y^2}\), 2021 S2 Q3).

Mini-example. \(\frac{3xy^2}{x^2+y^2}=3r\cos\theta\sin^2\theta\), so \(|f|\le 3r\to0\).

M4 · Radial substitution \(u=x^2+y^2\)

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} F(x^2+y^2)=\lim_{u\to0^+}F(u).$$
\(x,y\) appear only through \(x^2+y^2\): \(\frac{\sin(9x^2+9y^2)}{6x^2+6y^2}\) (2023 S1 Q2), \(\frac{e^{x^2+y^2}-1}{x^2+y^2}\) (2024 S2 Q2(b)).

Mini-example. \(\frac{\sin 9u}{6u}=\frac{9}{6}\cdot\frac{\sin 9u}{9u}\to\frac32\). Or use L'Hôpital in \(u\) (or in \(r\)), as the official 2024 S2 solution does.

M5 · Continuity at a piecewise point / "find \(c\)"

$$f \text{ continuous at }(a,b)\iff \lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=f(a,b).$$
"Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)?", "Does \(g\) have a removable discontinuity?", "Find \(c\) such that \(f\) is continuous".
  1. Write the definition.
  2. Limit: DNE (M2) ⇒ not continuous, for any value of \(f(0,0)\). Exists (M3/M4) ⇒ compare with \(f(0,0)\).
  3. Conclude in words.

M6 · Partial derivative from the definition

$$f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h},\qquad f_y(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}.$$
"Use the definition of the partial derivative", or any "do \(f_x(0,0)\), \(f_y(0,0)\) exist?" for a piecewise \(f\). Also when the rule-based formula is undefined at the point.

Mini-example. \(f=\frac{2x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\): \(f(h,0)=2h\), so \(f_x(0,0)=\lim\frac{2h-0}{h}=2\). \(f(0,h)=h\), so \(f_y(0,0)=1\).

Use the given \(f(0,0)\) (it may be 4, or 1, or \(c\)), not 0 by habit. In 2025 S1 Q2, \(f(0,0)=1\) and \(f(h,0)=1\), so the quotient is \(\frac{1-1}{h}=0\).

M7 · Partial derivatives by rules; Clairaut check

$$f_{xy}=f_{yx}\quad\text{(Clairaut, when both are continuous)}.$$
Any "find \(f_x\), \(f_y\)" or "evaluate \(f_z(2,1,0)\)". Also the first step of every tangent-plane, approximation, gradient and optimisation question.

Mini-example. \(f=x^3y^2+\sin(xy)\): \(f_x=3x^2y^2+y\cos(xy)\), \(f_{xy}=6x^2y+\cos(xy)-xy\sin(xy)\). The same answer comes from \(f_y=2x^3y+x\cos(xy)\) differentiated by \(x\). ✓

M8 · Tangent plane (and normal vector)

$$z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b),\qquad \mathbf n=\langle f_x(a,b),f_y(a,b),-1\rangle.$$
"Equation of the tangent plane to \(z=f(x,y)\) at \((a,b)\)", "normal vector to the plane", "common tangent plane".

Mini-example. \(f=x^2+3xy\) at \((1,2)\): \(f=7\), \(f_x=2x+3y=8\), \(f_y=3x=3\), so \(z=7+8(x-1)+3(y-2)\), i.e. \(z=8x+3y-7\).

M9 · Linear approximation / small change

$$f(a+\Delta x,b+\Delta y)\approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y.$$
"Use the linear approximation (tangent plane) to estimate \(f(-0.95,0.95)\)", "estimate \(\sqrt{(3.02)^2+(1.97)^2+(5.99)^2}\)", "approximate change in \(V\) when \(r\) increases by… and \(h\) decreases by…".
Recipe: choose a nearby base point where \(f\) is exact (integers, \(\ln 1\), \(\sqrt{49}\)) → compute \(f,f_x,f_y\) there → plug in \(\Delta x,\Delta y\) with their signs → simplify exactly, then use the given decimal hint (e.g. \(\ln2\approx0.6931\)).

M10 · Worst-case (maximum) error

$$|E|\approx|f_x(a,b)|\,E_1+|f_y(a,b)|\,E_2\ (+\,|f_z|E_3).$$
"Measured with an error of at most…", "estimate the maximum error in the calculated…". If errors are given as percentages, convert: \(0.5\%\) of 40 is \(0.2\).

Mini-example. \(A=xy\), \(x=10\), \(y=5\), each error \(\le0.1\): \(|E|\approx5(0.1)+10(0.1)=1.5\).

M11 · Quadratic approximation

$$Q=P+\tfrac12f_{xx}(x-a)^2+f_{xy}(x-a)(y-b)+\tfrac12f_{yy}(y-b)^2.$$
"Find the quadratic (second-order) approximation", "use it to estimate…". It's on the formula sheet, so the marks are for correct second partials and clean substitution.

Mini-example. \(e^x\cos y\) at \((0,0)\): \(Q=1+x+\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac12y^2\).

Example 1 — Limit DNE using the axes: \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2-3y^2}{2x^2+y^2}\)

Q. Determine whether the limit exists. [5 marks]

Path 1: the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\), \(x\ne0\)).

$$f(x,0)=\frac{x^2}{2x^2}=\frac12\ \Longrightarrow\ \lim_{x\to0}f(x,0)=\frac12=L_1.$$

Path 2: the \(y\)-axis (\(x=0\), \(y\ne0\)).

$$f(0,y)=\frac{-3y^2}{y^2}=-3\ \Longrightarrow\ \lim_{y\to0}f(0,y)=-3=L_2.$$

Conclusion. Since \(L_1=\tfrac12\ne-3=L_2\), \(f\) approaches different values along two different paths, so \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) does not exist.

Along \(y=mx\) you get \(\frac{1-3m^2}{2+m^2}\), which varies with \(m\). That also works, but the axes are quicker. The marking scheme gives ~2 marks per path + 1 for the conclusion (2025 S1 Q2), so show both computations and write the conclusion sentence.

Example 2 — Lines all agree, a curve doesn't: \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{3x^4y}{x^8+y^2}\)

Q. (a) Find the limit along every line \(y=mx\) and along \(x=0\). (b) Show the limit does not exist. [4 + 5 marks]

(a) Along \(y=mx\) (\(x\ne0\)):

$$f(x,mx)=\frac{3mx^5}{x^8+m^2x^2}=\frac{3mx^3}{x^6+m^2}\ \xrightarrow[x\to0]{}\ \frac{0}{m^2}=0\quad(m\ne0),$$

and for \(m=0\), \(f(x,0)=0\). Along \(x=0\), \(f(0,y)=0\). So every straight line through the origin gives 0.

(b) Balance the denominator: \(x^8\) and \(y^2\) are equal when \(y=x^4\). Along \(y=kx^4\):

$$f(x,kx^4)=\frac{3kx^8}{x^8+k^2x^8}=\frac{3k}{1+k^2}.$$

With \(k=1\) (the path \(y=x^4\)): \(f\equiv\tfrac32\), so the limit along it is \(\tfrac32\). Along the \(x\)-axis the limit is 0. Since \(\tfrac32\ne0\), the limit does not exist.

Part (a) gives the same value on every line, and that is not evidence the limit exists. Straight lines are only a tiny family of paths.

Example 3 — Proving a limit exists (polar + squeeze): \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}\)

Q. Evaluate the limit, if it exists. [6 marks]

Guess. Along the axes: \(f(x,0)=x\to0\), \(f(0,y)=y\to0\). Candidate \(L=0\).

Polar coordinates. Let \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), so \((x,y)\to(0,0)\iff r\to0^+\) for any \(\theta\):

$$f=\frac{r^3(\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta)}{r^2}=r(\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta).$$

Bound independent of \(\theta\). Since \(|\cos\theta|\le1\) and \(|\sin\theta|\le1\),

$$0\le|f(x,y)-0|=r\,|\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta|\le 2r.$$

As \(r\to0\), \(2r\to0\), so by the squeeze theorem \(|f|\to0\) regardless of the path. Hence \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}=0\).

The phrase that earns the mark: "the bound \(2r\) does not depend on \(\theta\), so the convergence holds along every path".

Example 4 — Find \(c\) for continuity (radial function)

Q. Let \(f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\cos(x^2+y^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}, &(x,y)\ne(0,0)\\ c, &(x,y)=(0,0)\end{cases}\). Find \(c\) such that \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\). [8 marks]

\(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\) iff \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)=f(0,0)=c\). So compute the limit.

\(f\) depends only on \(u=x^2+y^2\), and \((x,y)\to(0,0)\iff u\to0^+\):

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f=\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{1-\cos u}{u^2}\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{\sin u}{2u}=\frac12.$$

(L'Hôpital applies since it is \(\frac00\). Alternatively, \(1-\cos u=2\sin^2\frac u2\) gives \(\frac{2\sin^2(u/2)}{u^2}=\frac12\big(\frac{\sin(u/2)}{u/2}\big)^2\to\frac12\).)

Conclusion. The limit exists and equals \(\tfrac12\), so \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\) exactly when \(\boxed{c=\tfrac12}\). For any other \(c\), the discontinuity is removable.

Example 5 — The full piecewise question: continuity + partials from the definition

Q. Let \(f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^4}{(x^2-y)^2+x^4}, &(x,y)\ne(0,0)\\ 0, &(x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}\)

(a) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? Justify. [7 marks] (b) Determine whether \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) exist. [5 marks]

(a) \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\) iff \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)=f(0,0)=0\).

Along \(x=0\) (\(y\ne0\)): \(f(0,y)=\frac{0}{y^2}=0\), so the limit along this path is \(L_1=0\).

Along \(y=x^2\) (\(x\ne0\)), which kills the bracket: \(f(x,x^2)=\frac{x^4}{0+x^4}=1\), so \(L_2=1\).

\(L_1\ne L_2\), so \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f\) does not exist. Hence \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).

(b) \((0,0)\) is a piecewise point, so use the definition.

$$f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{h^4}{h^4+h^4}-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1/2}{h},$$

which does not exist (\(\to+\infty\) as \(h\to0^+\), \(-\infty\) as \(h\to0^-\)). So \(f_x(0,0)\) does not exist.

$$f_y(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{0}{h^2}-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}0=0.$$

So \(f_y(0,0)\) exists and equals 0.

Compare with the real 2025 S2 Q4 (§4 Type C), where both partials exist and \(f\) is still discontinuous. The partials only see the axes. Here \(f(h,0)=\tfrac12\ne f(0,0)\), so \(f\) even jumps along the \(x\)-axis.

Example 6 — Partials by rules, second partials, Clairaut check

Q. For \(f(x,y)=x^2\sin(3y)+ye^{2x}\), find all first- and second-order partial derivatives, verify Clairaut's theorem, and evaluate \(f_{xy}(0,0)\). [6 marks]

$$f_x=2x\sin3y+2ye^{2x},\qquad f_y=3x^2\cos3y+e^{2x}.$$ $$f_{xx}=2\sin3y+4ye^{2x},\qquad f_{yy}=-9x^2\sin3y.$$ $$f_{xy}=(f_x)_y=6x\cos3y+2e^{2x},\qquad f_{yx}=(f_y)_x=6x\cos3y+2e^{2x}.$$

So \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\), as Clairaut's theorem guarantees (all second partials are continuous on \(\mathbb R^2\)). And \(f_{xy}(0,0)=0+2=2\).

Example 7 — Tangent plane, normal vector, linear approximation

Q. Let \(f(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+3y}\). (a) Find the tangent plane to \(z=f(x,y)\) at \((1,1)\). (b) Give a normal vector to it. (c) Use the linear approximation to estimate \(f(1.04,\,0.96)\). [4 + 2 + 4 marks]

(a) \(f(1,1)=\sqrt4=2\).

$$f_x=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+3y}}\Rightarrow f_x(1,1)=\frac12,\qquad f_y=\frac{3}{2\sqrt{x^2+3y}}\Rightarrow f_y(1,1)=\frac34.$$ $$z=2+\tfrac12(x-1)+\tfrac34(y-1)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z=\tfrac12x+\tfrac34y+\tfrac34.$$

(b) \(\mathbf n=\langle f_x,f_y,-1\rangle=\langle\tfrac12,\tfrac34,-1\rangle\), or any multiple such as \(\langle2,3,-4\rangle\).

(c) \(\Delta x=0.04\), \(\Delta y=-0.04\):

$$f(1.04,0.96)\approx2+\tfrac12(0.04)+\tfrac34(-0.04)=2+0.02-0.03=1.99.$$

(True value \(\sqrt{3.9616}=1.99037\ldots\). The approximation is excellent because \((1.04,0.96)\) is close to \((1,1)\).)

Example 8 — Maximum error in a computed quantity

Q. The radius and height of a cylinder are measured as \(r=3\) cm and \(h=10\) cm, with errors of at most \(0.05\) cm and \(0.1\) cm respectively. Estimate the maximum error in the computed volume \(V=\pi r^2h\), and the maximum relative error. [6 marks]

$$V_r=2\pi rh=60\pi,\qquad V_h=\pi r^2=9\pi\quad(\text{at }r=3,\ h=10).$$ $$|E|\approx|V_r|E_r+|V_h|E_h=60\pi(0.05)+9\pi(0.1)=3\pi+0.9\pi=3.9\pi\ \text{cm}^3\ (\approx12.25\ \text{cm}^3).$$

Relative error: \(\dfrac{3.9\pi}{V}=\dfrac{3.9\pi}{90\pi}=\dfrac{13}{300}\approx4.3\%\).

Check with the percentage shortcut: \(V\propto r^2h\), so relative error \(\approx2\cdot\frac{0.05}{3}+\frac{0.1}{10}=\frac1{30}+\frac1{100}=\frac{13}{300}\). ✓

Type A — "Show the limit does not exist" (two-path test) ~10–14 marks

Looks like: a rational function with \(\frac00\) at the origin, often with scaffolding: "find the limit along \(y=mx\)… along \(y=kx^2\)… does the limit exist?" (2024 S1 Q2), or "find \(\lim_{y\to0}g(\alpha y,y)\)… show the limit does not exist" (2023 S2 Q8).

Routine:
  1. Name each path explicitly ("Along \(y=x^3\), \(x\ne0\)…") and substitute to get a one-variable function.
  2. Simplify and take the one-variable limit. Keep the parameter (\(m\), \(k\)) and handle the special case \(m=0\)/\(k=0\) separately.
  3. If the scaffolded families all agree, balance degrees to find a new path.
  4. Conclude: "\(L_1\ne L_2\), so the limit does not exist. Hence \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\)" (if continuity is asked).
Worked past-paper example — 2024 S1 Q2 (12 marks)

Q. Consider \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^3y}{2x^6+y^2}\) on \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\). (a) What value does \(f\) approach as \((x,y)\to(0,0)\) along \(y=mx\)? [4] (b) … along \(y=kx^2\)? [4] (c) Does \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) exist? Explain. [4]

(a) \(f(x,mx)=\dfrac{mx^4}{2x^6+m^2x^2}=\dfrac{mx^2}{2x^4+m^2}\to0\) as \(x\to0\) (for \(m\ne0\). For \(m=0\), \(f\equiv0\)). Answer: 0.

(b) \(f(x,kx^2)=\dfrac{kx^5}{2x^6+k^2x^4}=\dfrac{kx}{2x^2+k^2}\to0\) (\(k\ne0\). For \(k=0\), \(f\equiv0\)). Answer: 0.

(c) Balance \(x^6\) with \(y^2\): take \(y=x^3\). Then \(f(x,x^3)=\dfrac{x^6}{2x^6+x^6}=\dfrac13\to\dfrac13\). Along \(y=mx\) the limit is 0, but along \(y=x^3\) it is \(\tfrac13\). Different limits along two paths, so the limit does not exist.

Parts (a) and (b) are bait. Answering "(c) yes, 0" loses all 4 marks. In general \(y=kx^3\) gives \(\frac{k}{2+k^2}\), which depends on \(k\).

Also: 2023 S2 Q8 (14 marks), a domain-aware path

\(g(x,y)=\dfrac{2y^3x^2}{y^5+x^3}\) on \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{y=-x^{3/5}\}\). (a) \(g(\alpha y,y)=\dfrac{2\alpha^2y^5}{y^3(y^2+\alpha^3)}=\dfrac{2\alpha^2y^2}{y^2+\alpha^3}\to0\) for every \(\alpha\) (and \(g(0,y)=0\)). (b) Along \(y=0\): \(g=0\to0\). The official solution then hugs the excluded curve: along \(y=-x^{3/5}(x^{4/5}+1)\) (which stays in the domain for small \(x>0\)), the limit is \(\tfrac25\). Since \(0\ne\tfrac25\), the limit does not exist. Lesson: when the denominator can vanish near the origin, a path that runs close to the zero set of the denominator makes \(g\) do anything you like.

Type B — "Evaluate the limit / find \(c\) / removable discontinuity" (limit EXISTS) ~6–12 marks

Looks like: a function of \(x^2+y^2\) only, or a bounded factor times something that vanishes. Wording: "evaluate the limit, if it exists", "find \(c\) such that \(f\) is continuous", "does \(g\) have a removable discontinuity?"

Routine: polar coordinates (or \(u=x^2+y^2\)) → reduce to \(r\to0\) → standard limit / L'Hôpital / squeeze with a \(\theta\)-free bound → compare with \(f(0,0)\) → conclude.
Worked past-paper example — 2023 S1 Q2 (12 marks)

Q. A function has a removable discontinuity at \((a,b)\) if \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f\) exists but \(f\) is not continuous at \((a,b)\). Let \(g(x,y)=\dfrac{\sin(9x^2+9y^2)}{6x^2+6y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(g(0,0)=0\). Does \(g\) have a removable discontinuity at \((0,0)\)?

Use polar coordinates: \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), and \((x,y)\to(0,0)\iff r\to0\).

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g=\lim_{r\to0}\frac{\sin(9r^2)}{6r^2}=\frac96\lim_{r\to0}\frac{\sin(9r^2)}{9r^2}=\frac32\cdot1=\frac32.$$

So the limit exists and equals \(\tfrac32\), but \(g(0,0)=0\ne\tfrac32\). Hence \(g\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\), and yes, the discontinuity is removable (redefining \(g(0,0)=\tfrac32\) would fix it).

Also: 2021 S2 Q3 (10 marks) and 2024 S2 Q2(b) (6 marks)

2021 S2 Q3: \(f=y\sin\frac1{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=c\). Polar: \(f=r\sin\theta\sin\frac1{r^2}\), and \(-r\le r\sin\theta\sin\frac1{r^2}\le r\) for all \(\theta\). Squeeze gives limit 0, so \(c=0\) makes \(f\) continuous.

Type C — Piecewise function: continuity + partial derivatives at the origin 12 marks

Looks like: "Let \(f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\ldots&(x,y)\ne(0,0)\\ \ldots&(x,y)=(0,0)\end{cases}\). (a) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? (b) Do \(f_x(0,0)\), \(f_y(0,0)\) exist?" This is the flagship of 2025 S2, 2025 S1 and 2022 S2.

Routine:
  1. (a) Definition of continuity → Type A or B on the limit → compare with \(f(0,0)\) → conclude.
  2. (b) Write \(f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}\) in full (2–3 marks for the definition alone) → evaluate \(f(h,0)\) from the top formula → simplify → state "exists and equals …" or "does not exist because …". Repeat for \(f_y\) with \(f(0,h)\).
Worked past-paper example — 2025 S2 Q4 (12 marks)

Q. Let \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^6}{(x^2-y)^2+x^6}\) if \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), and \(f(0,0)=0\). (a) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? Justify. [7] (b) Determine whether \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) exist. Justify. [5]

(a) \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\) iff \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)=f(0,0)=0\).

Along \(x=0\) (\(y\ne0\)): \(f(0,y)=\frac{0}{y^2}=0\Rightarrow L_1=0\).

Along \(y=x^2\) (\(x\ne0\)): \(f(x,x^2)=\frac{x^6}{(x^2-x^2)^2+x^6}=\frac{x^6}{x^6}=1\Rightarrow L_2=1\).

\(L_1\ne L_2\), so the limit does not exist, and therefore \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).

(b)

$$f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{h^6}{h^4+h^6}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h^6}{h^5(1+h^2)}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h}{1+h^2}=0.$$ $$f_y(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{0-0}{h}=0.$$

Both partial derivatives exist and equal 0, even though \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).

Why a curve was essential: every straight line \(y=mx\) gives \(\frac{x^6}{x^2(x-m)^2+x^6}\to0\) too. Only the path that makes \((x^2-y)\) vanish exposes the discontinuity.

Type D — Tangent plane + linear approximation ~10–12 marks

Worked past-paper example — 2022 S1 Q1 (10 marks)

Q. \(f(x,y)=x^2y-y^3+\ln x\). (a) Find the quadratic approximation at \((1,1)\). [5] (b) Use it to approximate \(f(1.1,1)\). [5]

(a) \(f(1,1)=0\). \(f_x=2xy+\frac1x\Rightarrow3\). \(f_y=x^2-3y^2\Rightarrow-2\). \(f_{xx}=2y-\frac1{x^2}\Rightarrow1\). \(f_{xy}=2x\Rightarrow2\). \(f_{yy}=-6y\Rightarrow-6\).

$$Q=3(x-1)-2(y-1)+\tfrac12(x-1)^2+2(x-1)(y-1)-3(y-1)^2.$$

(b) \(x-1=0.1,\ y-1=0\): \(Q=0.3+\tfrac12(0.01)=0.305\). (Actual \(0.305310\ldots\).)

§6 · Traps & marks lost

Using paths to "prove" a limit exists. "Along the axes and along \(y=mx\) the limit is 0, so the limit is 0" scores nothing. Agreement along any number of paths proves nothing. Existence needs a \(\theta\)-free bound (polar + squeeze) or a reduction to one variable (\(u=x^2+y^2\)).
Polar with \(\theta\) left in the denominator. Writing \(\lim_{r\to0}\frac{r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta}{r^2\cos^4\theta+\sin^2\theta}=0\) "for each \(\theta\)" is only the straight-line check in disguise. It fails near \(\sin\theta=0\), where the denominator is tiny. Only accept polar when you can bound by \(g(r)\) alone.
Stopping after the scaffolded paths. 2024 S1 Q2 asks for lines, then parabolas \(y=kx^2\) (both give 0), then "does the limit exist?". The answer is no (use \(y=x^3\) to get \(\tfrac13\)). If the question pointedly asks "does it exist?" after two agreeing families, be suspicious.
Missing the conclusion sentences. The 2025 S1 and 2022 S2 marking schemes award a separate mark for "\(L_1\ne L_2\) so the limit does not exist" and another for "hence \(f\) is not continuous". State the definition of continuity (1 mark) and say the words.
Differentiating the top formula to get \(f_x(0,0)\). The formula for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\) says nothing about the origin, where \(f\) is defined separately. Often the derivative formula is \(\frac00\) there anyway. You must use \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}\). Writing the definition is 2–3 marks on its own (2025 S1 MS: 2 marks; 2022 S2 MS: 3 marks).
Using \(f(0,0)=0\) when it isn't. 2022 S1 Q3 sets \(f(0,0)=4\). 2025 S1 Q2 has \(f(0,0)=1\). Read the bottom line of the piecewise definition and use that value in the difference quotient.
"Partials exist ⇒ continuous". False (2025 S2 Q4: both partials are 0, but \(f\) is discontinuous). And "not continuous ⇒ partials don't exist" is also false. Answer the two parts independently.
Paths outside the domain or not through the point. A path must pass through \((a,b)\) and lie in \(D\). For 2024 S2 Q2(a), the limit is at \((1,0)\), so the paths are \(x=1\) and \(y=0\), not the axes through the origin. For 2023 S2 Q8 the curve \(y=-x^{3/5}\) is excluded.
Sign of \(\Delta x\) with negative base points. 2025 S2 Q2(c): \(x-a=-0.95-(-1)=+0.05\). A wrong sign flips the whole estimate. (The official solution even contains the typo "\(f(-0.95,1.95)\)". Copy the point from the question, not from memory.)
Rounding too early. No calculator. Keep \(\ln 2\), \(e^6\), \(\pi\), \(\frac{0.06}{7}\) exact until the last line, then use the given hint (\(\ln2\approx0.6931\)). Marks are for the method and a clean exact form.
Forgetting the absolute values in the maximum error. The errors can have either sign, so the worst case adds magnitudes: \(|f_x|E_1+|f_y|E_2\). A negative partial must be made positive. (Contrast: "estimate the change" keeps signs.)
Quadratic approximation: \(\tfrac12\) on the mixed term. The formula sheet has \(\tfrac12f_{xx}\), \(f_{xy}\) (no half), \(\tfrac12f_{yy}\). Putting \(\tfrac12\) on \(f_{xy}\) is the classic slip.
Tangent plane not written as a plane. Leave the answer as \(z=\ldots\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\) (expanded or point form). Don't stop at listing \(f_x\) and \(f_y\). And the normal vector's third component is \(-1\), not \(+1\) or \(f(a,b)\).

§7 · Self-check

1. Why can the two-path test prove a limit does NOT exist, but never that it DOES?

If the limit existed, every path would give it. So one disagreement is a counter-example. Existence is a claim about all (infinitely many) paths, and finitely many checks can't cover them.

2. \(f=\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\). Limit along \(y=x\)? Along the \(x\)-axis? Conclusion?

\(y=x\): \(\frac{x^2}{2x^2}=\frac12\). \(x\)-axis: 0. Different, so the limit DNE (2022 S2 Q2(b)).

3. Which path would you try for \(\dfrac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\)? For \(\dfrac{xy^3}{x^2+y^6}\)?

Balance the denominator: \(y=x^2\) (gives \(\tfrac12\)). For the second, \(x=y^3\) (gives \(\tfrac12\)). Lines give 0 in both cases.

4. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{\sin\big(2(x^2+y^2)\big)}{x^2+y^2}\).

\(u=x^2+y^2\to0^+\): \(\frac{\sin2u}{u}=2\cdot\frac{\sin2u}{2u}\to2\).

5. In polar form, \(\frac{x^2y^2}{x^2+y^2}=\)? What's the limit?

\(r^2\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta\), with \(|\cdot|\le r^2\to0\). So the limit is 0.

6. State the three conditions hidden in "\(f\) is continuous at \((a,b)\)".

\(f(a,b)\) is defined. \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f\) exists. The two are equal.

7. Write the limit definition of \(f_y(0,0)\). Which points does it look at?

\(f_y(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}\). Only points \((0,h)\) on the \(y\)-axis.

8. True or false: if \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) both exist, then \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\).

False. 2025 S2 Q4 is a counter-example: both partials are 0, but the limit along \(y=x^2\) is 1 while \(f(0,0)=0\).

9. What does Clairaut's theorem say, and how do you use it in an exam?

If \(f_{xy},f_{yx}\) are continuous, then \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\). Use it as a free error check: compute both mixed partials and confirm they match.

10. Tangent plane to \(z=xe^y\) at \((2,0)\)?

\(f=2\), \(f_x=e^y=1\), \(f_y=xe^y=2\). So \(z=2+(x-2)+2y=x+2y\).

11. A normal vector to the tangent plane of \(z=f(x,y)\) at \((a,b)\)?

\(\langle f_x(a,b),\,f_y(a,b),\,-1\rangle\).

12. "Estimate the change" vs "estimate the maximum error": what's the difference in the formula?

Change: \(\Delta z\approx f_x\Delta x+f_y\Delta y\) with actual signed increments. Maximum error: \(|f_x|E_1+|f_y|E_2\). All terms are positive (worst case).

13. 1% error in \(x\), 2% in \(y\). Max % error in \(x^2y^3\)?

\(2(1\%)+3(2\%)=8\%\).

14. Which coefficient in the quadratic approximation has no \(\tfrac12\)?

The mixed term \(f_{xy}(a,b)(x-a)(y-b)\).

15. Can a function be continuous at a point where \(f_x\) doesn't exist?

Yes. \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) at the origin (a cone's tip) is continuous, but \(\frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\frac{|h|}{h}\) has no limit.

8.1 · Limits by substitution, cancelling & standard limits

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — cancel the \(\tfrac00\)

Q. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(2,2)}\frac{x^2-y^2}{x-y}\).

Direct substitution gives \(\frac00\). But for \(x\ne y\) (the domain), \(\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x-y}=\dfrac{(x-y)(x+y)}{x-y}=x+y\). This is a polynomial, hence continuous, so

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(2,2)}\frac{x^2-y^2}{x-y}=\lim_{(x,y)\to(2,2)}(x+y)=4.$$
Worked B — a function of one combination

Q. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{\sin(x^2+3y^2)}{x^2+3y^2}\).

Let \(u=x^2+3y^2\). As \((x,y)\to(0,0)\), \(u\to0^+\) (and \(u\ne0\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\)). So the limit is \(\displaystyle\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{\sin u}{u}=1\).

This works because \(f\) depends on \((x,y)\) only through \(u\), and \(u\to0\) along every path.

Solution

STANDARD · 4 marks Q3. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{e^{2x^2+2y^2}-1}{x^2+y^2}\).

Solution

Polar / \(u=x^2+y^2=r^2\to0^+\): \(\displaystyle\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{e^{2u}-1}{u}\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{2e^{2u}}{1}=2\). (Or \(2\cdot\frac{e^{2u}-1}{2u}\to2\cdot1\).) The limit is 2.

STANDARD · 4 marks Q4. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{1-\cos\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}{x^2+y^2}\).

Solution

In polar form, \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=r\): \(\displaystyle\lim_{r\to0^+}\frac{1-\cos r}{r^2}\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\lim\frac{\sin r}{2r}=\frac12\). The limit is \(\tfrac12\), independent of \(\theta\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 5 marks Q5. Let \(g(x,y)=\dfrac{\ln(1+x^2+y^2)}{x^2+y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\) and \(g(0,0)=1\). Is \(g\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? Justify.

Solution

\(g\) is continuous at \((0,0)\) iff \(\lim g=g(0,0)=1\). With \(u=x^2+y^2\to0^+\): \(\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\lim\frac{1/(1+u)}{1}=1\). The limit exists and equals \(1=g(0,0)\), so \(g\) is continuous at \((0,0)\).

8.2 · Showing a limit does not exist (two-path test)

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — axes suffice

Q. Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2+3xy}{x^2+y^2}\) does not exist.

Along \(y=0\) (\(x\ne0\)): \(f(x,0)=\frac{x^2}{x^2}=1\to1\).

Along \(x=0\) (\(y\ne0\)): \(f(0,y)=\frac0{y^2}=0\to0\).

\(1\ne0\), so the limit does not exist. (More generally, \(y=mx\) gives \(\frac{1+3m}{1+m^2}\), which depends on \(m\).)

Worked B — lines fail, so balance the degrees

Q. (a) Find the limit of \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy^3}{x^2+y^6}\) along every line \(y=mx\). (b) Show \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f\) does not exist.

(a) \(f(x,mx)=\dfrac{m^3x^4}{x^2+m^6x^6}=\dfrac{m^3x^2}{1+m^6x^4}\to0\) (also for \(m=0\)). Along \(x=0\): \(f\equiv0\).

(b) Balance \(x^2\) with \(y^6\): take \(x=y^3\). Then \(f(y^3,y)=\dfrac{y^6}{y^6+y^6}=\dfrac12\to\dfrac12\). Along lines, 0. Along \(x=y^3\), \(\frac12\). So the limit does not exist.

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 4 marks Q1. Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{3x^2-2y^2}{x^2+4y^2}\) does not exist.

Solution

\(y=0\): \(\frac{3x^2}{x^2}=3\to3\). \(x=0\): \(\frac{-2y^2}{4y^2}=-\frac12\to-\frac12\). \(3\ne-\frac12\), so the limit DNE.

STANDARD · 5 marks Q2. Let \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{4x^2+y^2}\). (a) Find the limit of \(f\) along \(y=mx\) as \(x\to0\). [3] (b) Hence show \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f\) does not exist. [2]

(a) \(f(x,mx)=\dfrac{mx^2}{4x^2+m^2x^2}=\dfrac{m}{4+m^2}\) (constant), so the limit is \(\dfrac{m}{4+m^2}\).

(b) \(m=0\) gives 0. \(m=2\) gives \(\frac28=\frac14\). Different paths, different limits, so the limit DNE.

EXAM-LEVEL · 8 marks Q3. Let \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^4y^2}{x^8+y^4}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\). (a) Show \(f\to0\) along both axes and along every line \(y=mx\). [4] (b) Show that \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f\) does not exist. [4]

Solution

(a) \(f(x,0)=0\), \(f(0,y)=0\). For \(m\ne0\): \(f(x,mx)=\dfrac{m^2x^6}{x^8+m^4x^4}=\dfrac{m^2x^2}{x^4+m^4}\to\dfrac0{m^4}=0\).

(b) Balance \(x^8\) with \(y^4\): \(y=x^2\). Then \(f(x,x^2)=\dfrac{x^8}{x^8+x^8}=\dfrac12\to\dfrac12\ne0\). Two paths give different limits, so the limit DNE.

STRETCH · 8 marks Q4. Let \(g(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{x+y}\), defined on \(\{(x,y): x+y\ne0\}\). (a) Show \(g\to0\) along both axes and along every line \(y=mx\) with \(m\ne-1\). [3] (b) By approaching along the curve \(y=-x+x^2\) (which lies in the domain for \(x\ne0\)), show that \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g\) does not exist. [5]

Solution

(a) Axes: \(g=0\). Line \(y=mx\), \(m\ne-1\): \(g=\dfrac{mx^2}{(1+m)x}=\dfrac{m}{1+m}x\to0\).

(b) On \(y=-x+x^2\): \(x+y=x^2\ne0\), so the curve is in the domain, and \(xy=-x^2+x^3\). So

$$g=\frac{-x^2+x^3}{x^2}=x-1\to-1.$$

Along the axes the limit is 0, along this curve it is \(-1\), so the limit DNE. (Same idea as the official 2023 S2 Q8 solution: hug the curve where the denominator vanishes.)

8.3 · Proving a limit exists (polar coordinates + squeeze)

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — a bound independent of \(\theta\)

Q. Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{3xy^2}{x^2+y^2}=0\).

Let \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\). Then \((x,y)\to(0,0)\iff r\to0^+\), and

$$\frac{3xy^2}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{3r^3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta}{r^2}=3r\cos\theta\sin^2\theta.$$

So \(0\le|f|\le3r\) for every \(\theta\). Since \(3r\to0\), the squeeze theorem gives \(\lim f=0\).

Worked B — \(u\ln u\) via L'Hôpital

Q. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}(x^2+y^2)\ln(x^2+y^2)\).

With \(u=x^2+y^2\to0^+\): \(\displaystyle\lim_{u\to0^+}u\ln u=\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{\ln u}{1/u}\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{1/u}{-1/u^2}=\lim_{u\to0^+}(-u)=0.\)

The limit is 0. (It's a \(0\cdot(-\infty)\) form. Rewrite it as \(\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\) before using L'Hôpital.)

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 3 marks Q1. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^4-y^4}{x^2+y^2}\).

Solution

Factor: \(x^4-y^4=(x^2-y^2)(x^2+y^2)\), so \(f=x^2-y^2\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\). A polynomial, so the limit is \(0-0=\mathbf 0\).

STANDARD · 4 marks Q2. Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}xy\cos\!\Big(\frac1{x^2+y^2}\Big)=0\).

Solution

\(|\cos(\cdot)|\le1\), so \(0\le|f|\le|xy|=r^2|\cos\theta\sin\theta|\le r^2\). Since \(r^2\to0\), squeeze gives \(f\to0\). (The cosine oscillates wildly but is bounded, and it gets multiplied by something that goes to 0.)

STANDARD · 4 marks Q3. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^3-xy^2}{x^2+y^2}\).

Solution

Polar: \(\dfrac{r^3\cos\theta(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)}{r^2}=r\cos\theta\cos2\theta\), so \(|f|\le r\to0\). The limit is 0 by the squeeze theorem.

EXAM-LEVEL · 6 marks Q4. Let \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{\sqrt{1+x^2+y^2}-1}{x^2+y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\) and \(f(0,0)=c\). Find the value of \(c\) that makes \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\).

Solution

\(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\) iff \(c=\lim f\). With \(u=x^2+y^2\), rationalise:

$$\frac{\sqrt{1+u}-1}{u}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1+u}+1}{\sqrt{1+u}+1}=\frac{u}{u(\sqrt{1+u}+1)}=\frac1{\sqrt{1+u}+1}\ \xrightarrow[u\to0^+]{}\ \frac12.$$

So \(c=\tfrac12\).

STRETCH · 6 marks Q5. Let \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2y}{2x^4+y^2}\). (a) Show that in polar coordinates, \(\lim_{r\to0^+}f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)=0\) for every fixed \(\theta\). [3] (b) Explain why this does not prove that \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f=0\), and show the limit in fact does not exist. [3]

Solution

(a) \(f=\dfrac{r^3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta}{2r^4\cos^4\theta+r^2\sin^2\theta}=\dfrac{r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta}{2r^2\cos^4\theta+\sin^2\theta}\). If \(\sin\theta\ne0\): the limit is \(\frac{0}{\sin^2\theta}=0\). If \(\sin\theta=0\): \(f\equiv0\). So the limit is 0 for every fixed \(\theta\).

(b) A fixed \(\theta\) is a straight line, so (a) only checks lines. There's no bound \(|f|\le g(r)\) uniform in \(\theta\): the denominator gets small when \(\sin\theta\approx0\). Along \(y=x^2\): \(f=\frac{x^4}{2x^4+x^4}=\frac13\). Lines give 0 and this parabola gives \(\frac13\), so the limit DNE.

8.4 · Continuity & partial derivatives at a piecewise point

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — continuous, and both partials exist

Q. \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{2x^3+y^3}{x^2+y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(f(0,0)=0\). (a) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? (b) Find \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) if they exist.

(a) Polar: \(f=r(2\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta)\), so \(|f-0|\le3r\to0\). So \(\lim f=0=f(0,0)\), and \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\).

(b) \(f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{2h^3/h^2-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}2=2\).

\(f_y(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{h^3/h^2}{h}=1\). Both exist: \(f_x(0,0)=2\), \(f_y(0,0)=1\).

Worked B — the value at the origin changes everything

Q. \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(f(0,0)=1\). (a) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? (b) Do \(f_x(0,0)\), \(f_y(0,0)\) exist?

(a) Along \(y=0\): \(f=1\to1\). Along \(x=0\): \(f=-1\to-1\). The limit DNE, so \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\), whatever \(f(0,0)\) is.

(b) \(f_x(0,0)=\lim\dfrac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim\dfrac{1-1}{h}=\lim0=0\). It exists.

\(f_y(0,0)=\lim\dfrac{f(0,h)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim\dfrac{-1-1}{h}=\lim\dfrac{-2}{h}\), which is unbounded, so \(f_y(0,0)\) does not exist.

Had \(f(0,0)\) been \(-1\), it would be the other way round. The given value at the point is part of the difference quotient.

WARM-UP · 5 marks Q1. \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy^2}{x^2+y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(f(0,0)=0\). (a) Show \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\). [3] (b) Find \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\). [2]

Solution

(a) Polar: \(f=r\cos\theta\sin^2\theta\), \(|f|\le r\to0=f(0,0)\). Continuous.

(b) \(f(h,0)=0\) and \(f(0,h)=0\), so both quotients are \(\frac{0-0}{h}=0\): \(f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0\).

STANDARD · 8 marks Q2. \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^3-4y^3}{x^2+y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(f(0,0)=c\). (a) Find \(c\) so that \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\). [4] (b) With this \(c\), find \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) from the definition. [4]

Solution

(a) Polar: \(f=r(\cos^3\theta-4\sin^3\theta)\), \(|f|\le5r\to0\). The limit is 0, so \(c=0\).

(b) \(f_x(0,0)=\lim\frac{h^3/h^2-0}{h}=1\). \(f_y(0,0)=\lim\frac{-4h^3/h^2-0}{h}=-4\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 12 marks Q3. Let \(f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{y^4}{(y^2-x)^2+y^4}, &(x,y)\ne(0,0)\\ 0,&(x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}\) (a) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? Justify. [7] (b) Determine whether \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) exist. Justify. [5]

Solution

(a) Continuous iff \(\lim f=f(0,0)=0\). Along \(y=0\) (\(x\ne0\)): \(f=\frac0{x^2}=0\to0\). Along \(x=y^2\) (\(y\ne0\)): \(f=\frac{y^4}{0+y^4}=1\to1\). \(0\ne1\), so the limit DNE and \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).

(b) \(f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-0}{h}=\lim\frac{0}{h}=0\): exists. \(f_y(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0,h)-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{h^4}{h^4+h^4}=\lim\frac{1/2}{h}\): unbounded, so \(f_y(0,0)\) does not exist.

STRETCH · 12 marks Q4. Let \(f(x,y)=1\) if \(y=x^2\), and \(f(x,y)=0\) otherwise. (a) Determine \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f\), if it exists. [5] (b) Is \(f\) continuous at \((0,0)\)? [2] (c) Use the definition to determine whether \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) exist. [5]

Solution

(a) Along \(y=x^2\): \(f\equiv1\to1\). Along \(y=0\), \(x\ne0\) (not on the parabola, since \(0\ne x^2\)): \(f\equiv0\to0\). Different, so the limit DNE.

(b) Continuity needs \(\lim f=f(0,0)\). The limit doesn't exist, so \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).

(c) Note \(f(0,0)=1\) since \(0=0^2\). \(f_x(0,0)=\lim\frac{f(h,0)-1}{h}=\lim\frac{0-1}{h}=\lim\frac{-1}{h}\): DNE. \(f_y(0,0)=\lim\frac{f(0,h)-1}{h}\), and \((0,h)\) is on \(y=x^2\) only if \(h=0\), so again \(\frac{-1}{h}\): DNE. Neither partial exists. (Contrast 2025 S1 Q2, where \(f\) equals 1 on the whole \(x\)-axis, so \(f_x(0,0)=0\).)

8.5 · Partial derivatives by rules, higher order & Clairaut

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — first and mixed partials

Q. \(f(x,y)=x^2y^3-\dfrac{4x}{y}\). Find \(f_x,f_y\), evaluate them at \((1,2)\), and verify \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\).

\(f_x=2xy^3-\dfrac4y\Rightarrow f_x(1,2)=16-2=14\).

\(f_y=3x^2y^2+\dfrac{4x}{y^2}\Rightarrow f_y(1,2)=12+1=13\).

\(f_{xy}=\partial_y(2xy^3-4y^{-1})=6xy^2+\dfrac4{y^2}\). \(f_{yx}=\partial_x(3x^2y^2+4xy^{-2})=6xy^2+\dfrac4{y^2}\). Equal ✓.

Worked B — three variables

Q. \(f(x,y,z)=xe^{yz}+z\ln x\). Evaluate \(f_x,f_y,f_z\) at \((1,0,2)\).

\(f_x=e^{yz}+\dfrac zx\Rightarrow1+2=3\). \(f_y=xze^{yz}\Rightarrow1\cdot2\cdot1=2\). \(f_z=xye^{yz}+\ln x\Rightarrow0+0=0\).

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 3 marks Q1. \(f(x,y)=\sin(2x-3y)\). Find \(f_x(\tfrac\pi3,0)\) and \(f_y(\tfrac\pi3,0)\).

Solution

\(f_x=2\cos(2x-3y)\), \(f_y=-3\cos(2x-3y)\). At \((\frac\pi3,0)\): \(\cos\frac{2\pi}3=-\frac12\). So \(f_x=-1\), \(f_y=\frac32\).

STANDARD · 4 marks Q2. \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\). Find \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), and evaluate at \((1,1)\).

Solution

Quotient rule: \(f_x=\dfrac{(x^2+y^2)-x(2x)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}=\dfrac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\). \(f_y=\dfrac{-2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\). At \((1,1)\): \(f_x=0\), \(f_y=-\frac24=-\frac12\).

STANDARD · 5 marks Q3. Verify Clairaut's theorem for \(f(x,y)=x^4y^2-3xy^5+\cos(xy)\).

Solution

\(f_x=4x^3y^2-3y^5-y\sin(xy)\Rightarrow f_{xy}=8x^3y-15y^4-\sin(xy)-xy\cos(xy)\).

\(f_y=2x^4y-15xy^4-x\sin(xy)\Rightarrow f_{yx}=8x^3y-15y^4-\sin(xy)-xy\cos(xy)\). Equal ✓.

EXAM-LEVEL · 4 marks Q4. Show that \(u(x,t)=e^{-8t}\sin(2x)\) satisfies the heat equation \(u_t=2u_{xx}\).

Solution

\(u_t=-8e^{-8t}\sin2x\). \(u_x=2e^{-8t}\cos2x\), \(u_{xx}=-4e^{-8t}\sin2x\), so \(2u_{xx}=-8e^{-8t}\sin2x=u_t\) ✓.

8.6 · Tangent planes & linear approximation

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — plane + estimate

Q. \(f(x,y)=xe^{xy}\). (a) Find the tangent plane at \((2,0)\). (b) Estimate \(f(2.1,-0.05)\).

(a) \(f(2,0)=2\). \(f_x=e^{xy}+xye^{xy}\Rightarrow1\). \(f_y=x^2e^{xy}\Rightarrow4\). So \(z=2+(x-2)+4y\), i.e. \(z=x+4y\).

(b) \(f(2.1,-0.05)\approx2.1+4(-0.05)=2.1-0.2=1.9\). (True \(1.8906\ldots\).)

Worked B — three variables, invent the base point

Q. Use a linear approximation to estimate \((1.01)(1.98)^2(3.02)\).

Let \(f=xy^2z\), base point \((1,2,3)\), \(f=12\). \(f_x=y^2z=12\), \(f_y=2xyz=12\), \(f_z=xy^2=4\).

$$f\approx12+12(0.01)+12(-0.02)+4(0.02)=12+0.12-0.24+0.08=11.96.$$

(True \(11.958\ldots\).)

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 4 marks Q1. Find the tangent plane to \(z=x^2y-3xy^2\) at \((2,1)\).

Solution

\(f(2,1)=4-6=-2\). \(f_x=2xy-3y^2=1\). \(f_y=x^2-6xy=-8\). \(z=-2+(x-2)-8(y-1)\), i.e. \(z=x-8y+4\).

STANDARD · 8 marks Q2. Let \(f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2-4)\). (a) Find the tangent plane at \((2,1)\). [5] (b) Use it to estimate \(f(2.02,0.97)\). [3]

Solution

(a) \(f(2,1)=\ln1=0\). \(f_x=\frac{2x}{x^2+y^2-4}=4\). \(f_y=\frac{2y}{x^2+y^2-4}=2\). \(z=4(x-2)+2(y-1)\).

(b) \(\approx4(0.02)+2(-0.03)=0.08-0.06=0.02\). (True \(0.0211\).)

STANDARD · 6 marks Q3. Let \(f(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2-3x\). (a) Give a normal vector to the tangent plane of \(z=f(x,y)\) at \((1,1)\). [2] (b) Find the point on the surface where the tangent plane is horizontal, and write that plane. [4]

Solution

(a) \(f_x=2x+y-3\), \(f_y=x+2y\). At \((1,1)\): \(f_x=0\), \(f_y=3\), so \(\mathbf n=\langle0,3,-1\rangle\).

(b) Horizontal means \(f_x=f_y=0\): \(2x+y=3\), \(x+2y=0\), giving \(x=2\), \(y=-1\). \(f(2,-1)=4-2+1-6=-3\). Point \((2,-1,-3)\), plane \(z=-3\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 10 marks Q4. Show that the surfaces \(z_1=x^2+y^2\) and \(z_2=-x^2-y^2+8x+4y-10\) meet at \((x,y)=(2,1)\) and have a common tangent plane there. Find that plane.

Solution

Heights: \(z_1(2,1)=5\), \(z_2(2,1)=-4-1+16+4-10=5\). Same point \((2,1,5)\).

Slopes: \(z_{1,x}=2x=4\), \(z_{1,y}=2y=2\). \(z_{2,x}=-2x+8=4\), \(z_{2,y}=-2y+4=2\). Same \(x\)- and \(y\)-slopes, so the same tangent plane:

$$z=5+4(x-2)+2(y-1)=4x+2y-5.$$

(In fact \(z_1-(4x+2y-5)=(x-2)^2+(y-1)^2\ge0\) and \(z_2\) lies below by the same amount: an upward and a downward paraboloid kissing at one point.)

8.7 · Small changes & maximum error

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — signed change

Q. A cone has \(r=3\) cm, \(h=12\) cm, so \(V=\frac13\pi r^2h=36\pi\). Estimate the change in \(V\) if \(r\) increases by \(0.1\) cm and \(h\) decreases by \(0.2\) cm.

\(V_r=\frac23\pi rh=24\pi\), \(V_h=\frac13\pi r^2=3\pi\).

$$\Delta V\approx24\pi(0.1)+3\pi(-0.2)=2.4\pi-0.6\pi=1.8\pi\ \text{cm}^3\ (\text{an increase}).$$
Worked B — percentage error

Q. \(Q=x^2y^3\), where \(x\) is measured with at most 1% error and \(y\) with at most 2%. Estimate the maximum percentage error in \(Q\).

\(|E_Q|\approx|2xy^3|E_x+|3x^2y^2|E_y\). Divide by \(Q=x^2y^3\):

$$\frac{|E_Q|}{Q}\approx2\frac{E_x}{x}+3\frac{E_y}{y}=2(0.01)+3(0.02)=0.08=8\%.$$

✏️ Your turn

WARM-UP · 3 marks Q1. \(z=x^2y\). Estimate \(\Delta z\) when \((x,y)\) moves from \((3,2)\) to \((3.01,1.98)\).

Solution

\(z_x=2xy=12\), \(z_y=x^2=9\). \(\Delta z\approx12(0.01)+9(-0.02)=0.12-0.18=-0.06\).

STANDARD · 4 marks Q2. The sides of a rectangle are measured as 6 m and 8 m, each with error at most 0.05 m. Estimate the maximum error in the computed length of the diagonal.

Solution

\(D=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=10\). \(D_x=\frac xD=0.6\), \(D_y=\frac yD=0.8\). \(|E|\approx0.6(0.05)+0.8(0.05)=0.07\) m.

STANDARD · 5 marks Q3. The pressure of a gas is \(P=\dfrac{8T}{V}\). \(T=300\) and \(V=50\) are measured with errors at most \(1.5\) and \(0.25\) respectively. Estimate the maximum error in \(P\), and express it as a percentage of \(P\).

Solution

\(P=48\). \(P_T=\frac8V=0.16\), \(P_V=-\frac{8T}{V^2}=-0.96\). \(|E|\approx0.16(1.5)+|-0.96|(0.25)=0.24+0.24=0.48\), which is \(\frac{0.48}{48}=1\%\). (Check: relative errors \(\frac{1.5}{300}+\frac{0.25}{50}=0.5\%+0.5\%\).)

EXAM-LEVEL · 8 marks Q4. The area of a triangle is \(A=\tfrac12ab\sin C\). Measurements give \(a=4\), \(b=6\), \(C=\frac\pi6\), with maximum errors \(0.02\), \(0.03\) and \(0.01\) (radians). Estimate the maximum error in \(A\), leaving surds exact.

Solution

\(A=\frac12(24)(\frac12)=6\). \(A_a=\frac12b\sin C=\frac32\), \(A_b=\frac12a\sin C=1\), \(A_C=\frac12ab\cos C=12\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2=6\sqrt3\).

$$|E|\approx\tfrac32(0.02)+1(0.03)+6\sqrt3(0.01)=0.06+0.06\sqrt3\ (\approx0.164).$$

8.8 · Quadratic approximation

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — at the origin

Q. \(f(x,y)=e^x\cos y\). Find \(Q(x,y)\) at \((0,0)\) and estimate \(f(0.1,0.2)\) with \(P\) and \(Q\).

\(f=1\). \(f_x=e^x\cos y=1\). \(f_y=-e^x\sin y=0\). \(f_{xx}=1\). \(f_{xy}=-e^x\sin y=0\). \(f_{yy}=-e^x\cos y=-1\).

$$Q=1+x+\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac12y^2.$$

\(P(0.1,0.2)=1.1\). \(Q(0.1,0.2)=1+0.1+0.005-0.02=1.085\). (True \(1.08314\ldots\).)

Worked B — a cubic

Q. \(f(x,y)=x^3+xy^2-2y\). Find \(Q\) at \((1,1)\) and estimate \(f(1.1,0.9)\).

\(f(1,1)=0\). \(f_x=3x^2+y^2=4\). \(f_y=2xy-2=0\). \(f_{xx}=6x=6\). \(f_{xy}=2y=2\). \(f_{yy}=2x=2\).

$$Q=4(x-1)+3(x-1)^2+2(x-1)(y-1)+(y-1)^2.$$

At \((1.1,0.9)\): \(0.4+0.03-0.02+0.01=0.42\). (True \(0.422\). The error is only the cubic terms.)

✏️ Your turn

STANDARD · 6 marks Q1. \(f(x,y)=\sqrt{x+y}\). Find \(Q\) at \((3,1)\) and use it to estimate \(f(3.2,1.2)\).

Solution

\(f=2\). \(f_x=f_y=\frac1{2\sqrt{x+y}}=\frac14\). \(f_{xx}=f_{xy}=f_{yy}=-\frac1{4(x+y)^{3/2}}=-\frac1{32}\).

$$Q=2+\tfrac14(x-3)+\tfrac14(y-1)-\tfrac1{64}(x-3)^2-\tfrac1{32}(x-3)(y-1)-\tfrac1{64}(y-1)^2.$$

At \((3.2,1.2)\): \(2+0.05+0.05-\frac{0.04+0.08+0.04}{64}=2.1-0.0025=2.0975\). (True \(\sqrt{4.4}=2.09762\).)

STANDARD · 6 marks Q2. \(f(x,y)=xe^y+y^2\). Find \(P\) and \(Q\) at \((1,0)\), and estimate \(f(0.9,0.1)\) with each.

Solution

\(f=1\). \(f_x=e^y=1\). \(f_y=xe^y+2y=1\). \(f_{xx}=0\). \(f_{xy}=e^y=1\). \(f_{yy}=xe^y+2=3\).

\(P=1+(x-1)+y\). \(Q=P+(x-1)y+\tfrac32y^2\).

At \((0.9,0.1)\): \(P=1-0.1+0.1=1\). \(Q=1+(-0.1)(0.1)+1.5(0.01)=1.005\). (True \(1.00465\).)

EXAM-LEVEL · 10 marks Q3. Let \(f(x,y)=x^2\ln y+y\). (a) Find the quadratic approximation at \((2,1)\). [6] (b) Use it to approximate \(f(2.1,1.1)\), and compare with the linear approximation. [4]

Solution

(a) \(f(2,1)=0+1=1\). \(f_x=2x\ln y=0\). \(f_y=\frac{x^2}{y}+1=5\). \(f_{xx}=2\ln y=0\). \(f_{xy}=\frac{2x}{y}=4\). \(f_{yy}=-\frac{x^2}{y^2}=-4\).

$$Q=1+5(y-1)+4(x-2)(y-1)-2(y-1)^2.$$

(b) \(x-2=y-1=0.1\): \(Q=1+0.5+0.04-0.02=1.52\). Linear: \(P=1+0.5=1.5\). (True \(1.52032\ldots\), so \(Q\) is far better.)

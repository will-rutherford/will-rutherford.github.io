S1 Question 1 — Common tangent plane [10 marks]

10 marks Show that the surfaces $$z_1=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\qquad z_2=\frac1{10}\left(x^2+y^2\right)+\frac52$$ intersect at \((x,y)=(3,4)\), and have a common tangent plane at that point.
Worked solution
Two graphs \(z=f(x,y)\) have the same tangent plane at \((a,b)\) iff they have the same height, the same \(x\)-slope and the same \(y\)-slope there — because the tangent plane \(z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)\) is built from exactly those three numbers. (Ch 4)
1. Same height (they intersect): $$z_1(3,4)=\sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5,\qquad z_2(3,4)=\frac{25}{10}+\frac52=5.$$ Both surfaces pass through \((3,4,5)\), so they intersect there.
2. Same \(x\)-slope: $$\frac{\partial z_1}{\partial x}=\frac12(x^2+y^2)^{-1/2}(2x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac35,\qquad \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial x}=\frac{2x}{10}=\frac x5\;\Rightarrow\;\frac35.$$ 3. Same \(y\)-slope: $$\frac{\partial z_1}{\partial y}=\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac45,\qquad \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial y}=\frac y5\;\Rightarrow\;\frac45.$$ 4. Write the plane (the same for both): $$z=5+\tfrac35(x-3)+\tfrac45(y-4)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z=\frac{3x+4y}{5}.$$
Both surfaces pass through \((3,4,5)\) with \(f_x=\tfrac35,\ f_y=\tfrac45\), so they share the tangent plane \(z=\dfrac{3x+4y}{5}\) (equivalently \(3x+4y-5z=0\)).
The official solution earns the marks with three sentences: "same height", "same \(x\) slope", "same \(y\) slope ⇒ they share a tangent plane". Say each in words — a "show that" question is marked on the explicit comparison, not just the numbers.
Checking only the partial derivatives is not enough — if the heights differed you'd get two parallel planes, not a common one. Do the height check first.

S1 Question 2 — Limit that does not exist [10 marks]

Let \(g:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be given by $$g(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2} & (x,y)\ne(0,0)\\[4pt] c & (x,y)=(0,0)\end{cases}$$
8 marks(a) Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} g(x,y)\) does not exist.
Worked solution
To show a 2-D limit does not exist, find two paths into \((0,0)\) along which \(g\) tends to different values (two-path test). The powers \(x^4\) and \(y^2\) are balanced when \(y\sim x^2\), so the "interesting" path is the parabola \(y=x^2\). (Ch 4)
Path 1 — the \(x\)-axis, \(y=0\) (\(x\ne0\)): $$g(x,0)=\frac{x^2\cdot0}{x^4+0}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\lim_{x\to0}g(x,0)=0.$$ Path 2 — the parabola \(y=x^2\) (\(x\ne0\)): $$g(x,x^2)=\frac{x^2\cdot x^2}{x^4+x^4}=\frac{x^4}{2x^4}=\frac12\;\Rightarrow\;\lim_{x\to0}g(x,x^2)=\frac12.$$ Since \(0\ne\tfrac12\), \(g\) approaches different values along different paths to \((0,0)\).
The limit does not exist (\(0\) along \(y=0\), \(\tfrac12\) along \(y=x^2\)).
Every straight line \(y=mx\) gives \(g(x,mx)=\dfrac{mx^3}{x^4+m^2x^2}=\dfrac{mx}{x^2+m^2}\to0\). Students who test only lines (or only the axes) wrongly conclude the limit is 0. When the denominator mixes \(x^4\) with \(y^2\), try \(y=kx^2\): \(g=\dfrac{k}{1+k^2}\), which depends on \(k\).
For 8 marks write both substitutions in full, state "\(x\ne0\)" on each path, and finish with an explicit sentence: "the limits along two paths differ, so the limit does not exist."
2 marks(b) Is there any value of \(c\) which would make \(g(x,y)\) continuous? Justify your answer.
Worked solution
Continuity at \((0,0)\) needs \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g=g(0,0)\). If the limit doesn't exist, no choice of \(g(0,0)=c\) can fix it.
No. Continuity at the origin requires \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g(x,y)\) to exist and equal \(c\); by (a) the limit does not exist, so no value of \(c\) makes \(g\) continuous.
(Away from the origin \(g\) is a quotient of polynomials with non-zero denominator, so it is continuous there — the only problem point is \((0,0)\).)

S1 Question 3 — Implicit tangent line [10 marks]

10 marks Let \(C\) be the curve defined by equation $$e^{xy}+ye^{y}=2e,$$ for \(0\le x\le 2\). Find an equation of the tangent line to the curve \(C\) at \(y=1\).
Worked solution
Curve given implicitly by \(F(x,y)=0\) → implicit differentiation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{F_x}{F_y}\) (on today's formula sheet). First find the missing coordinate of the point. (Ch 5)
1. Find \(x\) when \(y=1\): \(e^{x}+e=2e\Rightarrow e^x=e\Rightarrow x=1\). Point \((1,1)\) (inside \(0\le x\le2\)).
2. Partial derivatives of \(F(x,y)=e^{xy}+ye^y-2e\): $$F_x=ye^{xy},\qquad F_y=xe^{xy}+e^y+ye^y.$$ 3. Slope at \((1,1)\): $$\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}=-\frac{1\cdot e}{e+e+e}=-\frac13.$$ 4. Tangent line: $$y-1=-\tfrac13(x-1)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y=\frac{4-x}{3}.$$
Tangent line at \((1,1)\): \(y=-\tfrac13(x-1)+1=\dfrac{4-x}{3}\).
\(F_y\) needs the product rule on \(ye^y\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(ye^y)=e^y+ye^y\). Dropping the \(e^y\) term gives slope \(-\tfrac12\) — a common lost mark.

S1 Question 4 — Elliptical contours [10 marks]

Consider the surface $$z=\frac{8y}{x^2+2y^2}.$$
6 marks(a) Show that all contours \(z=z_0\) (with \(z_0\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\)) are ellipses.
Worked solution
Contour = set \(z=z_0\). Clear the fraction, gather terms, complete the square and divide to reach standard form \(\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{A^2}+\dfrac{(y-k)^2}{B^2}=1\). (Ch 3)
Set \(z=z_0\ne0\) and rearrange: $$x^2+2y^2=\frac{8y}{z_0}\;\Rightarrow\;x^2+2\Big(y^2-\frac{4}{z_0}y\Big)=0.$$ Complete the square in \(y\): \(y^2-\dfrac4{z_0}y=\Big(y-\dfrac2{z_0}\Big)^2-\dfrac4{z_0^2}\), so $$x^2+2\Big(y-\frac2{z_0}\Big)^2=\frac8{z_0^2}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{x^2}{8/z_0^2}+\frac{\big(y-\frac2{z_0}\big)^2}{4/z_0^2}=1.$$ Both denominators are positive for every \(z_0\ne0\), so this is an ellipse in standard form, centre \(\big(0,\tfrac2{z_0}\big)\), semi-axes \(\dfrac{2\sqrt2}{|z_0|}\) (in \(x\)) and \(\dfrac{2}{|z_0|}\) (in \(y\)).
Every contour \(z=z_0\ne0\) is the ellipse \(\dfrac{x^2}{8/z_0^2}+\dfrac{(y-2/z_0)^2}{4/z_0^2}=1\), centred at \((0,2/z_0)\).
Each of these ellipses passes through the origin (put \(x=y=0\): \(0+2\cdot\frac4{z_0^2}=\frac8{z_0^2}\) ✓), but the surface is undefined at \((0,0)\). Strictly, each contour is the ellipse with the origin removed — mark it with an open circle on a sketch. (The official solution doesn't mention this; it is a free "precision" point.)
Say where the sign of \(z_0\) goes: \(z_0>0\) ellipses sit above the \(x\)-axis, \(z_0<0\) below; larger \(|z_0|\) → smaller ellipse closer to the origin.
4 marks(b) Sketch the contour for \(z_0=2\).
Worked solution
Substitute \(z_0=2\) into the standard form, then read off centre, semi-axes and intercepts — these are what a marker ticks on a sketch.
$$\frac{x^2}{2}+(y-1)^2=1.$$ Centre \((0,1)\); semi-axis \(\sqrt2\) horizontally, \(1\) vertically. Key points: \((0,0)\) (open — excluded), \((0,2)\), \((\pm\sqrt2,1)\). The ellipse touches the \(x\)-axis only at the origin.
Ellipse \(\frac{x^2}{2}+(y-1)^2=1\): centre \((0,1)\), \(y\)-intercepts \(0\) and \(2\), widest points \((\pm\sqrt2,1)\); origin hollow.
Label the centre, both axis lengths and every intercept. An unlabelled oval scores little even if it's the right shape.

S1 Question 5 — Temperature on a plate [10 marks]

The temperature (in degrees Celcius) at a point \((x,y)\) on a metal plate in the \(xy\)-plane is $$T(x,y)=\frac{xy}{1+x^2+y^2}.$$
6 marks(a) Find the rate of change of temperature at \((1,1)\) in the direction of \(2\mathbf i-\mathbf j\).
Worked solution
"Rate of change in the direction of \(\mathbf a\)" = directional derivative \(D_{\mathbf u}T=\nabla T\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf a}{\|\mathbf a\|}\). Normalise first. (Ch 5)
Gradient (quotient rule): $$T_x=\frac{y(1+x^2+y^2)-xy(2x)}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}=\frac{y(1-x^2+y^2)}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2},\qquad T_y=\frac{x(1+x^2-y^2)}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}.$$ At \((1,1)\): \(T_x=\dfrac{3-2}{9}=\dfrac19,\ T_y=\dfrac19\), so \(\nabla T(1,1)=\tfrac19(1,1)\).
Unit direction: \(\mathbf u=\dfrac{(2,-1)}{\sqrt5}\). $$D_{\mathbf u}T(1,1)=\frac19(1,1)\cdot\frac{(2,-1)}{\sqrt5}=\frac{2-1}{9\sqrt5}=\frac1{9\sqrt5}.$$
\(D_{\mathbf u}T(1,1)=\dfrac1{9\sqrt5}=\dfrac{\sqrt5}{45}\ ^\circ\mathrm C\) per unit distance.
Using \((2,-1)\) without dividing by \(\sqrt5\) gives \(\tfrac19\), which is \(\sqrt5\) times too big. Always normalise the direction vector.
4 marks(b) An ant at \((1,1)\) walks in the direction of greatest temperature decrease. Find a vector in that direction.
Worked solution
Temperature increases fastest along \(\nabla T\), so it decreases fastest along \(-\nabla T\).
$$-\nabla T(1,1)=-\tfrac19(1,1)=\left(-\tfrac19,-\tfrac19\right).$$
Direction \(-\nabla T(1,1)=-\tfrac19(\mathbf i+\mathbf j)\), i.e. any positive multiple such as \(-\mathbf i-\mathbf j\) (south-west). The rate of decrease there is \(\|\nabla T\|=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{9}\).
The question asks for "a vector", so any positive multiple of \(-\nabla T\) scores; the official answer adds "This is South West" — a nice sanity sentence.

S1 Question 6 — Chain rule, exponential-polar coordinates [10 marks]

Let \(f(x,y)\) have continuous partial derivatives, and let \(x=e^u\cos\theta\) and \(y=e^u\sin\theta\).
5 marks(a) Express \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\) in terms of \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\).
Worked solution
\(f\) depends on \((u,\theta)\) only through \(x\) and \(y\) → tree diagram: \(f\to x,y\to u,\theta\). Each partial = sum over the two branches. (Ch 5)
Derivatives of the substitution: \(x_u=e^u\cos\theta,\ y_u=e^u\sin\theta,\ x_\theta=-e^u\sin\theta,\ y_\theta=e^u\cos\theta\). $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}=e^u\left(\cos\theta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right),$$ $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}=e^u\left(-\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\cos\theta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).$$
\(f_u=e^u(\cos\theta\,f_x+\sin\theta\,f_y),\qquad f_\theta=e^u(-\sin\theta\,f_x+\cos\theta\,f_y).\)
Draw the tree diagram in your answer — markers give method credit for it even if an algebra slip follows.
5 marks(b) Use your result from part (a) to show that $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=e^{-u}\left(\cos\theta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}-\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\right).$$
Worked solution
"Show that" → start from the right-hand side, substitute (a), expand, and simplify with \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) until only \(f_x\) remains.
$$\begin{aligned} e^{-u}\big(\cos\theta\,f_u-\sin\theta\,f_\theta\big) &=e^{-u}\Big(\cos\theta\,e^u(\cos\theta\,f_x+\sin\theta\,f_y)-\sin\theta\,e^u(-\sin\theta\,f_x+\cos\theta\,f_y)\Big)\\ &=\cos^2\theta\,f_x+\cos\theta\sin\theta\,f_y+\sin^2\theta\,f_x-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,f_y\\ &=(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)\,f_x=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}. \end{aligned}$$
RHS \(=f_x\), as required. ∎
Don't start from \(f_x\) and "work towards" the RHS by guessing — you can't invert the chain rule directly. Work RHS → LHS and show every expansion line; the \(f_y\) cross-terms cancelling is the step the marker is looking for.

S1 Question 7 — Where to park (Lagrange) [10 marks]

10 marks You drive along a circular road given by the equation $$x^2+y^2=45.$$ There is a tourist attraction at the point \((-1,-2)\). You plan to leave your car and walk to the attraction. Where should you park to minimise the distance you have to walk? (Your car cannot drive off the road.)
(Hint: The distance between two points \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\) is given by the equation \(D=\sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2}\).)
Worked solution
Optimise a function subject to an equation constraint ("cannot drive off the road") → Lagrange multipliers, \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\). Minimise \(D^2\) instead of \(D\) — same minimiser, no square roots. (Ch 6)
Minimise \(f(x,y)=(x+1)^2+(y+2)^2\) subject to \(g(x,y)=x^2+y^2=45\). $$\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g:\quad (2(x+1),\,2(y+2))=\lambda(2x,\,2y).$$
(1) x + 1 = λx (2) y + 2 = λy (3) x² + y² = 45
From (1), \(x\ne0\) (else \(1=0\)), so \(\lambda=\dfrac{x+1}{x}\). Substitute into (2): $$y+2=\frac{x+1}{x}\,y=y+\frac yx\;\Rightarrow\;2=\frac yx\;\Rightarrow\;y=2x.$$ Into (3): \(x^2+4x^2=45\Rightarrow x^2=9\Rightarrow x=\pm3\), giving candidates \((3,6)\) and \((-3,-6)\).
Compare: $$D(3,6)=\sqrt{4^2+8^2}=\sqrt{80}=4\sqrt5,\qquad D(-3,-6)=\sqrt{(-2)^2+(-4)^2}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5.$$ The circle is closed and bounded, so the continuous \(D\) attains a minimum on it, and it must be at one of the Lagrange candidates.
Park at \((-3,-6)\); the walk is \(\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5\). (The point \((3,6)\) is the farthest point, \(\sqrt{80}\).)
Geometric check: the nearest point on a circle centred at the origin lies on the ray from the origin through the attraction: \(\sqrt{45}\cdot\frac{(-1,-2)}{\sqrt5}=3(-1,-2)=(-3,-6)\) ✓.
Writing "\((\pm3,\pm6)\)" (as the official solution does) suggests four points; only the same-sign pairs satisfy \(y=2x\). Always list candidates explicitly and evaluate \(f\) at each.

S1 Question 8 — Critical points [10 marks]

Let \(f(x,y)=4xy-\tfrac13(x+y)^3\).
5 marks(a) Find all the critical points of \(f\).
Worked solution
Critical points: solve \(f_x=0\) and \(f_y=0\) simultaneously. Subtracting the equations is the fast route when they share a term. (Ch 6)
$$f_x=4y-(x+y)^2,\qquad f_y=4x-(x+y)^2.$$ Setting both to zero: \(4y=(x+y)^2=4x\Rightarrow y=x\). Then $$0=4x-(2x)^2=4x(1-x)\;\Rightarrow\;x=0\ \text{or}\ x=1.$$
Critical points: \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\).
Chain rule on \(\tfrac13(x+y)^3\): its \(x\)-derivative is \((x+y)^2\cdot1\), not \(\tfrac13\cdot3(x+y)^2\cdot 2\). Keep the inner derivative (=1) honest.
5 marks(b) Classify one of these critical points.
Worked solution
Second-derivative test: \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\). \(D\lt0\) saddle; \(D\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}\lt0\) local max; \(D\gt0,\ f_{xx}\gt0\) local min; \(D=0\) inconclusive.
$$f_{xx}=-2(x+y),\qquad f_{yy}=-2(x+y),\qquad f_{xy}=4-2(x+y).$$ $$D(x,y)=4(x+y)^2-\big(4-2(x+y)\big)^2.$$
Point\(f_{xx}\)\(f_{xy}\)\(D\)Type
\((0,0)\)04\(0-16=-16\lt0\)saddle point
\((1,1)\)\(-4\)0\(16-0=16\gt0\)local maximum, \(f(1,1)=\tfrac43\)
Either: \((0,0)\) is a saddle (\(D=-16\lt0\)), or \((1,1)\) is a local maximum (\(D=16\gt0,\ f_{xx}=-4\lt0\)).
The question only asks for one; the official solution does both. Classify both anyway if time allows — it protects you if one has an arithmetic slip. (The official file also carries a stray "Haven't finished this" note — ignore it.)

S1 Question 9 — Arc length of a polar curve [10 marks]

Consider a polar curve parameterised by $$\mathbf r(t)=f(t)\cos(t)\,\mathbf i+f(t)\sin(t)\,\mathbf j,\qquad a\le t\le b.$$
5 marks(a) Show that the arclength of such a curve is \(\displaystyle l=\int_a^b\sqrt{(f(t))^2+(f'(t))^2}\,dt.\)
Worked solution
Arc length \(=\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\) (on today's sheet). Differentiate with the product rule, square, and let \(\cos^2+\sin^2=1\) do the work. (Ch 7)
$$\mathbf r'(t)=\big(f'\cos t-f\sin t\big)\mathbf i+\big(f'\sin t+f\cos t\big)\mathbf j.$$ $$\begin{aligned}\|\mathbf r'\|^2&=f'^2\cos^2t-2ff'\sin t\cos t+f^2\sin^2t+f'^2\sin^2t+2ff'\sin t\cos t+f^2\cos^2t\\&=f'^2(\cos^2t+\sin^2t)+f^2(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)=f^2+f'^2.\end{aligned}$$ Hence \(l=\displaystyle\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt=\int_a^b\sqrt{f(t)^2+f'(t)^2}\,dt\). ∎
Show the cross terms \(\mp2ff'\sin t\cos t\) explicitly and say they cancel — that's the "show" in "show that".
5 marks(b) Find the arclength for \(f(t)=3(1-\cos t)\), \(a=0\), \(b=2\pi\). (Hint, you may find the formula \(\cos(2x)=1-2\sin^2x\) useful.)
Worked solution
Plug into (a); use the half-angle identity \(1-\cos t=2\sin^2\frac t2\) (the hint with \(x=t/2\)) to turn the square root into something integrable.
\(f=3(1-\cos t),\ f'=3\sin t\): $$f^2+f'^2=9(1-2\cos t+\cos^2t)+9\sin^2t=9(2-2\cos t)=18(1-\cos t)=36\sin^2\tfrac t2.$$ So \(\sqrt{f^2+f'^2}=6\left|\sin\tfrac t2\right|=6\sin\tfrac t2\) because \(\sin\tfrac t2\ge0\) for \(0\le t\le2\pi\). $$l=\int_0^{2\pi}6\sin\tfrac t2\,dt=6\Big[-2\cos\tfrac t2\Big]_0^{2\pi}=-12(\cos\pi-\cos0)=-12(-2)=24.$$
\(l=24\) (the cardioid \(r=3(1-\cos t)\) has length \(8\times3\)).
\(\sqrt{\sin^2(t/2)}=|\sin(t/2)|\). It happens to be non-negative on \([0,2\pi]\) — say so. On a range like \([0,4\pi]\) ignoring the absolute value would give 0, an obviously impossible length.

S1 Question 10 — Conservative field & line integral [10 marks]

5 marks(a) Show that the field \(\mathbf F(x,y)=(\cos(x)+y^2)\,\mathbf i+(2xy+1)\,\mathbf j\) is conservative.
Worked solution
2-D field \(\mathbf F=P\mathbf i+Q\mathbf j\) on all of \(\mathbb R^2\): conservative iff \(P_y=Q_x\). (Ch 7)
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big(\cos x+y^2\big)=2y,\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(2xy+1\big)=2y.$$
\(P_y=Q_x=2y\) everywhere on \(\mathbb R^2\), so \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
Official comment: equality of the cross-partials implies conservative "from lectures"; the simply-connected-domain subtlety is deferred to MATH2001. Mentioning "\(\mathbf F\) is defined and smooth on all of \(\mathbb R^2\)" is a tidy justification.
5 marks(b) For the field \(\mathbf F\) in part (a) evaluate the line integral \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\), where \(C\) is the path with parametric equation \(\mathbf r(t)=t\,\mathbf i+t^2\,\mathbf j,\ 0\le t\le1\).
Worked solution
Conservative field → find a potential \(\phi\) with \(\nabla\phi=\mathbf F\), then \(\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\phi(\text{end})-\phi(\text{start})\). (Parametrising also works — shown as a check.)
Potential: \(\phi=\int(\cos x+y^2)\,dx=\sin x+xy^2+g(y)\). Then \(\phi_y=2xy+g'(y)=2xy+1\Rightarrow g'(y)=1\Rightarrow g=y\). $$\phi(x,y)=\sin x+xy^2+y.$$ Endpoints: \(t=0\to(0,0)\), \(t=1\to(1,1)\). $$\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\phi(1,1)-\phi(0,0)=(\sin1+1+1)-0=2+\sin1.$$ Check by parametrisation: \(\mathbf r'=(1,2t)\), \(\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(\cos t+t^4,\ 2t^3+1)\), $$\int_0^1\big(\cos t+t^4+4t^4+2t\big)dt=\Big[\sin t+t^5+t^2\Big]_0^1=2+\sin1.$$ ✓ Both methods agree.
\(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=2+\sin 1.\)
Leave \(\sin1\) exact (1 radian) — no calculator, and "\(\sin 1^\circ\)" is wrong.

S2 Question 1 — Hyperbolic contour [5 marks]

5 marks Consider the surface \(z=(x-3)(y+1)\). Sketch the contour \(z=-2\). Indicate all \(x\)-intercepts and \(y\)-intercepts.
Worked solution
\(z=(x-h)(y-k)\) is a saddle; its contours \((x-h)(y-k)=c\) are rectangular hyperbolas with asymptotes \(x=h,\ y=k\). Solve for \(y\), find asymptotes and intercepts, decide which quadrants the branches occupy. (Ch 3)
$$(x-3)(y+1)=-2\;\Longleftrightarrow\;y=-\frac{2}{x-3}-1.$$ Asymptotes: \(x=3\) (vertical), \(y=-1\) (horizontal); centre \((3,-1)\).
\(x\)-intercept (\(y=0\)): \((x-3)(1)=-2\Rightarrow x=1\).
\(y\)-intercept (\(x=0\)): \((-3)(y+1)=-2\Rightarrow y+1=\tfrac23\Rightarrow y=-\tfrac13\).
Branches: the product \((x-3)(y+1)\) is negative, so the branches lie where \(x-3\) and \(y+1\) have opposite signs: upper-left (\(x\lt3,\ y\gt-1\), e.g. \((2,1)\)) and lower-right (\(x\gt3,\ y\lt-1\), e.g. \((4,-3)\)).
Hyperbola \(y=-\dfrac2{x-3}-1\), asymptotes \(x=3,\ y=-1\); \(x\)-intercept \((1,0)\), \(y\)-intercept \(\big(0,-\tfrac13\big)\); branches in the upper-left and lower-right of the asymptote cross.
The official handwritten solution labels the centre "(3, 1)" — it is \((3,-1)\) (the sketch itself correctly draws \(y=-1\)). Sign slips on the asymptote are the classic lost mark here.
Draw the asymptotes dashed and label them, mark both intercepts with their values. The lower-right branch has no intercepts — say so.

S2 Question 2 — Tangent plane perpendicular to a line [5 marks]

5 marks Find \(a\) and \(b\) such that the plane tangent to the surface \(x^4-y+z=0\) at the point \(P=(a,b,2)\) is perpendicular to the line \(\dfrac{x-3}{32}=-(y-1)=z+4\).
Worked solution
A plane is perpendicular to a line iff the plane's normal is parallel to the line's direction. Normal to \(F(x,y,z)=0\) is \(\nabla F\); direction of a symmetric-form line is read off the denominators. (Ch 4)
Normal: \(F=x^4-y+z\Rightarrow\nabla F=(4x^3,-1,1)\); at \(P\): \(\mathbf N=(4a^3,-1,1)\).
Direction of the line: write it as \(\dfrac{x-3}{32}=\dfrac{y-1}{-1}=\dfrac{z+4}{1}\), so \(\mathbf d=(32,-1,1)\).
Parallel: \((4a^3,-1,1)=\lambda(32,-1,1)\). The \(y\)- and \(z\)-components give \(\lambda=1\); then \(4a^3=32\Rightarrow a^3=8\Rightarrow a=2\).
\(P\) lies on the surface: \(a^4-b+2=0\Rightarrow16-b+2=0\Rightarrow b=18\).
\(a=2,\ b=18\); \(P=(2,18,2)\).
Rewriting \(-(y-1)\) as \(\dfrac{y-1}{-1}\) is essential — reading the \(y\)-direction as \(+1\) makes the system inconsistent. Also: perpendicular plane ⇒ normal parallel to the line (not normal·direction = 0, which is the condition for the line to lie along the plane).
The official solution goes via the graph \(z=y-x^4\) and \(z-f(a,b)=f_x(x-a)+f_y(y-b)\), reaching the same normal \((4a^3,-1,1)\). Either route is full marks.

S2 Question 3 — Continuity via squeeze [10 marks]

10 marks Let \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be given by $$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}y\sin\left(\dfrac1{x^2+y^2}\right), & (x,y)\ne(0,0)\\[4pt] c, & (x,y)=(0,0)\end{cases}$$ where \(c\in\mathbb R\). Find \(c\) such that \(f(x,y)\) is continuous at \((0,0)\).
Worked solution
Bounded oscillating factor × something going to 0 → squeeze theorem. Show the limit exists, then set \(c\) equal to it. (Ch 4)
Since \(\left|\sin\left(\frac1{x^2+y^2}\right)\right|\le1\) for all \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), $$0\le|f(x,y)|=|y|\left|\sin\tfrac{1}{x^2+y^2}\right|\le|y|.$$ As \((x,y)\to(0,0)\), \(|y|\to0\), so by the squeeze theorem \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)=0\).
Polar version (as in the official solution): with \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\), \(f=r\sin\theta\,\sin(1/r^2)\) and \(-r\le f\le r\) for all \(\theta\); both bounds \(\to0\) as \(r\to0^+\).
Continuity requires \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f=f(0,0)=c\).
\(c=0\). Then \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)=0=f(0,0)\), so \(f\) is continuous at \((0,0)\).
"\(\sin(1/(x^2+y^2))\) has no limit, so the limit doesn't exist" is wrong — the factor \(y\to0\) crushes the oscillation. And checking a few paths never proves a limit exists; you need the squeeze bound valid for all directions.
For full marks finish with the continuity sentence: "\(\lim f = f(0,0)\), hence continuous." The limit alone is only part of the answer.

S2 Question 4 — Helix of given length [10 marks]

10 marks Consider the helix $$x=\cos3t,\quad y=\sin3t,\quad z=1+4t;\qquad 0\le t\le a,$$ where \(a\in\mathbb R\). Find \(a\) such that the helix has length 20.
Worked solution
Arc length \(=\int_0^a\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\). A helix has constant speed, so length = speed × time. (Ch 7)
$$\mathbf r'(t)=(-3\sin3t,\ 3\cos3t,\ 4),\qquad \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt{9\sin^23t+9\cos^23t+16}=\sqrt{25}=5.$$ $$\text{length}=\int_0^a5\,dt=5a=20\;\Rightarrow\;a=4.$$
\(a=4\).
Chain rule: \(\frac{d}{dt}\cos3t=-3\sin3t\). Forgetting the 3 gives speed \(\sqrt{17}\) and an ugly \(a\) — in a no-calculator exam an ugly answer is a warning sign.

S2 Question 5 — Critical points on a punctured domain [15 marks]

Let \(f:\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(x,y):x=0\text{ or }y=0\}\to\mathbb R\) be given by \(f(x,y)=4x+y+\dfrac{2}{xy}\).
(a)(a) Find all critical points of \(f\).
Worked solution
Set \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\). Clear denominators, then divide/subtract the two equations to get a relation between \(x\) and \(y\); use \(x,y\ne0\) (domain) to cancel. (Ch 6)
$$f_x=4-\frac{2}{x^2y}=0\;\Rightarrow\;4x^2y=2\quad(1),\qquad f_y=1-\frac{2}{xy^2}=0\;\Rightarrow\;xy^2=2\quad(2).$$ From (1) and (2): \(4x^2y=xy^2\Rightarrow xy(4x-y)=0\). Since \(x\ne0,\ y\ne0\) on the domain, \(y=4x\).
Into (1): \(4x^2(4x)=2\Rightarrow16x^3=2\Rightarrow x^3=\tfrac18\Rightarrow x=\tfrac12\), so \(y=2\).
The only critical point is \(\left(\tfrac12,\,2\right)\).
State why you may divide by \(xy\): "\(x\ne0\) and \(y\ne0\) on the domain." That sentence is exactly what the official solution writes and what markers look for.
(b)(b) Classify all critical points of \(f\). [(a)+(b) = 15 marks]
Worked solution
Second-derivative (Hessian) test at each critical point.
$$f_{xx}=\frac{4}{x^3y},\qquad f_{yy}=\frac{4}{xy^3},\qquad f_{xy}=\frac{2}{x^2y^2}.$$ $$D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=\frac{16}{x^4y^4}-\frac{4}{x^4y^4}=\frac{12}{x^4y^4}.$$ At \(\left(\tfrac12,2\right)\): \(x^4y^4=\tfrac1{16}\cdot16=1\), so \(D=12\gt0\); \(f_{xx}=\dfrac{4}{\frac18\cdot2}=16\gt0\).
\(\left(\tfrac12,2\right)\) is a local minimum, with \(f\left(\tfrac12,2\right)=2+2+2=6\).
The official working has a crossed-out first attempt at \(D\) (\(\tfrac{8}{x^5y^3}-\ldots\)) — computing \(D\) symbolically first and simplifying before substituting avoids exactly that mess.
It is a local, not global, minimum: e.g. \(f(-1,-1)=-5+2=-3\lt6\). Don't upgrade "local" without justification.

S2 Question 6 — Lagrange on a sphere [10 marks]

10 marks Use a Lagrange multiplier to find the points on the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\dfrac{27}{2}\) where \(x+2y+z\) attains its maximum value.
Worked solution
Explicit instruction "use a Lagrange multiplier": \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) plus the constraint. The sphere is closed and bounded, so a max and min exist and occur among the candidates. (Ch 6)
\(f=x+2y+z,\ g=x^2+y^2+z^2-\tfrac{27}2\). \(\nabla f=(1,2,1),\ \nabla g=(2x,2y,2z)\):
1 = 2xλ 2 = 2yλ 1 = 2zλ
\(\lambda\ne0\) (since \(2x\lambda=1\)), so \(x=\dfrac1{2\lambda},\ y=\dfrac1\lambda,\ z=\dfrac1{2\lambda}\). Into the constraint: $$\frac1{\lambda^2}\left(\frac14+1+\frac14\right)=\frac{27}{2}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{3}{2\lambda^2}=\frac{27}{2}\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda^2=\frac19\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda=\pm\frac13.$$
\(\lambda\)Point\(f\)
\(\tfrac13\)\(\left(\tfrac32,3,\tfrac32\right)\)\(9\)
\(-\tfrac13\)\(\left(-\tfrac32,-3,-\tfrac32\right)\)\(-9\)
Maximum value \(9\), attained at \(\left(\tfrac32,\,3,\,\tfrac32\right)\).
Sanity check with Cauchy–Schwarz: \(\max(x+2y+z)=\|(1,2,1)\|\cdot\text{radius}=\sqrt6\cdot\sqrt{27/2}=\sqrt{81}=9\) ✓, and the max point is the radius in the direction \((1,2,1)\).
Read the constraint carefully: it is \(\tfrac{27}{2}\), not 27. With 27 you'd get \(\lambda^2=\tfrac1{18}\) and irrational points — another "ugly numbers = re-read the question" signal.

S2 Question 7 — Rate of change toward a point [15 marks]

15 marks Let the electric potential \(P\) at any point \((r,s)\ne(0,0)\) be \(P(r,s)=\ln\sqrt{r^2+s^2}\). Find the rate of change of \(P\) at \((3,4)\) in the direction toward the point \((1,5)\).
Worked solution
"Rate of change in the direction toward point \(Q\)" → directional derivative with \(\mathbf u=\dfrac{Q-P_0}{\|Q-P_0\|}\). Simplify \(\ln\sqrt{\cdot}=\tfrac12\ln(\cdot)\) first. (Ch 5)
Direction: \((1,5)-(3,4)=(-2,1)\), \(\|(-2,1)\|=\sqrt5\), so \(\mathbf u=\left(-\tfrac2{\sqrt5},\tfrac1{\sqrt5}\right)\).
Gradient: \(P=\tfrac12\ln(r^2+s^2)\Rightarrow P_r=\dfrac{r}{r^2+s^2},\ P_s=\dfrac{s}{r^2+s^2}\). At \((3,4)\): \(\nabla P=\left(\tfrac3{25},\tfrac4{25}\right)\). $$D_{\mathbf u}P(3,4)=\left(\tfrac3{25},\tfrac4{25}\right)\cdot\left(-\tfrac2{\sqrt5},\tfrac1{\sqrt5}\right)=\frac{-6+4}{25\sqrt5}=-\frac{2}{25\sqrt5}.$$
\(D_{\mathbf u}P(3,4)=-\dfrac{2}{25\sqrt5}=-\dfrac{2\sqrt5}{125}\) — the potential is decreasing in that direction.
Direction "toward \((1,5)\)" is end − start \(=(1,5)-(3,4)\), not the point \((1,5)\) itself, and not \((3,4)-(1,5)\) (that flips the sign).
15 marks for a 4-line computation: the marks are in the visible steps — direction vector, its norm, unit vector, general gradient, gradient at the point, dot product. Show each.

S2 Question 8 — Work by a non-conservative force [15 marks]

Let \(\mathbf F(x,y)=-2y^2\,\mathbf i+3xy\,\mathbf j\).
(a)(a) Determine whether \(\mathbf F\) is a conservative vector field.
Worked solution
Test \(P_y\) vs \(Q_x\). (Ch 7)
$$P=-2y^2\Rightarrow P_y=-4y,\qquad Q=3xy\Rightarrow Q_x=3y.$$ \(-4y\ne3y\) whenever \(y\ne0\).
\(P_y\ne Q_x\), so \(\mathbf F\) is not conservative.
Consequence for (b): no potential function exists, so you cannot use "\(\phi(\text{end})-\phi(\text{start})\)". You must parametrise and integrate.
(b)(b) Determine the work done by the force \(\mathbf F\) in moving a particle along the path parametrised by \(\mathbf r(t)=(t^3-2t)\,\mathbf i+(t^2-2)\,\mathbf j,\ 0\le t\le1\). [(a)+(b) = 15 marks]
Worked solution
Work \(=\int_a^b\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\) — the official 5-step routine: par → diff → subs → dot → int.
diff: \(\mathbf r'(t)=(3t^2-2,\ 2t)\).
subs: \(\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=\big(-2(t^2-2)^2,\ 3(t^3-2t)(t^2-2)\big)=\big(-2(t^2-2)^2,\ 3t(t^2-2)^2\big)\).
dot: $$\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf r'=-2(t^2-2)^2(3t^2-2)+6t^2(t^2-2)^2=(t^2-2)^2(-6t^2+4+6t^2)=4(t^2-2)^2.$$ int: $$W=4\int_0^1(t^4-4t^2+4)\,dt=4\left[\frac{t^5}5-\frac{4t^3}3+4t\right]_0^1=4\left(\frac15-\frac43+4\right)=4\cdot\frac{43}{15}=\frac{172}{15}.$$
\(W=\dfrac{172}{15}\).
Spot the common factor: \(t^3-2t=t(t^2-2)\). Factoring \((t^2-2)^2\) out before expanding collapses the integrand to \(4(t^2-2)^2\) — far less algebra than expanding a degree-6 polynomial.

S2 Question 9 — Linear approximation [10 marks]

10 marks Let \(f(x,y)=\ln(x^2y)-x\sqrt y\) and \(P=\left(-\tfrac12,4\right)\). Find the linear approximation of \(f\) around \(P\).
Worked solution
\(L(x,y)=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)\) — three numbers to compute. (Ch 4)
Value: \(f\left(-\tfrac12,4\right)=\ln\!\left(\tfrac14\cdot4\right)-\left(-\tfrac12\right)(2)=\ln1+1=1\).
Partials: $$f_x=\frac{2xy}{x^2y}-\sqrt y=\frac2x-\sqrt y\;\Rightarrow\;f_x(P)=-4-2=-6,$$ $$f_y=\frac{x^2}{x^2y}-\frac{x}{2\sqrt y}=\frac1y-\frac{x}{2\sqrt y}\;\Rightarrow\;f_y(P)=\frac14+\frac18=\frac38.$$ $$L(x,y)=1-6\left(x+\tfrac12\right)+\tfrac38(y-4)=-6x+\tfrac38y-\tfrac72.$$
\(L(x,y)=1-6\left(x+\tfrac12\right)+\tfrac38(y-4)\).
\(x=-\tfrac12\) is negative, so \(-x\sqrt y=+1\) and \(-\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt y}=+\tfrac18\). Sign errors at negative points are the #1 slip on this question. (\(\ln(x^2y)\) is fine at negative \(x\) because \(x^2\gt0\); simplifying to \(2\ln x\) would not be valid there.)

S2 Question 10 — Chain rule \(dz/dt\) [5 marks]

5 marks Let \(z=xe^{y^2+4}\) where \(x=\dfrac1t\) and \(y=\cos2t\). Determine \(\dfrac{dz}{dt}\) at \(t=\dfrac\pi4\).
Worked solution
One parameter, two intermediate variables → \(\dfrac{dz}{dt}=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{dy}{dt}\) (tree diagram \(z\to x,y\to t\)). (Ch 5)
$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=e^{y^2+4},\quad \frac{dx}{dt}=-\frac1{t^2},\quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=2xy\,e^{y^2+4},\quad \frac{dy}{dt}=-2\sin2t.$$ At \(t=\tfrac\pi4\): \(x=\tfrac4\pi,\ y=\cos\tfrac\pi2=0\). The second term contains the factor \(y=0\), so it vanishes: $$\frac{dz}{dt}=e^{0+4}\left(-\frac1{(\pi/4)^2}\right)+0=-\frac{16e^4}{\pi^2}.$$
\(\dfrac{dz}{dt}\Big|_{t=\pi/4}=-\dfrac{16e^4}{\pi^2}.\)
Evaluate \(x,y\) at the given \(t\) before expanding — spotting \(y=0\) kills half the work.

What 2021 teaches

Patterns

  • Zero ODEs. Both papers are pure multivariable calculus, matching the modern weighting — high-value drills.
  • Every chapter-4-to-7 routine appears at least once per paper: tangent plane / linear approx, 2-D limit (DNE by two paths or exists by squeeze), directional derivative, chain rule, critical points + Hessian, Lagrange, arc length, conservative test + line integral.
  • The limit question flips between years: S1 = "show it does not exist" (two paths, one of them \(y=x^2\)); S2 = "find \(c\) for continuity" (squeeze, limit exists). Know both directions cold.
  • The conservative-field question flips too: S1 conservative ⇒ potential shortcut; S2 not conservative ⇒ must parametrise. Always test \(P_y=Q_x\) first — it tells you which route (b) needs.
  • Lagrange problems have clean answers. \(\lambda\) comes out as \(\pm\tfrac13\), points are integers or halves. If your numbers go ugly, re-check the constraint.
  • Contour sketches are marked on labels: centre, asymptotes, intercepts, and (S1 Q4) excluded points.

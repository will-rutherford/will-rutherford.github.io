§1 · Concepts in depth

This chapter needs one algebra skill above all: completing the square. If \(2x^2-12x = 2(x-3)^2-18\) doesn't feel automatic, do the drill in the Ground Floor first. Everything else here is built on it.

🧭 A surface is a landscape; a contour map is its topographic map📘 Workbook §2.1, 2.3

A function of two variables \(z=f(x,y)\) gives a height \(z\) above every point \((x,y)\) of the floor. Put all the points \((x,y,f(x,y))\) together and you get a surface in 3-D: a hill, a bowl, a saddle, a ridge.

You can't draw 3-D surfaces reliably by hand, especially in an exam. So you slice the surface with flat planes and draw the 2-D curves you get:

  • Horizontal slices \(z=c\) give contours (also called level curves). You draw them in the \(xy\)-plane, like the height lines on a hiking map. Each one is labelled with its height \(c\).
  • Vertical slices \(x=k\) or \(y=k\) give cross-sections (also called traces). You draw them in the \(yz\)-plane (for \(x=k\)) or the \(xz\)-plane (for \(y=k\)). Each one shows the profile you'd see if you cut the hill with a knife.

Summary p 5 puts it in one line: “Contour: \(z=f(x,y)=C\) is a horizontal cross-section… Cross-section: the intersection of a surface with a vertical plane such as \(x=k\) or \(y=C\).”

🧭 Why every slice in this course is a conic📘 Workbook §2.2

Almost every surface you meet in MATH1052 is quadratic: the variables appear at most squared (\(x^2, y^2, xy, x, y\)). Set \(z=c\) and you're left with a degree-2 equation in \(x\) and \(y\). Every degree-2 curve in the plane is a conic: a circle, ellipse, parabola or hyperbola, or a degenerate version (a point, two lines, or nothing at all).

So the whole chapter comes down to one skill used over and over:

The master routine
  1. Set the slice: \(z=c\) (contour), or \(x=k\) / \(y=k\) (cross-section). If the question gives a point, first compute \(c=f(\text{point})\).
  2. Clear any roots, fractions or exponentials so you have a polynomial equation. Record any restriction this creates, e.g. \(z\ge -3\), or “point \((0,0)\) excluded”.
  3. Complete the square in each variable.
  4. Divide to get “\(=1\)” (or “\(y=\dots\)” for a parabola). This is the standard form.
  5. Read off the type, centre/vertex, semi-axes, orientation and asymptotes.
  6. Intercepts: set \(y=0\) to get the \(x\)-intercepts and \(x=0\) to get the \(y\)-intercepts. If there are none, say so.
  7. Sketch and label: axes named, key points marked with coordinates, asymptotes dashed, each curve tagged with its level.

🧭 Identify the conic by eye, before any algebra📘 Workbook §2.2

Look only at the squared terms (T04 Q1 is this drill). Move everything to one side, then:

Squared termsConicExample
\(x^2\) and \(y^2\) with the same sign, equal coefficientsCircle\(x^2+y^2-2=0\)
\(x^2\) and \(y^2\) with the same sign, different coefficientsEllipse\(4x^2+9y^2-8x=32\)

“Same sign” gives an ellipse only if the right-hand side has the right sign. After completing the square you might get \((x-1)^2+2(y+3)^2=0\) (a single point) or \(=-4\) (no curve at all). Opposite signs with right-hand side \(0\) give two crossing lines. These degenerate cases appear exactly at the max, min or saddle height of a surface. Say “the contour is the single point…” or “the contour is the pair of lines…”, and never force an ellipse.

Completing the square — the only algebra you need📘 Workbook §2.2.1

Recipe (for \(Ax^2+Bx\)):
  1. Factor out the coefficient of \(x^2\) first: \(Ax^2+Bx = A\left(x^2+\frac BAx\right)\).
  2. Halve the new \(x\)-coefficient and square it: \(x^2+px = \left(x+\frac p2\right)^2-\frac{p^2}{4}\).
  3. Multiply back by \(A\): the correction becomes \(-A\cdot\frac{p^2}{4}\). Forgetting to multiply the correction by \(A\) is the #1 algebra error in this chapter.
$$ 4y^2-8y = 4(y^2-2y) = 4\big[(y-1)^2-1\big] = 4(y-1)^2-4, \qquad -x^2+2x = -(x^2-2x) = -(x-1)^2+1.$$

Check every completed square by mentally expanding it back out. It takes five seconds and protects every mark that follows.

🧭 Hyperbolas: which way do they open, and where are the asymptotes?📘 Workbook §2.2.4

Get the equation into the form “\(\text{(positive square)} - \text{(negative square)} = 1\)”, with a \(+1\) on the right. The variable in the positive term is the direction the hyperbola opens.

  • \(\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}-\frac{(y+2)^2}{9}=1\): the \(x\) term is positive, so it's an \(x\)-hyperbola. It opens left and right, with vertices at \((1\pm2,\,-2)\).
  • \(\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}-\frac{(y+2)^2}{9}=-1\): multiply by \(-1\) to get \(\frac{(y+2)^2}{9}-\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}=1\). Now the \(y\) term is positive, so it's a \(y\)-hyperbola. It opens up and down, with vertices at \((1,\,-2\pm3)\).

Asymptotes: set the right-hand side to 0. \(\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}=\frac{(y+2)^2}{9}\) gives \(y+2=\pm\frac32(x-1)\). This works because far from the centre the “\(=1\)” becomes negligible compared with the huge squared terms, so the curve hugs the lines where the left side equals zero. Both hyperbolas above share these asymptotes, and they pass through the centre \((1,-2)\), not the origin.

Rectangular hyperbolas \((x-h)(y-k)=c\)📘 Workbook §2.2.4

When the surface has an \(xy\) term and no \(x^2\) or \(y^2\) (like \(z=(x-3)(y+1)\), 2021 S2 Q1), factor instead of completing the square. For example, \(xy-2x+y = (x+1)(y-2)+2\). Then the contour \(z=c\) is \((x+1)(y-2)=c-2\), which gives \(y=2+\frac{c-2}{x+1}\). That's the familiar \(1/x\) curve shifted, with a vertical asymptote at \(x=-1\) and a horizontal asymptote at \(y=2\). If the constant on the right is positive, the branches sit top-right and bottom-left of the centre. If it's negative, they sit in the other two quadrants.

🧭 Contour diagrams: the spacing is information📘 Workbook §2.3

A contour diagram shows several level curves, usually at equally spaced heights like \(z=1,2,3,\dots\), on one set of axes. The spacing tells you about the slope:

  • Close together means the height changes fast, so the surface is steep. Far apart means it's flat.
  • Plane \(z=ax+by+c\): the contours are equally spaced parallel lines (Summary p 5).
  • Paraboloid \(z=x^2+y^2\): circles of radius \(\sqrt z\), which crowd together further out, because a bowl gets steeper as you climb.
  • Cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\): circles of radius \(z\), equally spaced, because a cone has constant slope.
  • Saddle \(z=x^2-y^2\): hyperbolas that open left–right for \(z\gt0\) and up–down for \(z\lt0\). The level \(z=0\) is the pair of lines \(y=\pm x\), which are the common asymptotes.

T06 Q2 (steel-plate isotherms) asks you to notice exactly this: “the contours become closer together as you move away from the origin.” One sentence like that is the kind of observation markers reward.

Quadric surfaces — name them from the equation📘 Workbook §2.1, 2.4

Counting trick. With three squared variables: all \(+\) gives an ellipsoid; one minus with \(=1\) gives one sheet; two minus with \(=1\) (equivalently one minus with \(=-1\)) gives two sheets; \(=0\) gives a cone. With two squares and one linear variable, it's a paraboloid: same signs make a bowl, opposite signs a saddle. The linear or odd-one-out variable is the axis (T06 Q5: \(x-(y+1)^2+(z-2)^2=0\) is a saddle with the \(x\)-axis playing the role of \(z\)).

Cross-sections: label the right axes, keep the right half📘 Workbook §2.4

  1. Substitute the fixed value: \(y=k\) leaves \(z\) as a function of \(x\). Draw it on axes labelled \(x\) (horizontal) and \(z\) (vertical). For \(x=k\), use \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. Identify the conic exactly as for contours. The only difference is that one of the variables is now \(z\).
  3. “Show the cross-sections are parabolas” means: do this for a general \(k\), and show the equation has the form \(z=A(x-h)^2+B\) (or \((z-p)^2=x-q\)) for every \(k\). Then state the vertex in terms of \(k\).

Square roots give half-curves. For \(z=\sqrt{\dots}-3\), squaring gives \((z+3)^2=\dots\), but only points with \(z+3\ge0\) are on the surface. The cross-section is the upper half of the parabola. Any intercept with \(z\lt-3\) (like \(z=-5\) in 2025 S2 Q1(b)) is not on the surface. Likewise \(z=\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}\) is only the top half of a sphere, so its cross-sections are semicircles.

🧭 Rational surfaces: “show all contours are circles/ellipses/hyperbolas”📘 Workbook §2.3

For \(z=\dfrac{8y}{x^2+2y^2}\) (2021 S1 Q4) or \(z=\dfrac{2x}{x^2-y^2}\) (2022 S1 Q2), set \(z=z_0\), multiply by the denominator, and complete the square with \(z_0\) kept as a letter. The centre and size then depend on \(z_0\). For example, \(\frac{x^2}{8/z_0^2}+\frac{(y-2/z_0)^2}{4/z_0^2}=1\) is an ellipse for every \(z_0\ne0\), and that is exactly what the question asks you to show.

Multiplying by the denominator can quietly add points where the denominator is zero. Those points are not in the domain. In both exam examples every contour passes through the origin, which is excluded. Mark it with an open circle and write “\((0,0)\) excluded”. The official solutions skip this, but it is a correct and cheap extra line.

§2 · Key methods & when to use them

None of this is on the formula sheet. The exam skill is fast, clean algebra plus a fully labelled sketch. Every method below ends in something you draw.

M1 · Contour through a given point📘 Workbook §2.3

$$ c = f(x_0,y_0), \qquad \text{contour: } f(x,y)=c$$
“Sketch the contour which passes through \((x_0,y_0)\)” (2024 S1 Q3, 2024 S2 Q1, 2025 S1 Q3). First line of your answer = evaluate \(f\) at the point. That line is worth 1 mark by itself (2024 S2 marking scheme).

Mini-example. \(f=x^2+4y^2-2x\) through \((3,1)\): \(c=9+4-6=7\), so the contour is \((x-1)^2+4y^2=8\), i.e. \(\frac{(x-1)^2}{8}+\frac{y^2}{2}=1\).

M2 · Standard form by completing the square📘 Workbook §2.2

$$ A x^2 + Bx + Cy^2 + Dy + E = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; A\Big(x+\tfrac{B}{2A}\Big)^2 + C\Big(y+\tfrac{D}{2C}\Big)^2 = \tfrac{B^2}{4A}+\tfrac{D^2}{4C}-E $$
Any quadratic with \(x^2\) and/or \(y^2\) terms. Then divide by the right-hand side to get “\(=1\)”.

Mini-example. \(x^2-6x+4y^2+8y+9=0\) becomes \((x-3)^2-9+4(y+1)^2-4+9=0\), so \((x-3)^2+4(y+1)^2=4\), i.e. \(\frac{(x-3)^2}{4}+(y+1)^2=1\).

M3 · Intercepts (the marks are literally for these)📘 Workbook §2.2

$$ x\text{-intercepts: set } y=0;\qquad y\text{-intercepts: set } x=0 $$
Every sketch question says “label all \(x\)- and \(y\)-intercepts”. Solve each resulting one-variable quadratic. A negative square means no intercept. Write “no \(x\)-intercepts” explicitly (worth 1 mark in 2025 S1). A zero discriminant means the curve touches the axis at one point.

Mini-example. For \(\frac{(x-3)^2}{4}+(y+1)^2=1\), setting \(y=0\) gives \((x-3)^2=0\), so the curve touches the \(x\)-axis at \((3,0)\). Setting \(x=0\) gives \((y+1)^2=1-\frac94\lt0\), so there are no \(y\)-intercepts.

M4 · Hyperbola asymptotes📘 Workbook §2.2.4

$$ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2}-\frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2}=\pm1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{asymptotes } y-k=\pm\frac{b}{a}(x-h)$$
Any hyperbola, whenever the question says “asymptotes”, or even when it doesn't (2025 S1 gave 1 mark for them). Draw them dashed through the centre, then draw the branches hugging them.

Mini-example. \(y^2-\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}=1\): setting the right side to 0 gives \(y^2=\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}\), so \(y=\pm\frac{x-1}{2}\).

M5 · Several contours on one set of axes📘 Workbook §2.3

“Sketch the contours \(z=-2,0,1\) on the same set of axes” (2025 S2 Q1), “contours \(z=1\) and \(z=-4\)” (2023 S2 Q4). Complete the square once with a general \(c\), then substitute each level. Check the range first: some levels may be impossible (empty) or degenerate.

Mini-example. \(z=4x^2-y^2\). Level \(4\): \(x^2-\frac{y^2}{4}=1\). Level \(0\): the lines \(y=\pm2x\). Level \(-4\): \(\frac{y^2}{4}-x^2=1\). All three share the asymptotes \(y=\pm2x\).

M6 · Vertical cross-sections📘 Workbook §2.4

$$ y=k:\; z=f(x,k)\ \text{drawn in the } xz\text{-plane};\qquad x=k:\; z=f(k,y)\ \text{drawn in the } yz\text{-plane}$$
“Show that the cross-sections for \(y=k\) are parabolas” (2025 S2 Q1(b)), or “use contours and/or cross-sections to sketch the surface” (T06 Q4). Keep \(k\) general, then specialise.

Mini-example. For \(z=x^2-y^2+2y\) and \(x=k\): \(z=-(y-1)^2+1+k^2\). This is a down-parabola in the \(yz\)-plane with vertex \((y,z)=(1,\,1+k^2)\), for every \(k\).

M7 · “Show all contours are …” for a rational \(f\)📘 Workbook §2.3

\(z=\dfrac{\text{linear}}{\text{quadratic}}\) (2021 S1 Q4, 2022 S1 Q2). Set \(z=z_0\ne0\), multiply out, complete the square with \(z_0\) as a letter, and divide to reach “\(=1\)”. Name the family and give the centre in terms of \(z_0\). Mention the excluded point(s).

Mini-example. \(z=\frac{4x}{x^2+y^2}\): \(z_0(x^2+y^2)=4x\) gives \(\left(x-\frac2{z_0}\right)^2+y^2=\frac4{z_0^2}\). These are circles with centre \(\left(\frac2{z_0},0\right)\) and radius \(\frac2{|z_0|}\), all through the excluded origin.

M8 · Max/min of a quadratic surface by completing the square📘 Workbook §2.2–2.3

A follow-up part like “find the minimum value of \(z\)” (2024 S1 Q3(b)) or “determine the maximum temperature” (2024 S2 Q1(b)). The completed square you already have is the answer: \((x+1)^2+4(y-1)^2+1\ge1\), so the minimum is \(1\), at \((-1,1)\). For \(T=\frac{100}{D}\), \(T\) is largest where the positive denominator \(D\) is smallest. Ch 6 does this with derivatives, but this is faster.

M9 · The sketch-labelling checklist (use it on every sketch)📘 Workbook §2.2–2.4

  1. Axes drawn and named (\(x,y\) for contours; \(x,z\) or \(y,z\) for cross-sections).
  2. Each curve tagged with its level, e.g. “\(z=1\)”.
  3. Centre (ellipse/hyperbola) or vertex (parabola) marked with coordinates.
  4. All intercepts marked with exact values (\(1\pm2\sqrt5\), not \(5.47\)). Missing intercepts stated in words.
  5. Hyperbolas: asymptotes dashed, with their equations written.
  6. Excluded points as open circles; half-curves drawn as halves.
  7. Rough scale honest: an ellipse with \(a=2,b=1\) should look twice as wide as it is tall.

Example 1 — Parabolas, upright and sideways (vertex + intercepts)

Q. Sketch (a) \(y=x^2-4x-5\) and (b) \(x=-y^2+2y+3\), labelling the vertex and all intercepts. [3 + 3 marks]

(a) Complete the square: \(y=(x-2)^2-4-5=(x-2)^2-9\). This is an upward parabola (the coefficient of the square is \(+1\)) with vertex \((2,-9)\) and axis \(x=2\).

  • \(y\)-intercept: \(x=0\) gives \(y=-5\).
  • \(x\)-intercepts: \((x-2)^2=9\), so \(x-2=\pm3\), giving \(x=-1\) and \(x=5\). (Check: the roots are symmetric about \(x=2\).)

(b) \(x=-(y^2-2y)+3=-\big[(y-1)^2-1\big]+3=-(y-1)^2+4\). This is a sideways parabola opening left (the coefficient is \(-1\)) with vertex \((4,1)\) and axis \(y=1\).

  • \(x\)-intercept: \(y=0\) gives \(x=3\).
  • \(y\)-intercepts: \(0=-(y-1)^2+4\), so \(y-1=\pm2\), giving \(y=3\) and \(y=-1\).
🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: vertex \((2,-9)\) / \((4,1)\); every intercept \((-1,0),(5,0),(0,-5)\) / \((3,0),(0,3),(0,-1)\); correct opening direction; symmetric shape about the axis.
Example 2 — Ellipse from a messy quadratic (tangent intercept!)

Q. Identify and sketch \(9x^2+4y^2-36x+8y+4=0\). Label the centre, semi-axes and all intercepts. [6 marks]

Identify first: \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) have the same sign and different coefficients, so expect an ellipse.

$$9(x^2-4x)+4(y^2+2y)+4=0 \;\Rightarrow\; 9\big[(x-2)^2-4\big]+4\big[(y+1)^2-1\big]+4=0$$ $$9(x-2)^2+4(y+1)^2=36 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{(x-2)^2}{4}+\frac{(y+1)^2}{9}=1.$$

This is an ellipse with centre \((2,-1)\), semi-axis \(2\) horizontally and \(3\) vertically. The major axis is vertical. Extreme points: \((0,-1),(4,-1),(2,2),(2,-4)\).

  • \(x\)-intercepts (\(y=0\)): \(\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}=1-\frac19=\frac89\), so \((x-2)^2=\frac{32}{9}\), giving \(x=2\pm\frac{4\sqrt2}{3}\).
  • \(y\)-intercepts (\(x=0\)): \(1+\frac{(y+1)^2}{9}=1\), so \(y=-1\) (a double root). The ellipse touches the \(y\)-axis at \((0,-1)\), its leftmost point.
🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: centre \((2,-1)\); the four extreme points or the semi-axes \(2\) and \(3\); \(x\)-intercepts \(2\pm\frac{4\sqrt2}{3}\); the tangency at \((0,-1)\) (say “touches”); taller than wide.
Example 3 — Hyperbola: centre, vertices, asymptotes, missing intercepts

Q. Classify and sketch \(x^2-4y^2-2x-16y-19=0\), labelling intercepts and asymptotes. [7 marks]

$$(x-1)^2-1-4\big[(y+2)^2-4\big]-19=0 \;\Rightarrow\; (x-1)^2-4(y+2)^2=4 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{(x-1)^2}{4}-(y+2)^2=1.$$

The \(x\) term is positive, so this is an \(x\)-hyperbola (opening left/right) with centre \((1,-2)\), \(a=2\), \(b=1\) and vertices \((-1,-2)\) and \((3,-2)\).

  • Asymptotes (set the right side to 0): \((y+2)^2=\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}\), so \(y=-2\pm\frac12(x-1)\).
  • \(x\)-intercepts: \(\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}=1+4=5\), so \(x=1\pm2\sqrt5\).
  • \(y\)-intercepts: \(\frac14-(y+2)^2=1\), so \((y+2)^2=-\frac34\lt0\). No \(y\)-intercepts. This makes sense: the gap between the branches, from \(x=-1\) to \(x=3\), contains the \(y\)-axis.
🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: centre \((1,-2)\) (a dot, not on the curve); vertices \((-1,-2),(3,-2)\); both asymptotes dashed with equations; \(x\)-intercepts \(1\pm2\sqrt5\); the words “no \(y\)-intercepts”.
Example 4 — Saddle contour family \(z=4x^2-y^2\) for \(z=-4,0,4\)

Q. Sketch the contours \(z=-4, 0, 4\) of \(z=4x^2-y^2\) on the same axes. Label each contour and all intercepts. [6 marks] (Same style as T06 Q1.)

z = 4: 4x² − y² = 4 ⇒ x² − y²/4 = 1 x-hyperbola, vertices (±1, 0) x-int: x = ±1; y-int: −y² = 4 ⇒ none z = 0: 4x² − y² = 0 ⇒ (2x − y)(2x + y) = 0 ⇒ the two LINES y = 2x and y = −2x z = −4: 4x² − y² = −4 ⇒ y²/4 − x² = 1 y-hyperbola, vertices (0, ±2) y-int: y = ±2; x-int: 4x² = −4 ⇒ none All three share the asymptotes y = ±2x (the z = 0 contour itself).

The surface is a saddle (hyperbolic paraboloid). Along the \(x\)-axis it rises (\(z=4x^2\)); along the \(y\)-axis it falls (\(z=-y^2\)).

🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: each curve tagged \(z=4\), \(z=0\), \(z=-4\); intercepts \((\pm1,0)\) and \((0,\pm2)\); \(z=0\) drawn as two straight lines \(y=\pm2x\) (not a hyperbola); the positive-level curve opening left/right and the negative-level curve up/down.
Example 5 — Paraboloid: contours, a cross-section, and the minimum

Q. Let \(z=x^2+y^2-2x+4y+1\). (a) Find the minimum value of \(z\). (b) Sketch the contours \(z=-4, 0, 5\). (c) Sketch the cross-section \(y=-2\). [2 + 5 + 3 marks]

(a) \(z=(x-1)^2+(y+2)^2-4\ge-4\), with equality only at \((1,-2)\). The minimum is \(-4\). The surface is a circular paraboloid (bowl) with lowest point \((1,-2,-4)\).

(b) The contour \(z=c\) is \((x-1)^2+(y+2)^2=c+4\): a circle about \((1,-2)\) with radius \(\sqrt{c+4}\).

z = −4: radius 0 ⇒ the single POINT (1, −2) z = 0: radius 2 ⇒ x-int: (x−1)² + 4 = 4 ⇒ x = 1 (touches the x-axis at (1,0)) y-int: 1 + (y+2)² = 4 ⇒ y = −2 ± √3 z = 5: radius 3 ⇒ x-int: (x−1)² = 5 ⇒ x = 1 ± √5 y-int: (y+2)² = 8 ⇒ y = −2 ± 2√2

(c) With \(y=-2\), \(z=(x-1)^2-4\). In the \(xz\)-plane this is an up-parabola with vertex \((x,z)=(1,-4)\), \(x\)-intercepts \(x=-1, 3\) (where \(z=0\)), and \(z\)-intercept \(z=-3\).

🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: (b) concentric circles centred at \((1,-2)\), tagged \(z=0\) and \(z=5\), the point \(z=-4\) marked; every intercept, exact. (c) axes labelled \(x\) and \(z\); vertex \((1,-4)\); intercepts \(x=-1,3\) and \(z=-3\).
Example 6 — An \(xy\) surface: rectangular-hyperbola contour

Q. For \(z=xy-2x+y\), sketch the contour \(z=5\). Label all intercepts and asymptotes. [5 marks] (Same style as 2021 S2 Q1.)

There's an \(xy\) term and no squares, so factor rather than complete the square: \(xy-2x+y=(x+1)(y-2)+2\). Check: \(xy-2x+y-2+2\). ✓

$$ z=5:\quad (x+1)(y-2)=3 \;\Longrightarrow\; y=2+\frac{3}{x+1}.$$
  • This is a rectangular hyperbola with centre \((-1,2)\) and asymptotes \(x=-1\) and \(y=2\). Because \(3\gt0\), the branches lie top-right and bottom-left of the centre.
  • \(y\)-intercept: \(x=0\) gives \(y=2+3=5\).
  • \(x\)-intercept: \(y=0\) gives \((x+1)(-2)=3\), so \(x=-\frac52\).
🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: dashed asymptotes \(x=-1\), \(y=2\); intercepts \((0,5)\) (right branch) and \((-\frac52,0)\) (left branch); branches in the correct quadrants relative to \((-1,2)\).
Example 7 — Rational surface: “show every contour is a circle”

Q. Let \(z=\dfrac{4x}{x^2+y^2}\), \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\). (a) Show that for every \(c\ne0\) the contour \(z=c\) is a circle, and give its centre and radius. (b) Sketch the contours \(z=2\) and \(z=-1\). [5 + 4 marks]

(a) \(c(x^2+y^2)=4x\), so \(x^2-\frac4cx+y^2=0\). Completing the square:

$$\Big(x-\frac2c\Big)^2+y^2=\frac{4}{c^2}.$$

For every \(c\ne0\) this is a circle with centre \(\left(\frac2c,0\right)\) and radius \(\frac{2}{|c|}\). Since the centre is at distance \(\frac{2}{|c|}\) from the origin, every circle passes through \((0,0)\). That point is not in the domain, so it is removed from every contour.

(b) \(z=2\): \((x-1)^2+y^2=1\), with centre \((1,0)\), radius \(1\), and \(x\)-intercepts \(0\) (excluded) and \(2\). \(z=-1\): \((x+2)^2+y^2=4\), with centre \((-2,0)\), radius \(2\), and \(x\)-intercepts \(0\) (excluded) and \(-4\). (For \(c=0\): \(x=0\), the \(y\)-axis minus the origin.)

🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: the general standard form with \(c\) visible; centre and radius for each level; the two circles tangent to the \(y\)-axis at the origin, on opposite sides; open circle at \((0,0)\) with “excluded”.
Example 8 — Square-root surface: domain, contours, half-hyperbola cross-section

Q. Let \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2-4}\). (a) State the domain and range. (b) Sketch the contours \(z=0\), \(z=\sqrt5\), \(z=2\sqrt3\). (c) Sketch the cross-section \(y=0\). [2 + 4 + 4 marks]

(a) Need \(x^2+y^2\ge4\): the plane outside, and on, the circle of radius 2. Range \(z\ge0\).

(b) \(z=c\ge0\) gives \(x^2+y^2=4+c^2\): circles about the origin with radius \(\sqrt{4+c^2}\). So \(z=0\) has radius \(2\), \(z=\sqrt5\) has radius \(3\), and \(z=2\sqrt3\) has radius \(4\). Equal steps in radius need growing steps in \(z\).

(c) With \(y=0\), \(z=\sqrt{x^2-4}\). Squaring gives \(x^2-z^2=4\) with \(z\ge0\). In the \(xz\)-plane this is the upper half of an \(x\)-hyperbola: vertices \((\pm2,0)\), asymptotes \(z=\pm x\) (only the parts with \(z\ge0\), i.e. \(z=|x|\) far out), and nothing for \(|x|\lt2\).

🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: (b) three circles tagged with their \(z\)-values and radii \(2,3,4\). (c) axes \(x\) and \(z\); vertices \((\pm2,0)\); dashed guide lines \(z=\pm x\); only the upper halves drawn, with a note “\(z\ge0\)”.
Example 9 — Name a quadric and describe its traces

Q. Identify the surface \(4x^2+y^2-z^2=4\) and describe its traces in the planes \(z=k\), \(x=0\) and \(y=0\). [5 marks]

There are three squared variables with one minus sign and a positive right side, so this is a hyperboloid of one sheet around the \(z\)-axis (the odd one out).

  • \(z=k\): \(4x^2+y^2=4+k^2\), i.e. \(\frac{x^2}{(4+k^2)/4}+\frac{y^2}{4+k^2}=1\). This is an ellipse for every \(k\) (the surface is one connected tube), smallest at \(k=0\) (\(x^2+\frac{y^2}{4}=1\), the “waist”).
  • \(x=0\): \(y^2-z^2=4\), a hyperbola in the \(yz\)-plane opening along \(\pm y\).
  • \(y=0\): \(4x^2-z^2=4\), i.e. \(x^2-\frac{z^2}{4}=1\), a hyperbola in the \(xz\)-plane opening along \(\pm x\).
🏷️ What the marker wants: the name (“hyperboloid of one sheet”); the axis (\(z\)); the justification (“horizontal traces are ellipses for every \(z\), vertical traces are hyperbolas”).

📄 Real exam question — 2024 S2 Q1 [12 marks]

A thin metal plate is located in the \(xy\)-plane. The temperature \(T\) at a point \((x,y)\) on the metal plate is given by

$$T(x,y)=\frac{100}{3+x^2+2y^2-4y}.$$
  • (a) Sketch the contour of \(T\) which passes through the point \((x,y)=(2,1)\). Label the \(x\)-intercepts and \(y\)-intercepts of the contour. [8 marks]
  • (b) Determine the maximum temperature on the metal plate. [4 marks]
Full worked solution — 2024 S2 Q1(a)–(b)

(a) \(T(2,1)=\dfrac{100}{3+4+2-4}=\dfrac{100}{5}=20\). [1 mark]

The isotherm \(T=20\) is \(3+x^2+2y^2-4y=5\). Completing the square in \(y\): \(x^2+2\big[(y-1)^2-1\big]+3=5\), so

$$x^2+2(y-1)^2=4 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{(y-1)^2}{2}=1. \quad\textit{[3 marks]}$$

This is an ellipse with centre \((0,1)\), semi-axes \(2\) (horizontal) and \(\sqrt2\) (vertical).

  • \(x\)-intercepts (\(y=0\)): \(\frac{x^2}{4}+\frac12=1\), so \(x=\pm\sqrt2\).
  • \(y\)-intercepts (\(x=0\)): \((y-1)^2=2\), so \(y=1\pm\sqrt2\). [2 marks]

(b) \(3+x^2+2y^2-4y=x^2+2(y-1)^2+1\ge1\), with equality only at \((0,1)\). The denominator is positive and smallest there, so \(T\) is largest there: \(T_{\max}=\dfrac{100}{1}=\boxed{100}\) at \((0,1)\).

🏷️ What the marker wants labelled: centre \((0,1)\); \(x\)-intercepts \(\pm\sqrt2\); \(y\)-intercepts \(1\pm\sqrt2\); the point \((2,1)\) on the curve (it's the right-hand extreme point). The official sketch earns 2 marks for exactly these.
Also Type A — 2024 S1 Q3 (8 + 4 marks): \(z=4y^2-8y+x^2+2x+6\), contour through \((-1,2)\); minimum of \(z\)

Question (verbatim): Consider the surface \(z=4y^2-8y+x^2+2x+6\). (a) (8 marks) Sketch the contour which passes through \((x,y)=(-1,2)\). Label all \(x\)-intercepts and \(y\)-intercepts of this contour. (b) (4 marks) Find the minimum value of \(z\) on the surface.

  • \(x\)-intercept: \(y=0\) gives \((x+1)^2=0\), so \(x=-1\). The ellipse touches the \(x\)-axis at \((-1,0)\).

Also Type A — 2025 S1 Q3 (8 + 4 marks): \(f=4y^2-x^2+2x\), contour through \((1,1)\) — a hyperbola

Question (verbatim): Consider the surface given by \(f(x,y)=4y^2-x^2+2x\). (a) (8 marks) Sketch the contour which passes through \((x,y)=(1,1)\). Label any \(x\)-intercepts, \(y\)-intercepts and asymptotes of the contour. (b) (4 marks) Find and classify the critical point(s) of \(f\).

(a) \(f(1,1)=4-1+2=5\). Then \(4y^2-\big[(x-1)^2-1\big]=5\), so \(4y^2-(x-1)^2=4\):

$$ y^2-\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}=1. \qquad\textit{[3 marks: working + standard form]}$$

The \(y\) term is positive, so this is a \(y\)-hyperbola with centre \((1,0)\) and vertices \((1,\pm1)\). The given point \((1,1)\) is a vertex.

  • Asymptotes: \(y=\pm\frac12(x-1)\). [1 mark]
  • \(x\)-intercepts: \(y=0\) gives \(-(x-1)^2=4\), so no \(x\)-intercepts. [1 mark]
  • \(y\)-intercepts: \(x=0\) gives \(4y^2=5\), so \(y=\pm\frac{\sqrt5}{2}\). [1 mark]

(b) \(f=4y^2-(x-1)^2+1\), or via \(\nabla f=(-2x+2,\,8y)=\mathbf 0\): the only critical point is \((1,0)\). \(f\) increases in \(y\) and decreases in \(x\) away from it (Hessian determinant \(=(-2)(8)-0=-16\lt0\)), so it is a saddle point with \(f(1,0)=1\).

🏷️ Labels (MS: “2 marks for sketch; must label intercepts and asymptotes”): dashed asymptotes \(y=\pm\frac{x-1}{2}\) through \((1,0)\); vertices \((1,\pm1)\); \(y\)-intercepts \(\pm\frac{\sqrt5}{2}\); the words “no \(x\)-intercepts”.

📄 Real exam question — 2023 S2 Q4(b) [9 marks]

Consider the surface $$z=\frac14x^2-\frac19y^2-x+\frac29y+\frac89.$$ Sketch the contours \(z=1\) and \(z=-4\). In addition, for each contour, describe what kind of curve it is. Hint: If \(z=0\), then \(y=(8-3x)/2\) or \(y=(3x-4)/2\).

Full worked solution — 2023 S2 Q4(b)

$$z=\tfrac14(x^2-4x+4)-\tfrac19(y^2-2y+1)=\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}-\frac{(y-1)^2}{9}.$$

This is a saddle centred at \((2,1)\). The hint is the \(z=0\) contour, \(y-1=\pm\frac32(x-2)\), which is exactly the pair of asymptotes.

\(z=1\): \(\dfrac{(x-2)^2}{4}-\dfrac{(y-1)^2}{9}=1\). This is a horizontal (\(x\)-) hyperbola with centre \((2,1)\), \(a=2\), \(b=3\), and vertices \((0,1)\) and \((4,1)\).

  • \(x\)-intercepts: \(\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}=1+\frac19\), so \(x=2\pm\frac{2\sqrt{10}}{3}\). \(y\)-intercept: \(x=0\) gives \(y=1\), the vertex \((0,1)\) sitting on the \(y\)-axis.

Asymptotes for both: \(y=1\pm\frac32(x-2)\), i.e. \(y=\frac{3x-4}{2}\) and \(y=\frac{8-3x}{2}\).

🏷️ Labels: centre \((2,1)\); the two dashed asymptotes (the hint lines); \(z=1\) branches through \((0,1),(4,1)\); \(z=-4\) branches through \((2,7),(2,-5)\); each tagged with its level and named (“horizontal hyperbola”, “vertical hyperbola”). 📄 Real exam question — 2021 S1 Q4 [10 marks]

Consider the surface $$z=\frac{8y}{x^2+2y^2}.$$ (a) Show that all contours \(z=z_0\) (with \(z_0\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\)) are ellipses. (6 marks) (b) Sketch the contour for \(z_0=2\). (4 marks)

Full worked solution — 2021 S1 Q4(a)–(b)

(a) Set \(z=z_0\ne0\): \(x^2+2y^2=\dfrac{8y}{z_0}\), so \(x^2+2\left(y^2-\dfrac{4}{z_0}y\right)=0\), and

$$x^2+2\Big(y-\frac{2}{z_0}\Big)^2-\frac{8}{z_0^2}=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{x^2}{8/z_0^2}+\frac{(y-2/z_0)^2}{4/z_0^2}=1.$$

Both denominators are positive for every \(z_0\ne0\), so each contour is an ellipse with centre \((0,\,2/z_0)\) and semi-axes \(\frac{2\sqrt2}{|z_0|}\) (horizontal) and \(\frac{2}{|z_0|}\) (vertical).

(b) \(z_0=2\): \(\dfrac{x^2}{2}+(y-1)^2=1\). This is an ellipse with centre \((0,1)\) and semi-axes \(\sqrt2\) and \(1\). Extreme points: \((\pm\sqrt2,1)\), \((0,2)\), \((0,0)\).

  • \(y\)-intercepts: \(y=0\) and \(y=2\). \(x\)-intercept: only \(x=0\) (the ellipse touches the \(x\)-axis at the origin).
The origin is on this ellipse but not in the domain (the denominator \(x^2+2y^2=0\) there). Draw an open circle at \((0,0)\). The official solution stops at the standard form; this extra line shows you understand what the equation means.
🏷️ Labels: centre \((0,1)\); \((\pm\sqrt2,1)\); top \((0,2)\); open circle at \((0,0)\).

📄 Real exam question — 2022 S1 Q2 [10 marks]

Consider the surface $$z=\frac{2x}{x^2-y^2}.$$ a) For any \(z_0\ne0\), show that the contours \(z=z_0\) of the surface are hyperbolas. (5 marks) b) Sketch the contour for \(z=1\). Be sure to indicate the hyperbola’s center, and asymptotes. (5 marks)

Full worked solution — 2022 S1 Q2(a)–(b)

(a) \(z_0(x^2-y^2)=2x\), so \(z_0x^2-2x-z_0y^2=0\), so \(z_0\left(x-\frac1{z_0}\right)^2-\frac1{z_0}-z_0y^2=0\). Multiply by \(z_0\):

$$z_0^2\Big(x-\frac1{z_0}\Big)^2-z_0^2y^2=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{(x-1/z_0)^2}{1/z_0^2}-\frac{y^2}{1/z_0^2}=1.$$

For every \(z_0\ne0\) this is an \(x\)-hyperbola with centre \((1/z_0,0)\) and asymptotes \(y=\pm(x-1/z_0)\).

(b) \(z=1\): \((x-1)^2-y^2=1\). Centre \((1,0)\), vertices \((0,0)\) and \((2,0)\), asymptotes \(y=\pm(x-1)\).

The vertex \((0,0)\) makes \(x^2-y^2=0\), so it is excluded. It is the only point of this hyperbola on the lines \(y=\pm x\) (solve \((x-1)^2-x^2=1\) to get \(x=0\)). Open circle at the origin; the left branch is otherwise complete.
🏷️ Labels (question says “be sure to indicate”): centre \((1,0)\) — a dot not on the curve; asymptotes \(y=x-1\) and \(y=1-x\) dashed; vertices \((0,0)^{\circ}\) (open) and \((2,0)\).

📄 Real exam question — 2020 S1 Q2 [10 marks]

Let a conic section be given by \(4x^2-y^2+4x=6y-4\). (i) Determine the type of conic section. (5 marks) (ii) Sketch the conic section. Be sure to identify the center, foci, or asymptotes. (5 marks)

Full worked solution — 2020 S1 Q2

(i) \(4x^2+4x-(y^2+6y)=-4\), so \(4\left(x+\frac12\right)^2-1-\big[(y+3)^2-9\big]=-4\), so

$$4\Big(x+\tfrac12\Big)^2-(y+3)^2=-12 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{(y+3)^2}{12}-\frac{(x+\frac12)^2}{3}=1.$$

This is a hyperbola opening up/down (a \(y\)-hyperbola) with centre \(\left(-\frac12,-3\right)\).

(ii) \(b=\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3\) (vertical), \(a=\sqrt3\). Vertices \(\left(-\frac12,\,-3\pm2\sqrt3\right)\). Asymptotes \(y+3=\pm\frac{2\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}\left(x+\frac12\right)\), i.e. \(y=-3\pm2\left(x+\frac12\right)\) (\(y=2x-2\) and \(y=-2x-4\)). Foci: \(c=\sqrt{12+3}=\sqrt{15}\), so the foci are at \(\left(-\frac12,\,-3\pm\sqrt{15}\right)\).

  • \(y\)-intercepts: \(x=0\) gives \(1-(y+3)^2=-12\), so \(y=-3\pm\sqrt{13}\). \(x\)-intercepts: \(y=0\) gives \(4x^2+4x+4=0\) with discriminant \(\lt0\), so none.
🏷️ Labels: centre \(\left(-\frac12,-3\right)\); vertices; foci; both asymptotes with equations; intercepts \(y=-3\pm\sqrt{13}\); “no \(x\)-intercepts”.
Also Type D — 2020 S2 Q8 (10 marks): ellipse, parametrisation, tangent parallel to \(y=-x\)

Question (verbatim): Sketch the conic section given by \(4x^2-16x+y^2-2y+13=0\). Find a parametric representation of this curve. Identify a point on the curve for which the velocity vector (tangent to the curve) is parallel to the line \(y=-x\).

§5 · Wording triggers — the money table

Read the verb, and you know the routine and what the marks are for.

When the exam says…It means / do this
“sketch the contour which passes through \((x_0,y_0)\)”Line 1: \(c=f(x_0,y_0)\) (1 mark). Then \(f=c\), standard form, intercepts, sketch. Check the point is on your curve.
“sketch the contours \(z=a, b, c\) on the same set of axes”One general standard form in \(c\); one diagram; every curve tagged with its level. Check the range first, since a level can be empty or degenerate.
“label all intercepts with the coordinate axes”\(y=0\) for \(x\)-intercepts and \(x=0\) for \(y\)-intercepts, for each curve. Exact surds. Write “none” when there are none.
“label … asymptotes” / “indicate the hyperbola’s center, and asymptotes”Hyperbola: set the right side to 0 to get the lines through the centre, drawn dashed with equations. Rectangular: \(x=h\), \(y=k\).
“describe what kind of curve it is” / “determine the type of conic”Name it precisely: “horizontal hyperbola centred \((2,1)\), \(a=2\), \(b=3\)”, “ellipse centred …”, “pair of lines”, “single point”.
“show that the contours \(z=z_0\) are ellipses/hyperbolas” (\(z_0\ne0\))Keep \(z_0\) as a letter; complete the square; reach “\(=1\)” with positive denominators; state “for all \(z_0\ne0\) …” and give the centre in terms of \(z_0\).
“show that the cross-sections for \(y=k\) are parabolas”Substitute \(y=k\) (general \(k\)); rearrange to parabola form in \(x\) and \(z\); give the vertex as a function of \(k\). Sketch on \(x\)–\(z\) axes.
“find the minimum value of \(z\)” / “maximum temperature” right after a contour partReuse the completed square: sum of squares plus a constant means the constant is the min, at the centre. For \(100/D\), max \(T\) is where \(D\) is smallest.
“isothermal curves / isotherms / level curves / contour lines”All mean contours \(f=c\).
“use contours and/or cross-sections to sketch the surface”A few contours and the \(x=0\), \(y=0\) sections; name the quadric (bowl / saddle / cone …).
“identify the surface”Quadric name + axis + centre/vertex (e.g. “saddle centred \((0,-1,2)\) with the \(x\)-axis in the role of \(z\)”).
“match … enter 0 if there is no match”Fingerprints: zero set, sign, symmetry, spacing. There's usually at least one 0.
\(z=\sqrt{\dots}\), \(\ln(\dots)\), a fractionState the domain/range restriction before squaring or clearing. Half-curves and excluded points follow from it.

§6 · Traps & marks lost

Not factoring the coefficient before completing the square. \(4y^2-8y\ne(2y-2)^2-4\) in a useful way. Write \(4(y^2-2y)=4(y-1)^2-4\). The correction term is multiplied by the coefficient.
Skipping the value at the given point. In 2024 S2 the first mark was for \(T(2,1)=20\) alone. Always write it as its own line.
Not stating that intercepts don't exist. 2025 S1 awarded a mark for “no \(x\)-intercepts”. If you silently omit them, the marker can't tell whether you checked.
Wrong hyperbola orientation. The positive term (after making the right side \(+1\)) decides the direction. With \(=-4\) on the right you must divide by \(-4\), which flips which term is positive (2023 S2: \(z=1\) opens sideways, \(z=-4\) opens up/down).
Asymptotes through the origin instead of the centre. For centre \((1,0)\) they are \(y=\pm\frac12(x-1)\), not \(y=\pm\frac12x\).
Calling a degenerate level a conic. At the critical height the “ellipse” is a point and the “hyperbola” is two lines (2023 S1: \(f=1\) is the two lines \(x=0\), \(y=-1\)).
Forgetting that \(\sqrt{\ }\ge0\). 2025 S2: \(z\ge-3\). The contour at a level below the range is empty, and the cross-section is a half-parabola, so \(z=-5\) is not an intercept of the surface.
Drawing through excluded points. Rational surfaces (2021 S1, 2022 S1) have contours through the origin, where \(f\) is undefined. Open circle.
Cross-section on the wrong axes. A \(y=k\) section lives in the \(xz\)-plane. Label the vertical axis \(z\), and give the vertex as \((x,z)\).
Touching vs crossing. A double root means the curve is tangent to the axis (2024 S1: the ellipse touches the \(x\)-axis at \((-1,0)\)). Draw it just kissing the axis and label it.
Decimals instead of exact values. No calculator: write \(1\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), \(2\pm\frac{2\sqrt{10}}{3}\). An approximate position on the sketch is fine, but the label must be exact.
Unlabelled curves in a multi-contour sketch. Three parabolas without “\(z=-2\)”, “\(z=0\)”, “\(z=1\)” tags can't be marked as correct.

§7 · Self-check

1. Without completing the square: what conic is \(3x^2-5y^2+6x-10y=4\)?

Opposite signs on \(x^2\) and \(y^2\), so a hyperbola (or a line pair if the constant works out to zero).

2. Complete the square: \(3x^2+12x\).

\(3(x^2+4x)=3\big[(x+2)^2-4\big]=3(x+2)^2-12\).

3. Which way does \(\frac{(y-2)^2}{4}-\frac{(x+1)^2}{9}=1\) open, and what are its vertices and asymptotes?

The \(y\) term is positive, so it opens up/down. Centre \((-1,2)\), vertices \((-1,0)\) and \((-1,4)\), asymptotes \(y-2=\pm\frac23(x+1)\).

4. What does \(\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}-\frac{(y+2)^2}{9}=-1\) become?

Multiply by \(-1\): \(\frac{(y+2)^2}{9}-\frac{(x-1)^2}{4}=1\). A \(y\)-hyperbola with the same centre and asymptotes as the \(=+1\) version.

5. The contour \(z=c\) of \(z=(x-1)^2+2(y+3)^2+5\) for \(c=5\)? For \(c=3\)?

\(c=5\): the single point \((1,-3)\) (the minimum). \(c=3\): empty (no points, since \(z\ge5\)).

6. Contours of a plane \(z=3x-y\) look like…?

Parallel straight lines \(y=3x-c\), equally spaced for equally spaced \(c\).

7. For \(z=x^2+y^2\), why do contours at \(z=1,2,3,4\) get closer together?

Radii are \(\sqrt z=1,1.41,1.73,2\). The gaps shrink because the bowl steepens as you go out.

8. \(y=k\) cross-section of \(z=x^2-y^2\)?

\(z=x^2-k^2\): an up-parabola in the \(xz\)-plane with vertex \((0,-k^2)\).

9. Name: \(x^2+y^2-z^2=-1\).

Hyperboloid of two sheets about the \(z\)-axis. Nothing for \(|z|\lt1\).

10. Name: \(y=x^2+z^2\).

Circular paraboloid opening along the \(+y\) axis (\(y\) is the linear variable).

11. \(z=\frac{6y}{x^2+y^2}\). What is every contour \(z=c\ne0\)?

\(x^2+\left(y-\frac3c\right)^2=\frac9{c^2}\): circles centred \((0,3/c)\) with radius \(3/|c|\), all through the excluded origin.

12. \((x+2)(y-1)=-3\): asymptotes and which quadrants?

Asymptotes \(x=-2\), \(y=1\). Since the constant is negative, the branches are top-left and bottom-right of \((-2,1)\).

13. You get \(y\)-intercept equation \((y-1)^2=0\). What do you write and draw?

“The curve touches the \(y\)-axis at \((0,1)\)”, drawn tangent, not crossing.

14. Why is \(z=-5\) not an intercept of the 2025 S2 cross-section?

\(z=\sqrt{x+y^2}-3\ge-3\). Squaring introduced the lower half, which isn't on the surface.

STANDARD · 5 marks Q2. For \(x^2+y^2-z^2=1\): (a) describe the contours \(z=k\) and explain why the surface is “one sheet”; (b) describe the traces \(x=0\) and \(x=1\).

Solution
(a) x² + y² = 1 + k²: a circle of radius √(1+k²) for EVERY k (never empty) — the slices never disappear, so the surface is one connected tube (smallest at z = 0). (b) x = 0: y² − z² = 1, a hyperbola in the yz-plane opening along ±y. x = 1: y² − z² = 0 ⇒ two lines z = ±y (degenerate: the plane is tangent to the waist).

EXAM-LEVEL · 4 marks Q3. Match each function with a description of its graph, or enter 0: \(g_1=e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\), \(g_2=\sin x\), \(g_3=x^2+y^2\), \(g_4=-(x^2+y^2)\). Graphs: (1) a single smooth bump with maximum 1, flattening to 0 far away; (2) an upward bowl; (3) corrugated ridges parallel to the \(y\)-axis.

Solution
g₁ → 1 (max e⁰ = 1 at the origin, → 0 as r → ∞) g₂ → 3 (independent of y: the curve sin x extruded along y) g₃ → 2 (bowl) g₄ → 0 (a DOWNWARD bowl: nothing matches — check the sign!)

STRETCH · 5 marks Q4. Identify \(x-(y-2)^2-(z+1)^2=0\): name, vertex and axis. Describe the trace in the plane \(x=4\) and in the plane \(z=-1\).

Solution
x = (y−2)² + (z+1)²: circular paraboloid, vertex (0, 2, −1), opening along +x (axis the line y = 2, z = −1). x = 4: (y−2)² + (z+1)² = 4 — circle of radius 2 centred (y,z) = (2, −1). z = −1: x = (y−2)² — parabola in the xy-plane, vertex (0, 2), opening in +x.

Question 1 — Planes, distance & intersection [10 marks]

A plane is given by \(P=(0,1,-1)+\lambda(1,0,2)+\mu(0,2,-4)\), where \(\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R\).
4 marks(i) Determine a normal vector to \(P\).
Worked solution
A plane in vector form \(A+\lambda\mathbf u+\mu\mathbf v\) has two in-plane directions. A normal is perpendicular to both, so take the cross product \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\).
Point on the plane \(A=(0,1,-1)\); directions \(\mathbf u=(1,0,2)\), \(\mathbf v=(0,2,-4)\). $$\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\1&0&2\\0&2&-4\end{vmatrix} =\mathbf i(0-4)-\mathbf j(-4-0)+\mathbf k(2-0)=(-4,\,4,\,2)=2(-2,\,2,\,1).$$ Check: \((-2,2,1)\cdot(1,0,2)=0\) ✓ and \((-2,2,1)\cdot(0,2,-4)=0\) ✓.
\(\mathbf n=(-2,\,2,\,1)\) (any non-zero multiple is fine).
Show the dot-product check — two quick lines that prove your normal is right and protect you from a sign slip in the middle component of the cross product.
4 marks(ii) Let \(X\) be a point on the line $$L:\quad -x=\frac{y+1}{3}=2-z.$$ Write down an expression for the distance from the point \(X\) to the plane \(P\).
Worked solution
Distance from a point to a plane = length of the projection of \(\overrightarrow{AX}\) onto the unit normal: \(d=\dfrac{|\overrightarrow{AX}\cdot\mathbf n|}{\|\mathbf n\|}\). First parametrise the line by setting the common ratio equal to \(\lambda\).
Parametrise \(L\): set \(-x=\dfrac{y+1}{3}=2-z=\lambda\): $$X=(x,y,z)=(-\lambda,\;-1+3\lambda,\;2-\lambda),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb R.$$ Vector from the plane point: \(\overrightarrow{AX}=X-A=(-\lambda,\;3\lambda-2,\;3-\lambda)\).
Project onto the normal (\(\|\mathbf n\|=\sqrt{4+4+1}=3\)): $$d=\frac{|\overrightarrow{AX}\cdot\mathbf n|}{\|\mathbf n\|}=\frac{|-2(-\lambda)+2(3\lambda-2)+1(3-\lambda)|}{3}=\frac{|2\lambda+6\lambda-4+3-\lambda|}{3}.$$
\(d(\lambda)=\dfrac{|7\lambda-1|}{3}\), where \(X=(-\lambda,-1+3\lambda,2-\lambda)\).
The official solution first writes the general form \(d=\dfrac{|-2a+2b+c-1|}{3}\) for \(X=(a,b,c)\) (i.e. the plane is \(-2x+2y+z=1\)), then substitutes the line. Either route is fine — the marks are for (1) parametrising the line, (2) the projection formula, (3) \(\|\mathbf n\|=3\), (4) simplifying.
Forgetting to divide by \(\|\mathbf n\|\) (using \(\mathbf n\) instead of the unit normal) gives \(|7\lambda-1|\) — three times too big. And keep the absolute value: distance is never negative.
2 marks(iii) Using this, determine the point of intersection between the line from (ii) and the plane \(P\).
Worked solution
"Using this" ⇒ the intersection point is the point of the line at distance zero from the plane.
$$d=0\iff 7\lambda-1=0\iff\lambda=\tfrac17.$$ $$x=-\tfrac17,\qquad y=-1+\tfrac37=-\tfrac47,\qquad z=2-\tfrac17=\tfrac{13}7.$$ Check in \(-2x+2y+z=1\): \(\tfrac27-\tfrac87+\tfrac{13}7=\tfrac77=1\) ✓.
Intersection point \(\left(-\tfrac17,\,-\tfrac47,\,\tfrac{13}7\right)\).

Question 2 — Conic section (hyperbola) [10 marks]

Let a conic section be given by $$4x^2-y^2+4x=6y-4.$$
5 marks(i) Determine the type of conic section.
Worked solution
Group \(x\)-terms and \(y\)-terms, complete the square in each, then divide to get "\(=1\)". Opposite signs on the squares ⇒ hyperbola; same signs ⇒ ellipse; one square only ⇒ parabola.
$$4x^2+4x-y^2-6y=-4$$ $$4\big[(x+\tfrac12)^2-\tfrac14\big]-\big[(y+3)^2-9\big]=-4$$ $$4(x+\tfrac12)^2-1-(y+3)^2+9=-4\;\Rightarrow\;4(x+\tfrac12)^2-(y+3)^2=-12.$$ Divide by \(-12\): $$\frac{(y+3)^2}{12}-\frac{(x+\tfrac12)^2}{3}=1,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{(y+3)^2}{(2\sqrt3)^2}-\frac{(x+\tfrac12)^2}{(\sqrt3)^2}=1.$$
A hyperbola centred at \(\left(-\tfrac12,-3\right)\), opening up/down (the \(y\)-term is positive), \(a=2\sqrt3\) (vertical), \(b=\sqrt3\).
When you divide by a negative number the signs of both squares flip. Here that is what makes the hyperbola open vertically, not horizontally. Students who don't flip sketch it sideways.
5 marks(ii) Sketch the conic section. Be sure to identify the center, foci, or asymptotes.
Worked solution
For \(\dfrac{(y-k)^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{b^2}=1\): vertices \((h,k\pm a)\), asymptotes \(y-k=\pm\dfrac ab(x-h)\), foci \((h,k\pm c)\) with \(c^2=a^2+b^2\).
Centre \((-\tfrac12,-3)\). Vertices \((-\tfrac12,\,-3\pm2\sqrt3)\approx(-0.5,\,0.46)\) and \((-0.5,\,-6.46)\).
Asymptotes — for large \(|x|,|y|\) the "1" is negligible: \(\dfrac{(y+3)^2}{12}\approx\dfrac{(x+\frac12)^2}{3}\Rightarrow y+3=\pm2(x+\tfrac12)\): $$y=2x-2\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=-2x-4.$$ Foci: \(c^2=12+3=15\Rightarrow\) foci \(\left(-\tfrac12,\,-3\pm\sqrt{15}\right)\approx(-0.5,\,0.87),\ (-0.5,\,-6.87)\).
Intercepts (nice for the sketch): \(x=0\Rightarrow y^2+6y-4=0\Rightarrow y=-3\pm\sqrt{13}\approx0.61,\,-6.61\); \(y=0\Rightarrow4x^2+4x+4=0\), no real roots, so no \(x\)-intercepts.
Two branches: an upper "U" with lowest point at \((-\tfrac12,-3+2\sqrt3)\approx(-0.5,0.46)\) crossing the \(y\)-axis at \(\approx0.61\), and a lower "∩" with highest point at \((-\tfrac12,-3-2\sqrt3)\approx(-0.5,-6.46)\). Both branches hug the dashed asymptotes \(y=2x-2\) and \(y=-2x-4\), which cross at the centre \((-\tfrac12,-3)\).
Hyperbola, centre \((-\tfrac12,-3)\), vertices \((-\tfrac12,-3\pm2\sqrt3)\), foci \((-\tfrac12,-3\pm\sqrt{15})\), asymptotes \(y=2x-2,\ y=-2x-4\).
The question says "center, foci, or asymptotes" — the official solution labels the centre, vertices and both asymptotes. Labelling foci as well costs 20 seconds and removes any doubt.

Question 3 — Critical points [10 marks]

Let \(h(x,y)=20+x^2-y^2+xy^2+y^3.\)
5 marks(i) Find all critical points of \(h(x,y)\).
Worked solution
Critical points: \(\nabla h=\mathbf 0\) (or undefined). Solve the easier equation for one variable and substitute.
$$\nabla h=(h_x,h_y)=\left(2x+y^2,\;-2y+2xy+3y^2\right),$$ defined for all \((x,y)\), so critical points need \(\nabla h=\mathbf 0\): $$\text{(1) } 2x+y^2=0,\qquad \text{(2) } -2y+2xy+3y^2=0.$$ From (1): \(x=-\tfrac{y^2}{2}\). Into (2): $$-2y+2\left(-\tfrac{y^2}2\right)y+3y^2=-2y-y^3+3y^2=-y(y^2-3y+2)=-y(y-1)(y-2)=0.$$ So \(y=0,1,2\) giving \(x=0,-\tfrac12,-2\).
Critical points: \((0,0),\ \left(-\tfrac12,1\right),\ (-2,2)\).
State "∇h is defined everywhere" — the official solution does, because the definition of critical point includes points where the gradient doesn't exist.
5 marks(ii) Classify each critical point as a local minimum, local maximum, or saddle point.
Worked solution
Second-derivative test (formula sheet): \(D=h_{xx}h_{yy}-h_{xy}^2\). \(D\lt0\) saddle; \(D\gt0\) and \(h_{xx}\gt0\) min; \(D\gt0\) and \(h_{xx}\lt0\) max; \(D=0\) inconclusive.
$$h_{xx}=2,\quad h_{xy}=2y,\quad h_{yy}=-2+2x+6y,\qquad D=\begin{vmatrix}2&2y\\2y&-2+2x+6y\end{vmatrix}.$$
Point\(D\)\(h_{xx}\)Type
\((0,0)\)\(2(-2)-0=-4\lt0\)—saddle
\((-\tfrac12,1)\)\(2(3)-2^2=2\gt0\)\(2\gt0\)local min
\((-2,2)\)\(2(6)-4^2=-4\lt0\)—saddle
\((0,0)\) saddle; \(\left(-\tfrac12,1\right)\) local minimum (value \(\tfrac{79}4\)); \((-2,2)\) saddle.
At \((-2,2)\): \(h_{yy}=-2+2(-2)+6(2)=6\) — a sign slip on the \(2x\) term turns this into \(D\gt0\) and a wrong "min". Substitute carefully, one term at a time.

Question 4 — From velocity to position [10 marks]

Consider a particle moving through space with velocity \(\mathbf v(t)=\cos(t)\,\mathbf i-\sin(t)\,\mathbf j+t\,\mathbf k.\)
3 marks(i) Determine its acceleration vector.
Worked solution
\(\mathbf a=\dfrac{d\mathbf v}{dt}\), component by component.
\(\mathbf a(t)=-\sin(t)\,\mathbf i-\cos(t)\,\mathbf j+\mathbf k\).
7 marks(ii) Determine the position vector, supposing the particle starts at position \((3,-2,3)\) at time \(t=0\).
Worked solution
Integrate each component of \(\mathbf v\), with a separate constant per component, then fix the constants from \(\mathbf r(0)\).
$$x(t)=\int\cos t\,dt=\sin t+a,\qquad x(0)=0+a=3\Rightarrow a=3.$$ $$y(t)=\int-\sin t\,dt=\cos t+b,\qquad y(0)=1+b=-2\Rightarrow b=-3.$$ $$z(t)=\int t\,dt=\tfrac{t^2}2+c,\qquad z(0)=c=3.$$
\(\mathbf r(t)=\big(\sin t+3\big)\mathbf i+\big(\cos t-3\big)\mathbf j+\left(\tfrac{t^2}2+3\right)\mathbf k.\)
The classic slip: \(y(0)=\cos 0+b=1+b\), not \(0+b\). Getting \(b=-2\) here is the most common lost mark. Always verify \(\mathbf r(0)=(3,-2,3)\) at the end.

Question 5 — Homogeneous ODE via \(y=xu\) [10 marks]

Consider the ODE $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{x+y}{x-y},$$ and show that it becomes separable using the substitution \(y(x)=xu(x)\). (You do not need to solve the ODE.)
Worked solution
Right side depends only on \(y/x\) (a "homogeneous" ODE) ⇒ \(y=xu\). Product rule gives \(y'=u+xu'\); the \(x\)'s cancel on the right; isolate \(u'\) as \(f(x)\,g(u)\).
Differentiate the substitution (product rule): $$y=xu\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dy}{dx}=u+x\frac{du}{dx}.$$ Substitute (assume \(x\ne0\)): $$u+x\frac{du}{dx}=\frac{x+xu}{x-xu}=\frac{x(1+u)}{x(1-u)}=\frac{1+u}{1-u}.$$ Isolate \(u'\): $$x\frac{du}{dx}=\frac{1+u}{1-u}-u=\frac{1+u-u(1-u)}{1-u}=\frac{1+u^2}{1-u}.$$ $$\frac{du}{dx}=\underbrace{\frac1x}_{f(x)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1+u^2}{1-u}}_{g(u)}.$$ This is of the form \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=f(x)g(u)\), hence separable: $$\int\frac{1-u}{1+u^2}\,du=\int\frac1x\,dx.$$
With \(y=xu\): \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=\dfrac1x\cdot\dfrac{1+u^2}{1-u}\) — a product of a function of \(x\) and a function of \(u\), so the ODE is separable.
In a "show that" question the final sentence earns the mark: name the form \(u'=f(x)g(u)\) and write the words "which is separable". State the assumption \(x\ne0\) (and \(u\ne1\), i.e. \(y\ne x\)) as the official solution does.
Writing \(y'=u'\) (forgetting the product rule) — the whole question collapses. \(y=xu\) is a product; its derivative has two terms.

Question 6 — Arc length & rate of change along a path [10 marks]

Consider a particle moving in the plane along the curve \(\mathbf r(t)=\big(R\cos(\omega t),\,R\sin(\omega t)\big)\) for some constants \(R,\omega\gt0\).
5 marks(i) Determine the distance the particle travels for \(t\in[0,2\pi]\).
Worked solution
Distance travelled = arc length \(=\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\).
$$\mathbf r'(t)=\big(-R\omega\sin(\omega t),\;R\omega\cos(\omega t)\big),$$ $$\|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt{R^2\omega^2\big(\sin^2(\omega t)+\cos^2(\omega t)\big)}=R\omega\quad(\text{since }R,\omega\gt0).$$ $$\text{distance}=\int_0^{2\pi}R\omega\,dt=2\pi R\omega.$$
Distance \(=2\pi R\omega\).
This is not the circumference \(2\pi R\). With angular speed \(\omega\) the particle goes round \(\omega\) times in \(t\in[0,2\pi]\), so it travels \(2\pi R\omega\). Also note \(\sqrt{R^2\omega^2}=R\omega\) only because \(R,\omega\gt0\) — say so.
5 marks(ii) Suppose the plane has a voltage given by \(V(x,y)=xy+x^2+y^3+3.\) Determine the change in voltage the particle experiences at time \(t\).
Worked solution
"Change experienced at time \(t\)" = rate \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}\) along the path ⇒ chain rule (formula sheet): \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}=V_x\dfrac{dx}{dt}+V_y\dfrac{dy}{dt}\).
\(V_x=y+2x,\quad V_y=x+3y^2;\quad \dfrac{dx}{dt}=-R\omega\sin(\omega t),\quad\dfrac{dy}{dt}=R\omega\cos(\omega t).\) $$\frac{dV}{dt}=\big(R\sin\omega t+2R\cos\omega t\big)(-R\omega\sin\omega t)+\big(R\cos\omega t+3R^2\sin^2\omega t\big)(R\omega\cos\omega t)$$ $$=-R^2\omega\sin^2\omega t-2R^2\omega\sin\omega t\cos\omega t+R^2\omega\cos^2\omega t+3R^3\omega\sin^2\omega t\cos\omega t.$$ Using \(\cos^2-\sin^2=\cos2\theta\) and \(2\sin\cos=\sin2\theta\):
\(\dfrac{dV}{dt}=R^2\omega\cos(2\omega t)-R^2\omega\sin(2\omega t)+3R^3\omega\sin^2(\omega t)\cos(\omega t).\)
Draw the tree diagram (\(V\to x,y\to t\)) as the official solution does — it shows the marker you know which derivatives multiply. The double-angle tidy-up is optional; the unsimplified line is already a full-mark answer.

Question 7 — Second-order inhomogeneous ODE [10 marks]

Find the general solution \(y(x)\) to the ODE $$4y''-4y'+5y=e^{3x}.$$
Worked solution
Constant-coefficient linear ODE: \(y=y_H+y_P\). \(y_H\) from the characteristic equation; \(y_P\) by undetermined coefficients — forcing \(e^{3x}\) ⇒ guess \(ae^{3x}\) (3 is not a root, so no extra \(x\)).
Homogeneous part. Characteristic equation \(4r^2-4r+5=0\): $$r=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{16-80}}{8}=\frac{4\pm8i}{8}=\tfrac12\pm i.$$ Complex roots \(\alpha\pm\beta i\) with \(\alpha=\tfrac12,\ \beta=1\): $$y_H=e^{x/2}\big(A\sin x+B\cos x\big),\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R.$$ Particular part. Try \(y_P=ae^{3x}\), \(y_P'=3ae^{3x}\), \(y_P''=9ae^{3x}\): $$4(9a)-4(3a)+5a=36a-12a+5a=29a=1\;\Rightarrow\;a=\tfrac1{29}.$$
\(y(x)=e^{x/2}\big(A\sin x+B\cos x\big)+\dfrac{1}{29}e^{3x},\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R.\)
Don't forget the leading 4: dividing the discriminant gives \(\sqrt{-64}=8i\), so \(\beta=\tfrac88=1\), not 2. And the "general solution" must contain two arbitrary constants — write "\(A,B\in\mathbb R\)".

Question 8 — Lagrange multipliers [10 marks]

Find the maximum and minimum values of the function \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^3\) subject to the constraint \(x^4+y^6-2=0\).
Worked solution
Extremise \(f\) on a curve \(g=0\) ⇒ Lagrange: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) plus the constraint. Factor instead of dividing, so the zero cases aren't lost. The constraint curve is closed and bounded, so a max and min exist.
\(\nabla f=(2x,\,3y^2)\), \(\nabla g=(4x^3,\,6y^5)\): $$\text{(1) }2x=4\lambda x^3,\qquad\text{(2) }3y^2=6\lambda y^5,\qquad\text{(3) }x^4+y^6=2.$$ (1) \(\Rightarrow 2x(1-2\lambda x^2)=0\Rightarrow x=0\ \text{or}\ 2\lambda x^2=1\).
(2) \(\Rightarrow 3y^2(1-2\lambda y^3)=0\Rightarrow y=0\ \text{or}\ 2\lambda y^3=1\).
Case \(x=0\): (3) gives \(y^6=2\Rightarrow y^3=\pm\sqrt2\). Then \(f=\pm\sqrt2\).
Case \(y=0\): (3) gives \(x^4=2\Rightarrow x^2=\sqrt2\). Then \(f=\sqrt2\).
Case \(x,y\ne0\): \(2\lambda x^2=1=2\lambda y^3\Rightarrow x^2=y^3\) (so \(y\gt0\)). Into (3): \(y^6+y^6=2\Rightarrow y^6=1\Rightarrow y=1\) (discard \(y=-1\) since \(y^3=x^2\ge0\)), \(x=\pm1\). Then \(f=1+1=2\).
Point(s)\(f\)
\((0,\,2^{1/6})\)\(\sqrt2\)
\((0,\,-2^{1/6})\)\(-\sqrt2\)
\((\pm2^{1/4},\,0)\)\(\sqrt2\)
\((\pm1,\,1)\)\(2\)
Maximum value \(2\) (at \((\pm1,1)\)); minimum value \(-\sqrt2\) (at \((0,-2^{1/6})\)).
Dividing (1) by \(x\) or (2) by \(y^2\) silently throws away the \(x=0\) case — and that is exactly where the minimum lives. Factor, list every case, then compare values.
Say why extrema exist: "\(g=0\) is a closed, bounded curve and \(f\) is continuous, so \(f\) attains a max and a min, which must be among the Lagrange points."

Question 9 — Conservative field & line integral [10 marks]

Let a vector field in \(\mathbb R^3\) be defined by \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=(2xy^2+3x^2,\;2yx^2,\;1).\)
Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\), where \(C\) is the curve \(\big(t^2-1,\ \sin^3(\tfrac{\pi t}{2}),\ 2t\big)\) for \(0\le t\le1\).
Worked solution
An ugly path (\(\sin^3\)) is the examiner's signal: don't parametrise — check \(\mathbf F\) is conservative, find a potential \(f\), and use the Fundamental Theorem: \(\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\).
Conservative check (\(\mathbf F=(P,Q,R)\)): $$P_y=4xy=Q_x,\qquad P_z=0=R_x,\qquad Q_z=0=R_y\ \checkmark$$ Potential: want \(f_x=2xy^2+3x^2\), \(f_y=2yx^2\), \(f_z=1\). $$f=\int(2xy^2+3x^2)\,dx=x^2y^2+x^3+g(y,z).$$ \(f_y=2x^2y+g_y=2x^2y\Rightarrow g_y=0\Rightarrow g=h(z)\); \(f_z=h'(z)=1\Rightarrow h=z+C\). $$f(x,y,z)=x^2y^2+x^3+z.$$ Endpoints: \(\mathbf r(0)=(-1,0,0)\), \(\mathbf r(1)=\big(0,\sin^3(\tfrac\pi2),2\big)=(0,1,2)\). $$\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=f(0,1,2)-f(-1,0,0)=(0+0+2)-(0-1+0)=2+1=3.$$
\(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=3.\)
The official solution simply asserts "the vector field is conservative". Show the three cross-partial checks — it's the justification that licenses using the potential.
\(f(-1,0,0)=(-1)^3=-1\), so subtracting it adds 1. Sign errors at the start point are the usual way to get 1 instead of 3.

Question 10 — Equilibrium solutions & stability [10 marks]

Consider the ODE \(\;x\dot y+x-y=y^2x\dot y+xy-1.\)
5 marks(i) Determine all equilibrium solutions to the ODE.
Worked solution
Equilibrium solution = constant solution \(y=c\). Put \(y'=0\) and \(y=c\); the identity must hold for all \(x\).
With \(y=c\), \(y'=0\): $$x-c=xc-1\iff (x+1)-c(x+1)=0\iff(1-c)(x+1)=0.$$ For this to hold for every \(x\) we need \(c=1\). (The factor \(x+1=0\) is a single \(x\)-value, not a solution \(y=\text{const}\).)
The only equilibrium solution is \(y=1\).
5 marks(ii) Determine if each equilibrium solution is stable or not. Justify your answer.
Worked solution
Write the ODE as \(y'=F(x,y)\) and study the sign of \(y'\) just above and just below the equilibrium (for large \(x\), here \(x\gt0\)). Both sides move toward it ⇒ stable; both away ⇒ unstable; one toward, one away ⇒ semi-stable.
Solve for \(y'\): $$xy'-y^2xy'=xy-1-x+y\;\Rightarrow\;x(1-y^2)\,y'=(x+1)(y-1)$$ $$y'=\frac{(x+1)(y-1)}{x(1-y^2)},\qquad x\ne0,\ y\ne\pm1.$$ Sign analysis for \(x\gt0\):
Region\(x+1\)\(y-1\)\(x\)\(1-y^2\)\(y'\)Motion
\(y\gt1\)+++−\(\lt0\)down, toward \(y=1\)
\(-1\lt y\lt1\)+−++\(\lt0\)down, away from \(y=1\)
\(y=1\) is semi-stable (official answer): solutions starting just above approach it, solutions starting just below move away — so it is not stable.
The official solution sketches the phase picture: arrows pointing down on both sides of the line \(y=1\). A one-line table of signs + that sketch is the full justification.
Deeper point (beyond the marking): the factor \(y-1\) actually cancels, \(y'=-\dfrac{x+1}{x(1+y)}\), and the coefficient of \(y'\) in the original ODE vanishes on \(y=1\) — the ODE is singular there. Nearby solutions don't creep up to \(y=1\) asymptotically; they reach it with slope \(\approx-\tfrac12\). Either way it is not stable. On the day, give the sign-analysis answer the marker expects (semi-stable / not stable) and, if you like, a one-line remark.

Question 1 — Equilibria & stability [10 marks]

Find all equilibrium solutions of the following ODE: $$\frac{dy}{dt}=e^{t+y}(3-y)(y+4)^2(t-2).$$ Decide which of these equilibrium solutions are stable. Justify your answer.
Worked solution
Equilibria: values of \(y\) making \(\dot y=0\) for all \(t\). Stability is about \(t\to\infty\), so do the sign analysis for large \(t\) (here \(t\gt2\)).
Equilibria [2 marks]. \(e^{t+y}\gt0\) always, and \(t-2\) is not identically zero, so \(\dot y\equiv0\iff(3-y)(y+4)^2=0\): $$y=3\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=-4.$$ Signs of the factors [3 marks]. For \(t\gt2\): \(t-2\gt0\); \(e^{t+y}\gt0\); \((y+4)^2\gt0\) for \(y\ne-4\); \(3-y\gt0\iff y\lt3\).
Direction field for \(t\gt2\) [3 marks]:
Region\(\dot y\)Motion
\(y\gt3\)\(\lt0\)decreases toward 3
\(-4\lt y\lt3\)\(\gt0\)increases toward 3, away from −4
\(y\lt-4\)\(\gt0\)increases toward −4
\(y=3\) is stable [1 mark]. \(y=-4\) is not stable (semi-stable: attracts from below, repels above) [1 mark].
Marking scheme: 2 + 3 (factor signs "or equivalent working") + 3 (direction table) + 1 + 1. Half the marks are for the justification, not the labels.
The \((t-2)\) factor reverses every arrow for \(t\lt2\). If you test signs at, say, \(t=0\) you'll get the stabilities backwards. Always analyse the long-run region \(t\gt2\) and say so. And a squared factor \((y+4)^2\) never changes sign — that's what makes −4 semi-stable.

Question 2 — Linear first-order ODE [10 marks]

Find the general solution of the following ODE, assuming \(0\lt x\lt\frac\pi2\): $$\sin(x)\cos^2(x)\,y'+\cos^3(x)\,y=1.$$
Worked solution
Linear in \(y\) and \(y'\) ⇒ divide by the coefficient of \(y'\) to get \(y'+p(x)y=q(x)\), then integrating factor \(I=e^{\int p\,dx}\).
Domain note [1 mark]. On \(0\lt x\lt\frac\pi2\): \(\sin x\gt0,\ \cos x\gt0\), so we may divide. $$y'+\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\,y=\frac{1}{\sin x\cos^2x}.$$ Integrating factor [3 marks]: $$I(x)=e^{\int\cot x\,dx}=e^{\ln|\sin x|}=|\sin x|=\sin x\quad(\text{as }\sin x\gt0).$$ Multiply through [2 marks]: $$\frac{d}{dx}\big[\sin x\;y\big]=\frac{\sin x}{\sin x\cos^2x}=\frac1{\cos^2x}=\sec^2x.$$ Integrate [2 marks]: \(\sin x\;y=\tan x+C\).
\(y=\dfrac{\tan x+C}{\sin x}=\dfrac1{\cos x}+\dfrac{C}{\sin x}=\sec x+C\csc x,\qquad C\in\mathbb R\) [2 marks].
The 1 mark for "\(\sin x,\cos x\gt0\)" is exactly why the question gives the interval: it lets you drop the absolute value \(|\sin x|\to\sin x\). Always say it.
Forgetting to divide by \(\sin x\cos^2x\) first and using \(p=\cos^3x\) as the "p" in \(e^{\int p}\) — the integrating factor must be built from the standard form.

Question 3 — Reduction of order [10 marks]

For some positive integer \(n\), the function \(y=x^n\) is a solution of the ODE $$2x^2y''-3xy'-3y=0.$$ Find the value of \(n\). Use the obtained solution and the method of reduction of order to find the general solution of the ODE, for \(x\gt0\).
Worked solution
Step 1: substitute the trial \(x^n\) to get a polynomial in \(n\). Step 2: reduction of order — set \(y=u\,y_1\); \(u\) satisfies \(u''+u'\left(p+\dfrac{2y_1'}{y_1}\right)=0\), which is first-order in \(v=u'\).
Find \(n\) [2 + 1 marks]. \(y=x^n,\ y'=nx^{n-1},\ y''=n(n-1)x^{n-2}\): $$\big(2n(n-1)-3n-3\big)x^n=0\;\Rightarrow\;2n^2-5n-3=(2n+1)(n-3)=0.$$ \(n\) is a positive integer ⇒ \(n=3\), \(y_1=x^3\).
Standard form [1 mark]: \(y''-\dfrac{3}{2x}y'-\dfrac{3}{2x^2}y=0\), so \(p(x)=-\dfrac3{2x}\).
Equation for \(u\) [1 + 1 marks] (with \(y=u\,x^3\), \(\;2y_1'/y_1=6/x\)): $$u''+u'\left(-\frac3{2x}+\frac6x\right)=u''+\frac{9}{2x}u'=0.$$ Solve for \(v=u'\) [1 + 1 marks]. \(v=0\) is a solution; if \(v\ne0\): $$\frac{v'}{v}=-\frac9{2x}\;\Rightarrow\;\ln|v|=-\tfrac92\ln x+c\;\Rightarrow\;v=Ax^{-9/2}.$$ Integrate [1 mark]: \(u=\int Ax^{-9/2}dx=-\tfrac27Ax^{-7/2}+B=\tilde A\,x^{-7/2}+B\).
\(y=u\,x^3=\tilde A\,x^{-1/2}+B\,x^3,\qquad \tilde A,B\in\mathbb R\) [1 mark].
Sanity check: the other root \(n=-\tfrac12\) of \(2n^2-5n-3=0\) is exactly the second solution you obtained — Euler-type ODEs always work this way. Great way to verify under exam pressure.
The \(u\)-equation uses \(p\) from the standard form (leading coefficient 1). Using \(-3x\) instead of \(-\tfrac3{2x}\) gives nonsense.

Question 4 — Intersection of two planes [10 marks]

Consider the planes \(2x+3y+4z=0\) and \(x-z=2\). Their intersection is a line \(L\).
7 marks(i) Find symmetric equations of \(L\).
Worked solution
Direction of \(L\) is perpendicular to both normals ⇒ \(\mathbf v=\mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2\). Then find any one point on both planes (set one coordinate to 0).
Normals [1 + 1]: \(\mathbf n_1=(2,3,4)\), \(\mathbf n_2=(1,0,-1)\).
Direction [1 + 1]: $$\mathbf v=\mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\2&3&4\\1&0&-1\end{vmatrix}=-3\mathbf i+6\mathbf j-3\mathbf k\;\parallel\;(-1,2,-1).$$ Point [1]: set \(z=0\): \(x=2\), then \(4+3y=0\Rightarrow y=-\tfrac43\). Point \(\left(2,-\tfrac43,0\right)\). Check: \(4-4+0=0\) ✓, \(2-0=2\) ✓.
Vector equation [1]: \(\mathbf r=\left(2,-\tfrac43,0\right)+\lambda(-1,2,-1)\), i.e. \(x=2-\lambda,\ y=-\tfrac43+2\lambda,\ z=-\lambda\).
Symmetric form [1] — solve each for \(\lambda\):
\(L:\quad 2-x=\dfrac{y+\frac43}{2}=-z\qquad\Big(\text{equivalently } \dfrac{x-2}{-1}=\dfrac{3y+4}{6}=\dfrac{z}{-1}\Big).\)
An alternative scheme on the same page solves the system by elimination: \(4\times(x-z=2)\) added to the first plane gives \(6x+3y=8\), leading to \(x=z+2=\dfrac{y-\frac83}{-2}\) — the same line. Either method earns full marks.
3 marks(ii) For what value of \(a\) is \(L\) parallel to the plane \(ax+4y-z=19\)?
Worked solution
A line is parallel to a plane ⇔ its direction is perpendicular to the plane's normal: \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v=0\).
$$\mathbf n=(a,4,-1)\ [1],\qquad \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v=(a,4,-1)\cdot(-1,2,-1)=-a+8+1=0\ [1].$$
\(a=9\) [1].
Bonus check: the point \((2,-\tfrac43,0)\) gives \(18-\tfrac{16}3\ne19\), so \(L\) is parallel to the plane and not lying inside it.

Question 5 — Tangent plane [10 marks]

(i) (7 marks) Find an equation of the plane tangent to the surface \(z=x^2+y^2+e^{xy}\) at the point \((3,2)\).
(ii) (3 marks) Find a normal vector to this plane.
7 marks(i) Tangent plane.
Worked solution
Tangent plane to \(z=f(x,y)\) at \((a,b)\) = the linear approximation on the formula sheet: \(z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)\).
$$f_x=2x+ye^{xy}\ [1],\qquad f_x(3,2)=6+2e^6\ [1]$$ $$f_y=2y+xe^{xy}\ [1],\qquad f_y(3,2)=4+3e^6\ [1]$$ $$f(3,2)=9+4+e^6=13+e^6\ [1]$$
\(z=13+e^6+(6+2e^6)(x-3)+(4+3e^6)(y-2)\) [1 + 1].
Leave \(e^6\) exact — no calculator, and the marker wants exact values.
3 marks(ii) Normal vector.
Worked solution
Rewrite the plane as \(ax+by+cz=d\); the normal is \((a,b,c)\).
$$(6+2e^6)x+(4+3e^6)y-z=d,\qquad d=3(6+2e^6)+2(4+3e^6)-(13+e^6)=13+11e^6\ [2].$$
\(\mathbf n=\big(6+2e^6,\;4+3e^6,\;-1\big)\) [1] (i.e. \((f_x,f_y,-1)\)).
The normal has a −1 in the \(z\)-slot (from moving \(z\) to the left side). Writing \((f_x,f_y,1)\) is a sign error.

Question 6 — Critical points [10 marks]

Let \(g(x,y)=x^3+y^2-6xy+6x+3y.\)
6 marks(i) Find ALL critical points of \(g(x,y)\).
Worked solution
Set \(\nabla g=\mathbf 0\). \(g_y\) is linear in \(y\) ⇒ solve it for \(y\), substitute into \(g_x=0\).
$$\nabla g=\big(3x^2-6y+6\ [1],\ \ 2y-6x+3\ [1]\big)=(0,0)\ [1].$$ \(g_y=0\Rightarrow y=3x-\tfrac32\). Into \(g_x=0\): $$3x^2-6\left(3x-\tfrac32\right)+6=3x^2-18x+15=0\;\Rightarrow\;x^2-6x+5=(x-1)(x-5)=0\ [1].$$
\(x=1\Rightarrow y=\tfrac32\): \(\left(1,\tfrac32\right)\) [1]; \(\;x=5\Rightarrow y=\tfrac{27}2\): \(\left(5,\tfrac{27}2\right)\) [1].
4 marks(ii) Classify ONE critical point from part (i) as a local minimum, local maximum, or saddle point.
Worked solution
Hessian determinant \(D=g_{xx}g_{yy}-g_{xy}^2\) (formula sheet).
$$D=\begin{vmatrix}g_{xx}&g_{xy}\\g_{yx}&g_{yy}\end{vmatrix}\ [1]=\begin{vmatrix}6x&-6\\-6&2\end{vmatrix}\ [1]=12x-36\ [1].$$
  • At \(\left(1,\tfrac32\right)\): \(D=12-36=-24\lt0\) ⇒ saddle point.
  • At \(\left(5,\tfrac{27}2\right)\): \(D=60-36=24\gt0\) and \(g_{xx}=6(5)=30\gt0\) ⇒ local minimum.
Either one earns the final mark: \(\left(1,\tfrac32\right)\) saddle, or \(\left(5,\tfrac{27}2\right)\) local minimum.
The official solution writes "\(g_{xx}=60\)" — a slip (it copied \(12x\)); the correct value is \(g_{xx}=6x=30\). The conclusion is unchanged, but it shows how easily \(D\) and \(g_{xx}\) get mixed up. Compute them on separate lines.

Question 7 — Global extrema on the unit ball [10 marks]

Consider the function \(f(x,y,z)=x+yz.\) Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to find the global minimum and global maximum of \(f(x,y,z)\), for \((x,y,z)\) in the unit ball.
Hint: The unit ball is the set of points \((x,y,z)\) such that \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le1\). It is known that the unit ball is closed and bounded, and that its boundary consists of the points satisfying the constraint \(x^2+y^2+z^2=1\).
Worked solution
Closed bounded region ⇒ global extrema exist and occur at interior critical points or on the boundary. Check the interior first, then Lagrange on the boundary sphere.
Where extrema can be [1]: at critical points of \(f\) inside, or on the boundary.
Interior [1 + 1]: \(\nabla f=(1,z,y)\ne\mathbf 0\) (first component is 1), so no interior critical points — the extrema lie on \(x^2+y^2+z^2=1\).
Lagrange [1 + 2]: \(g=x^2+y^2+z^2-1\), \(\nabla g=(2x,2y,2z)\): $$\text{(1) }1=2\lambda x,\qquad\text{(2) }z=2\lambda y,\qquad\text{(3) }y=2\lambda z.$$ (1) forces \(\lambda\ne0\) and \(x\ne0\).
Case \(yz\ne0\) [1 + 1]: (2)/(3): \(\dfrac zy=\dfrac yz\Rightarrow z^2=y^2\Rightarrow z=\pm y\); then (2) gives \(\lambda=\pm\tfrac12\), and (1) gives \(x=\pm1\). But then \(y^2+z^2=1-x^2=0\), contradicting \(yz\ne0\). No points.
Case \(yz=0\): if \(y=0\), (2) gives \(z=0\); if \(z=0\), (3) gives \(y=0\). So \(y=z=0\), \(x=\pm1\).
Candidates \((\pm1,0,0)\) with \(f=\pm1\) [1].
Global maximum \(1\) at \((1,0,0)\); global minimum \(-1\) at \((-1,0,0)\) [1].
Independent check you can write in one line: \(|yz|\le\tfrac12(y^2+z^2)\le\tfrac12(1-x^2)\), so \(f\le x+\tfrac12(1-x^2)\le1\) on the ball, with equality at \(x=1\). Similarly \(f\ge-1\).
Skipping the interior check loses 2–3 marks even when the final answer is right. The marking scheme pays for "no interior critical points, so extrema are on the boundary" explicitly.

Question 8 — Ellipse: sketch, parametrise, tangent direction [10 marks]

Sketch the conic section given by $$4x^2-16x+y^2-2y+13=0.$$ Find a parametric representation of this curve. Identify a point on the curve for which the velocity vector (tangent to the curve) is parallel to the line \(y=-x\).
Worked solution
Complete the square → standard ellipse → parametrise with \(x=h+a\cos t,\ y=k+b\sin t\). "Tangent parallel to \(y=-x\)" ⇒ \(\mathbf r'(t)\) is a multiple of \((1,-1)\).
Standard form [2]: $$4(x^2-4x+4)+(y^2-2y+1)=-13+16+1=4\;\Rightarrow\;(x-2)^2+\frac{(y-1)^2}{4}=1.$$ Ellipse [1], centre \((2,1)\) [1], semi-axes \(a=1\) (horizontal), \(b=2\) (vertical) [1].
Vertical ellipse centred at \((2,1)\): leftmost/rightmost points \((1,1),(3,1)\); bottom/top \((2,-1),(2,3)\). Since the bottom point is below the \(x\)-axis, the ellipse crosses it where \((x-2)^2=\tfrac34\), i.e. \(x=2\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\). Sketch with those four extreme points labelled [1].
Parametrisation [1]: $$\mathbf r(t)=\big(2+\cos t,\;1+2\sin t\big),\qquad 0\le t\lt2\pi.$$ Tangent vector [1]: \(\mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\;2\cos t)\).
Parallel to \(y=-x\) ⇔ \(\mathbf r'(t)=(s,-s)\) for some \(s\ne0\) ⇔ \(-\sin t=-2\cos t\) ⇔ \(\tan t=2\) ⇒ \(t=\arctan2\) [1].
With \(\cos t=\tfrac1{\sqrt5},\ \sin t=\tfrac2{\sqrt5}\):
\(\mathbf r_0=\big(2+\cos(\arctan2),\,1+2\sin(\arctan2)\big)=\left(2+\tfrac1{\sqrt5},\;1+\tfrac4{\sqrt5}\right)\) [1]. (The other point is \(\left(2-\tfrac1{\sqrt5},\,1-\tfrac4{\sqrt5}\right)\) at \(t=\arctan2+\pi\).)
The official answer leaves it as \(\cos(\arctan2)\) — acceptable, but simplifying via a 1–2–\(\sqrt5\) right triangle looks sharper and lets you verify: \(\mathbf r'=\left(-\tfrac2{\sqrt5},\tfrac2{\sqrt5}\right)\parallel(1,-1)\) ✓.
"Velocity parallel to \(y=-x\)" is about the direction vector \((1,-1)\), not the point lying on \(y=-x\). And \(a=1\) goes with \(\cos\) in \(x\), \(b=2\) with \(\sin\) in \(y\) — swapping them gives a curve that isn't the ellipse.

Question 9 — A bird's flight [10 marks]

In this problem, time is measured in seconds (s), length in metres (m) and temperature in degree Celsius (°C). A bird flies following the trajectory given by $$\mathbf r(t)=t\,\mathbf i+\frac{t^3}{3}\,\mathbf j+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}t^2\,\mathbf k,\qquad t\ge0.$$
4 marks(i) Compute the velocity, acceleration and speed of the bird at time \(t=2\).
Worked solution
\(\mathbf v=\mathbf r'\), \(\mathbf a=\mathbf v'\), speed \(=\|\mathbf v\|\). Look for a perfect square under the root.
$$\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf i+t^2\mathbf j+\sqrt2\,t\,\mathbf k\ [1],\qquad \mathbf a(t)=2t\,\mathbf j+\sqrt2\,\mathbf k\ [1]$$ $$\|\mathbf v(t)\|=\sqrt{1+t^4+2t^2}=\sqrt{(1+t^2)^2}=1+t^2\ [1].$$
At \(t=2\) [1]: \(\mathbf v(2)=\mathbf i+4\mathbf j+2\sqrt2\,\mathbf k\) m/s, \(\;\mathbf a(2)=4\mathbf j+\sqrt2\,\mathbf k\) m/s², \(\;\|\mathbf v(2)\|=5\) m/s.
Include units — the question defines them, and the marking scheme writes m/s and m/s² next to each answer.
3 marks(ii) Compute the length of the bird's flight path from time \(t=0\) to time \(t=2\).
Worked solution $$L=\int_0^2\|\mathbf v(t)\|\,dt\ [1]=\int_0^2(1+t^2)\,dt=\left[t+\tfrac{t^3}3\right]_0^2\ [1]=2+\tfrac83.$$
\(L=\dfrac{14}3\) m [1].
The examiner designed \(1+t^4+2t^2\) to be a perfect square so the integral is easy. If your integrand won't simplify, recheck \(\mathbf v\).
3 marks(iii) Assume the temperature at the point \((x,y,z)\) is given by \(T(x,y,z)=25-\dfrac{z}{100}\). Determine the rate of change in temperature experienced by the bird at time \(t\).
Worked solution
"Rate of change experienced along the path" ⇒ chain rule \(\dfrac{dT}{dt}=T_x\dot x+T_y\dot y+T_z\dot z\) (formula sheet).
$$\frac{dT}{dt}=0\cdot1+0\cdot t^2+\left(-\frac1{100}\right)\sqrt2\,t\ [1].$$
\(\dfrac{dT}{dt}=-\dfrac{\sqrt2\,t}{100}\) °C/s [1].

Question 10 — Work along a parabola [10 marks]

Determine the work done by the force $$\mathbf F(x,y,z)=x\,\mathbf i+y\,\mathbf j+e^z\cos(xy)\,\mathbf k$$ in moving an object along the parabola \(y=x^2,\ z=0\), from \(x=-1\) to \(x=2\).
Worked solution
Work \(=\int_a^b\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\) (formula sheet). Parametrise with \(x=t\). Notice \(z'=0\) kills the scary \(\mathbf k\)-component.
Parametrise [1 + 2]: \(\mathbf r(t)=(t,\,t^2,\,0)\), \(-1\le t\le2\). Derivative [1]: \(\mathbf r'(t)=(1,\,2t,\,0)\).
Field on the path [2]: \(\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=\big(t,\,t^2,\,e^0\cos(t^3)\big)\).
Set up [1] and dot [1]: $$W=\int_{-1}^{2}\big(t,\,t^2,\,\cos t^3\big)\cdot(1,\,2t,\,0)\,dt=\int_{-1}^{2}\big(t+2t^3\big)\,dt\ [1]$$ $$=\left[\frac{t^2}2+\frac{t^4}2\right]_{-1}^{2}=\left(2+8\right)-\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\right)=10-1.$$
\(W=9\) [1].
Alternative: \((x,y)\) part \(x\,\mathbf i+y\,\mathbf j=\nabla\big(\tfrac{x^2+y^2}2\big)\) and \(dz=0\), so \(W=\tfrac{x^2+y^2}2\Big|_{(-1,1)}^{(2,4)}=10-1=9\). Same answer in one line.

What 2020 teaches

1 · The ODE toolkit, drilled (6 of 20 questions)

  • Equilibria & stability (S1 Q10, S2 Q1): constant solutions → sign table → arrows. Half the marks are the justification. Watch factors like \((t-2)\) that flip direction, and squared factors that never change sign (→ semi-stable). → Ch 1
  • Integrating factor (S2 Q2): standard form first; use the given interval to drop \(|\cdot|\). → Ch 1
  • Substitution \(y=xu\) (S1 Q5): product rule, cancel \(x\), end with "\(u'=f(x)g(u)\), separable". → Ch 1
  • Undetermined coefficients (S1 Q7) and reduction of order (S2 Q3). → Ch 2

2 · The multivariable core — still examined today

  • Critical points + Hessian appear in both papers (S1 Q3, S2 Q6); Lagrange in both (S1 Q8 on a curve, S2 Q7 on a ball with interior check). → Ch 6
  • Curves: velocity/acceleration/position with ICs, arc length with a hidden perfect square, chain rule along a path (S1 Q4, Q6; S2 Q9). → Ch 7, Ch 5
  • Line integrals: ugly path ⇒ look for a potential (S1 Q9); nice path ⇒ parametrise directly (S2 Q10). → Ch 7
  • Conics: complete the square, divide to "=1", label centre/vertices/asymptotes or axes (S1 Q2, S2 Q8). → Ch 3

3 · Legacy topics

Lines & planes geometry (S1 Q1, S2 Q4) — cross products, point-to-plane distance, line of intersection. Useful vector fluency (normals, \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v=0\)), but not a standalone question on the recent papers. Tangent planes (S2 Q5) remain core → Ch 4.

4 · Time allocation

10 × 10 marks in 120 min ⇒ 12 min per question. The quick wins (S1 Q4, S2 Q5, Q9, Q10) take 6–8 min — bank that time for the Lagrange and stability questions, where case analysis and justification eat minutes.

5 · Marker habits (from the S2 scheme)

  • Marks are paid per step: gradient components, the \(D\) formula, each case, each constant. A wrong final answer with visible correct steps still scores 6–8/10.
  • State domains/assumptions ("\(\sin x\gt0\) on \((0,\tfrac\pi2)\)", "\(x\ne0\)", "\(R,\omega\gt0\)") — they are worth marks.
  • Keep exact values (\(e^6\), \(\sqrt2\), \(\arctan2\)); no calculator is allowed.

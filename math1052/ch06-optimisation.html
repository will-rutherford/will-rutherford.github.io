§1 · Concepts in depth

Rusty on single-variable max/min (set \(f'(x)=0\), use the sign of \(f''\), check the endpoints of a closed interval) or on factorising cubics and solving \(e^{u}=\dots\)? Read the Ground Floor first. Everything in this chapter is that same idea, done in 2 or 3 variables at once. You also need \(\nabla f\) from Chapter 5.

What we are hunting: local max, local min, saddle

Let \(z=f(x,y)\) be a surface. A point \((a,b)\) is a

  • local maximum if \(f(a,b)\ge f(x,y)\) for all \((x,y)\) near \((a,b)\) — a hilltop;
  • local minimum if \(f(a,b)\le f(x,y)\) for all \((x,y)\) near \((a,b)\) — a valley floor;
  • saddle point if, however close you look, there are points where \(f\) is higher and points where it is lower than \(f(a,b)\) (Summary p 7: "analogous to a point of inflection in higher dimensions") — a mountain pass: up along one direction, down along another.

A critical point is a point where either \(\nabla f(a,b)=\mathbf 0\), i.e. \(f_x=f_y=0\), or where \(\nabla f\) is undefined (Summary p 7; Poster B: "like a sharp point, e.g. top of a pyramid"). Every local max/min of a differentiable function is a critical point — but not every critical point is a max or min (saddles!).

🧭 Why \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\)? The tangent plane goes flat

At the top of a smooth hill, if you step in any direction \(\mathbf u\), the height is momentarily not changing: the directional derivative \(D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u\) is zero for every \(\mathbf u\). The only vector with zero dot product against every direction is \(\mathbf 0\). So \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\).

Equivalently, the tangent plane \(z=f(a,b)+f_x(x-a)+f_y(y-b)\) becomes \(z=f(a,b)\): horizontal. Critical points are exactly where the surface has a horizontal tangent plane — hilltop, valley floor, or the flat middle of a saddle.

🧭 Why the Hessian decides: it is just the quadratic approximation

At a critical point the linear terms vanish, so the quadratic approximation (Poster B, Chapter 4) with \(h=x-a,\ k=y-b\) is

$$ f(a+h,b+k)\approx f(a,b)+\tfrac12\big(A h^2+2Bhk+Ck^2\big),\qquad A=f_{xx},\ B=f_{xy},\ C=f_{yy}\ \text{at }(a,b).$$

Whether \(f\) goes up or down near the point is the sign of the bracket. Complete the square (when \(A\ne0\)):

$$ Ah^2+2Bhk+Ck^2 = A\Big(h+\tfrac{B}{A}k\Big)^2+\frac{AC-B^2}{A}\,k^2 .$$

The number \(AC-B^2\) is exactly the Hessian determinant

$$ D=\begin{vmatrix} f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\ f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{vmatrix}=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2 .$$
  • \(D\gt0\): both terms have the sign of \(A\). If \(A\gt0\) the bracket is always positive → local min. If \(A\lt0\) always negative → local max.
  • \(D\lt0\): the two terms have opposite signs, so the bracket is positive in some directions and negative in others → saddle.
  • \(D=0\): the quadratic part is degenerate (flat in some direction), so the cubic/quartic terms decide → test inconclusive. You must investigate by hand.

That is the whole second-derivative test. You don't need to reproduce this derivation — but knowing it means you will never mix up the table.

When \(D\gt0\), \(f_{xx}\) and \(f_{yy}\) automatically have the same sign (because \(f_{xx}f_{yy}\gt f_{xy}^2\ge0\)), so you may use either one. \(D\gt0\) with \(f_{xx}=0\) is impossible.

The formula sheet gives \(D\) — not the rules. The printed sheet shows the determinant \(\begin{vmatrix} f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\ f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{vmatrix}\) but not which sign means what. Learn the four rows above cold. Also: \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\) — the mixed partial is squared and subtracted.

🧭 Solving \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\): the marks are lost in the algebra

Differentiating is easy; the hard part is solving two (usually non-linear) simultaneous equations and finding all solutions. Examiners award ~half the marks for the critical points alone (2023 S1 Q5: 5 of 13; 2020 S2 Q6: 6 of 10). The disciplined approach:

  1. Factorise each equation fully and write it as a product \(=0\): e.g. \(f_x=2xy=0\), \(f_y=x^2+2y-4=0\).
  2. Branch on the factors of the simplest equation: "\(2xy=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(y=0\)". Write each case as a separate line.
  3. Feed each case into the other equation and solve: \(x=0\Rightarrow 2y-4=0\Rightarrow y=2\); \(y=0\Rightarrow x^2=4\Rightarrow x=\pm2\).
  4. List the points as coordinate pairs: \((0,2),(2,0),(-2,0)\).
  5. Sanity-check each pair in both original equations (10 seconds, saves the question).

Golden rules. Never divide by an expression that could be zero — split it off as a case instead. \(e^{(\cdot)}\) is never zero, so you may divide by it (2025 S2 Q6). If both equations are linear, it's just simultaneous equations. If they are symmetric (swap \(x\leftrightarrow y\) and the system is unchanged), subtracting them often gives \((x-y)(\dots)=0\).

When \(D=0\): settle it by hand

The test is silent, but the question is not. Standard moves (2022 S2 Q6(b) gave 3 of 6 marks for exactly this):

  • Cross-sections / paths. Restrict \(f\) to a line or curve through the point. If \(f-f(a,b)\) takes both signs arbitrarily close to the point → saddle (neither max nor min). E.g. \(f=2x^3+2y^3-3x^2y^2\) at \((0,0)\): along \(y=0\), \(f=2x^3\) is \(\gt0\) for \(x\gt0\) and \(\lt0\) for \(x\lt0\) → saddle.
  • Sign / square argument. If you can show \(f(x,y)\ge f(a,b)\) for all nearby points (e.g. \(f=x^4+y^2\ge0=f(0,0)\)) → local (even global) min.
  • Completing the square to rewrite \(f\) as a sum of squares.

"\(D=0\) so it is a saddle" and "\(D=0\) so there is no max or min" are both wrong statements. \(D=0\) only means this test can't decide.

🧭 Global (absolute) extrema: local is not enough

A global maximum is the largest value of \(f\) on the whole domain; local max only compares with neighbours. Two very different situations:

(1) Closed and bounded region \(S\) (a square, triangle, disk — includes its boundary, finite size). The Extreme Value Theorem (EVT): a continuous function on a closed and bounded set attains a global max and a global min. They can only occur at

  • critical points in the interior of \(S\), or
  • points on the boundary of \(S\) (including corners).

So: make a candidate list, evaluate \(f\) at every candidate, and pick the largest and smallest. No Hessian needed (Summary p 7: "compare the values of \(f\) at the critical points … and the extreme values of \(f\) on the boundary").

(2) Unbounded domain (all of \(\mathbb R^2\)). EVT does not apply; a global max may not exist. To prove "no global max", exhibit a path along which \(f\to+\infty\): 2023 S1 Q5(c) used \(f(x,0)=3x-x^3-1\to+\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\). To prove one does exist, usually rewrite \(f\) cleverly (2024 S2 Q1(b): the denominator \(x^2+2(y-1)^2+1\ge1\), so \(T\le100\)).

Boundary work: turning a 2-D problem into 1-D problems

On each piece of the boundary, parametrise and you get a single-variable function on a closed interval — the Ground Floor routine (derivative \(=0\) + endpoints):

🧭 Lagrange multipliers: where the contour just touches the constraint

Now the problem is: maximise/minimise \(f(x,y)\) subject to \(g(x,y)=0\) (you may only walk along the curve \(g=0\)). Picture the contours \(f=c\) and the constraint curve. As you walk along the curve you cross contours — \(f\) is changing. At a constrained max or min you stop crossing: the curve is tangent to a contour of \(f\) (Summary p 7: "the contour \(g=0\) touches one of the contours of \(f\), so they share a common tangent").

Gradients are perpendicular to contours (Chapter 5). Two curves tangent at a point have parallel normals, so

$$ \boxed{\ \nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g,\qquad g=0\ }$$

for some number \(\lambda\) (the Lagrange multiplier). In 2-D that is 3 equations in \(x,y,\lambda\); in 3-D, 4 equations in \(x,y,z,\lambda\). It works identically for a surface \(g(x,y,z)=0\).

Which candidate is the max? Lagrange only produces candidates. Evaluate \(f\) at every one: if the constraint is closed and bounded (circle, ellipse, sphere) EVT guarantees the largest is the max and the smallest is the min. If the constraint is unbounded (a line, a plane), compare with another point on the constraint to justify (2023 S2 Q2 official solution: \(f(7,0)=70\gt6=f(3,2)\), "so at \((3,2)\) there is a minimum").

The elimination-of-\(\lambda\) technique

\(\lambda\) is a helper — you almost never need its value. Kill it early:

  1. Write the component equations and number them ①②③(④).
  2. Split off the zero cases first. If an equation looks like \(yz^2=\lambda\), ask: what if \(z=0\)? what if \(\lambda=0\)? Handle those cases separately (usually they give \(f=0\) or contradict the constraint).
  3. In the remaining case (all relevant quantities \(\ne0\)), divide ①÷② or cross-multiply to get a relation like \(y=x\), \(x^2=y^2\), \(z=2x\).
  4. Substitute the relations into the constraint to get actual numbers.
  5. Evaluate \(f\) at every candidate; state the max and min with the points.

Alternative to dividing: rearrange both to "\(\lambda=\dots\)" and equate; or subtract to get a product \(=0\), e.g. \(\lambda(x+2y)=0\) (2023 S1 Q6). A product = 0 gives you the cases for free.

What does \(\lambda\) mean? (Tutorial 10 Q8 — background, not exam-core)

If the constraint is \(g(x,y)=c\) and \(M(c)\) is the optimal value, then \(\dfrac{dM}{dc}=\lambda\). So \(\lambda\) is the "shadow price": how much the best value improves per unit increase of the budget \(c\). Nice to know; the exam has never asked for it.

§2 · Key methods & when to use them

The sheet prints \(D\) and \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\). Everything else below — the classification signs, EVT, the boundary routine, the case analysis — is yours to bring.

1 · Critical points (Summary p 7)

$$ f_x(a,b)=0\quad\text{and}\quad f_y(a,b)=0 \qquad(\text{or }\nabla f\text{ undefined}).$$
"Find (all) the critical points", "determine the stationary points", or as step one of any classification / global-extremum question.

Mini-example. \(f=x^2+4y^2-2x+8y+1\): \(f_x=2x-2=0,\ f_y=8y+8=0\Rightarrow(1,-1)\).

2 · Second-derivative (Hessian) test (sheet gives \(D\))

$$ D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\ \text{ at the point:}\quad D\gt0,f_{xx}\gt0\Rightarrow\text{min};\ \ D\gt0,f_{xx}\lt0\Rightarrow\text{max};\ \ D\lt0\Rightarrow\text{saddle};\ \ D=0\Rightarrow ?$$
"Classify", "determine the nature of", "local max, local min or saddle". Compute the three second partials as functions first, then substitute each point.

Mini-example. Same \(f\): \(f_{xx}=2,f_{yy}=8,f_{xy}=0\Rightarrow D=16\gt0,\ f_{xx}\gt0\) → local min, \(f(1,-1)=-4\).

3 · The \(D=0\) fallback

Cross-sections through the point (\(y=0\), \(x=0\), \(y=x\), \(y=kx^2\)…) or a sign/square argument.

Only when the Hessian test gives \(D=0\) at a point you must classify.

Mini-example. \(f=x^2-y^4\) at \((0,0)\): \(D=2\cdot0-0=0\). Along \(y=0\): \(x^2\gt0\); along \(x=0\): \(-y^4\lt0\). Both signs → saddle.

4 · Global extrema on a closed bounded region (EVT)

  1. State: \(f\) is continuous and \(S\) is closed and bounded, so by the Extreme Value Theorem global max/min exist.
  2. Interior critical points — discard any outside \(S\) or on its boundary.
  3. Each boundary piece → 1-variable function → stationary points + endpoints.
  4. Table of all candidates and values → largest = global max, smallest = global min.
"Determine the global/absolute maximum and minimum of \(f\) on \(S\)" where \(S\) is a square, rectangle, triangle or disk; typically split (a) boundary (b) interior (c) global (2024 S1 Q5, 2025 S1 Q5).

5 · Existence of a global max/min on \(\mathbb R^2\)

No: find a line where \(f\to\pm\infty\), e.g. \(f(x,0)=x^3\to\pm\infty\). Yes: rewrite so a bound is obvious and attained, e.g. \(x^2+2(y-1)^2+1\ge1\) with equality at \((0,1)\).

"Does \(f\) attain a global/absolute maximum? Explain." (2023 S1 Q5c, 2023 S2 Q1b), "Determine the maximum temperature on the plate" (2024 S2 Q1b).

6 · Lagrange multipliers — two variables (sheet: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\))

$$ \begin{cases} f_x=\lambda g_x\\ f_y=\lambda g_y\\ g(x,y)=0\end{cases}$$
"Use the method of Lagrange multipliers", "subject to the constraint", "on the circle / ellipse / curve", "Epsilon walks along…".

Mini-example. \(f=x+2y\) on \(x^2+y^2=5\): \(1=2\lambda x,\ 2=2\lambda y\Rightarrow y=2x\Rightarrow5x^2=5\Rightarrow(\pm1,\pm2)\). \(f(1,2)=5\) max, \(f(-1,-2)=-5\) min.

7 · Lagrange multipliers — three variables

$$ \begin{cases} f_x=\lambda g_x\\ f_y=\lambda g_y\\ f_z=\lambda g_z\\ g(x,y,z)=0\end{cases}$$
Constraint is a sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=R^2\) or a plane \(ax+by+cz=d\) (2021 S2 Q6, 2024 S2 Q5, 2025 S1 Q6, 2025 S2 Q5).

Shortcut to check your answer (linear \(f\) on a sphere): \(\max\big(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x\big)\) on \(\|\mathbf x\|=R\) is \(R\|\mathbf a\|\), at \(\mathbf x=R\,\mathbf a/\|\mathbf a\|\). E.g. \(x-2y+2z\) on the unit sphere: max \(=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3\). Use it to check, but show the Lagrange working for the marks.

8 · Applied optimisation (word problems)

  1. Draw it; name the variables (with units).
  2. Objective \(f\) = the thing to max/min (volume, area, cost, distance²).
  3. Constraint \(g=0\) = the fixed quantity (surface area, volume, "lies on the sphere").
  4. Lagrange; use positivity (\(r,h\gt0\)) to discard cases; answer the question in words with units.
Boxes, cylinders (2024 S1 Q6), cones inscribed in spheres (2022 S2 Q7), distance from a point to a plane/curve (T10 Q5). Minimise distance² instead of distance — same minimiser, no square roots.

Example 3 — Exponential factor (2025 S2 style): \(f(x,y)=(x^2+y^2)e^{-x}\)

Q. Determine and classify all critical points of \(f(x,y)=(x^2+y^2)e^{-x}\). [12 marks]

Product rule in \(x\): \(f_x=2xe^{-x}-(x^2+y^2)e^{-x}=e^{-x}(2x-x^2-y^2)\). And \(f_y=2ye^{-x}\).

Since \(e^{-x}\ne0\) for all \(x\): \(f_y=0\Rightarrow y=0\). Then \(f_x=0\Rightarrow 2x-x^2=0\Rightarrow x(2-x)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(x=2\).

Critical points: \((0,0),\ (2,0)\).

Second partials (differentiate \(f_x=e^{-x}(2x-x^2-y^2)\) again by the product rule):

$$ f_{xx}=e^{-x}\big(x^2+y^2-4x+2\big),\qquad f_{yy}=2e^{-x},\qquad f_{xy}=-2ye^{-x}. $$

Example 4 — When \(D=0\): \(x^4+y^2\) versus \(x^3+y^2\)

Q. Show that \((0,0)\) is the only critical point of each function, that the second-derivative test fails there, and classify it anyway. (a) \(f=x^4+y^2\) (b) \(g=x^3+y^2\). [8 marks]

(a) \(f_x=4x^3,\ f_y=2y\Rightarrow(0,0)\) only. \(f_{xx}=12x^2=0,\ f_{yy}=2,\ f_{xy}=0\Rightarrow D(0,0)=0\): inconclusive. But \(f(x,y)=x^4+y^2\ge0=f(0,0)\) for all \((x,y)\), so \((0,0)\) is a local (in fact global) minimum.

(b) \(g_x=3x^2,\ g_y=2y\Rightarrow(0,0)\) only. \(g_{xx}=6x=0\Rightarrow D(0,0)=0\): inconclusive. Along the \(x\)-axis \(g(x,0)=x^3\), which is \(\gt0\) for \(x\gt0\) and \(\lt0\) for \(x\lt0\), arbitrarily close to the origin, while \(g(0,0)=0\). So \((0,0)\) is neither a max nor a min — a saddle point.

Same \(D=0\), opposite answers. That is exactly why "\(D=0\)" is never a final classification.

Example 6 — Global extrema on a triangle: \(f=x^2-xy+y^2-3x\), \(x,y\ge0,\ x+y\le4\)

Q. Let \(S\) be the closed triangle with vertices \((0,0),(4,0),(0,4)\). (a) Find the max and min of \(f\) on the boundary of \(S\). (b) Find the critical point(s) of \(f\) in the interior. (c) State the global max and min on \(S\). [13 marks]

(a) Boundary — three edges.

  • \(y=0,\ 0\le x\le4\): \(g(x)=x^2-3x\), \(g'(x)=2x-3=0\Rightarrow x=\tfrac32\): \(g=-\tfrac94\). Ends: \(g(0)=0,\ g(4)=4\).
  • \(x=0,\ 0\le y\le4\): \(g(y)=y^2\), increasing on \([0,4]\): min \(0\) at \((0,0)\), max \(16\) at \((0,4)\).
  • \(y=4-x,\ 0\le x\le4\): \(g(x)=x^2-x(4-x)+(4-x)^2-3x=3x^2-15x+16\). \(g'(x)=6x-15=0\Rightarrow x=\tfrac52\): \(g=-\tfrac{11}4\) at \((\tfrac52,\tfrac32)\). Ends: \(16\) at \((0,4)\), \(4\) at \((4,0)\).

On the boundary: max \(16\) at \((0,4)\); min \(-\tfrac{11}{4}\) at \((\tfrac52,\tfrac32)\).

(b) Interior. \(f_x=2x-y-3=0,\ f_y=-x+2y=0\Rightarrow x=2y\Rightarrow3y=3\Rightarrow(2,1)\). Inside? \(2\gt0,1\gt0,2+1=3\lt4\) ✓. \(f(2,1)=4-2+1-6=-3\). (\(D=4-1=3\gt0,\ f_{xx}=2\gt0\): a local min — not needed for (c), but free.)

(c) By the EVT (continuous \(f\), closed bounded \(S\)), compare: \(16,\ -\tfrac{11}4,\ -\tfrac94,\ 4,\ 0,\ -3\). Global max \(16\) at \((0,4)\); global min \(-3\) at \((2,1)\).

Example 7 — Lagrange in 2-D: \(f=xy\) on the ellipse \(x^2+4y^2=8\)

Q. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the max and min of \(f(x,y)=xy\) subject to \(x^2+4y^2=8\). [12 marks]

Let \(g(x,y)=x^2+4y^2-8\). \(\nabla f=(y,x),\ \nabla g=(2x,8y)\). Solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g,\ g=0\):

$$ y=2\lambda x\ \ ①,\qquad x=8\lambda y\ \ ②,\qquad x^2+4y^2=8\ \ ③. $$

Zero case. If \(x=0\), ① gives \(y=0\), but \((0,0)\) does not satisfy ③. So \(x\ne0\), and similarly \(y\ne0\).

Main case. Multiply ① and ②: \(xy=16\lambda^2xy\Rightarrow16\lambda^2=1\) (as \(xy\ne0\)) \(\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm\tfrac14\).

  • \(\lambda=\tfrac14\): \(y=\tfrac{x}{2}\). ③: \(x^2+x^2=8\Rightarrow x=\pm2\). Points \((2,1),(-2,-1)\), \(f=2\).
  • \(\lambda=-\tfrac14\): \(y=-\tfrac x2\). Points \((2,-1),(-2,1)\), \(f=-2\).

The ellipse is closed and bounded, so the extremes exist and are among these. Max \(2\) at \((2,1),(-2,-1)\); min \(-2\) at \((2,-1),(-2,1)\).

Example 8 — Lagrange in 3-D: maximise \(xyz\) with \(x+2y+3z=18\), \(x,y,z\gt0\)

Q. Find the maximum of \(f=xyz\) subject to \(x+2y+3z=18\) with \(x,y,z\gt0\). [10 marks]

$$ yz=\lambda\ ①,\qquad xz=2\lambda\ ②,\qquad xy=3\lambda\ ③,\qquad x+2y+3z=18\ ④. $$

All variables are positive so \(\lambda=yz\gt0\), and we may divide.

② ÷ ①: \(\dfrac{x}{y}=2\Rightarrow x=2y\).   ③ ÷ ①: \(\dfrac{x}{z}=3\Rightarrow x=3z\).

④: \(x+x+x=18\Rightarrow x=6,\ y=3,\ z=2\). \(f(6,3,2)=36\).

Why a max: on the closed triangle \(x,y,z\ge0\), \(x+2y+3z=18\), \(f\) is continuous so it has a max (EVT); on the edges \(f=0\), so the max is at the only interior candidate: max \(=36\) at \((6,3,2)\).

Example 9 — Distance to a plane: closest point of \(2x-y+2z=18\) to the origin

Q. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the point on the plane \(2x-y+2z=18\) closest to the origin, and the distance. [10 marks]

Minimise distance² \(f=x^2+y^2+z^2\) (same minimiser, no roots) subject to \(g=2x-y+2z-18=0\).

$$ 2x=2\lambda,\qquad 2y=-\lambda,\qquad 2z=2\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ x=\lambda,\ y=-\tfrac{\lambda}{2},\ z=\lambda. $$

Constraint: \(2\lambda+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}+2\lambda=18\Rightarrow\tfrac92\lambda=18\Rightarrow\lambda=4\). Point \((4,-2,4)\), \(f=16+4+16=36\), distance \(6\).

Why a min: \(f\) grows without bound on the plane (far-away points), so the single candidate is the minimum. Check with the point-to-plane formula: \(\dfrac{|18|}{\sqrt{4+1+4}}=6\) ✓.

📄 Real exam question — 2025 S2 Q6 [12 marks]

Determine and classify all critical points of the function

$$ h(x,y)=(y^2+2x-3)e^{-2x}. $$
Full worked solution — 2025 S2 Q6

Critical points. Product rule in \(x\):

$$ h_x=2e^{-2x}+(y^2+2x-3)(-2)e^{-2x}=e^{-2x}\big(-2y^2-4x+8\big),\qquad h_y=2ye^{-2x}. $$

Since \(e^{-2x}\ne0\): \(h_y=0\Rightarrow y=0\). Then \(h_x=0\Rightarrow -4x+8=0\Rightarrow x=2\). Only critical point: \((2,0)\).

Classification. Differentiate \(h_x=e^{-2x}(-2y^2-4x+8)\) again:

$$ h_{xx}=-2e^{-2x}(-2y^2-4x+8)+e^{-2x}(-4)=e^{-2x}(4y^2+8x-20),\quad h_{yy}=2e^{-2x},\quad h_{xy}=-4ye^{-2x}. $$

At \((2,0)\): \(h_{xx}=-4e^{-4},\ h_{yy}=2e^{-4},\ h_{xy}=0\), so

$$ D(2,0)=(-4e^{-4})(2e^{-4})-0^2=-8e^{-8}\lt0. $$

Since \(D\lt0\), the critical point \((2,0)\) is a saddle point. (\(h(2,0)=e^{-4}\).)

Official solution structure exactly: \(\nabla h=(0,0)\) → factor \(e^{-2x}\) → \(y=0\) → \(x=2\) → second partials → \(D\) → sentence. Note how \(e^{-4}\) and \(e^{-8}\) are left exact; only signs matter.

Worked: 2023 S2 Q1 — \(x^3+y^3+3xy\), classify and "absolute max?" [8+4]

\(f_x=3x^2+3y=0\Rightarrow y=-x^2\). \(f_y=3y^2+3x=0\Rightarrow x=-y^2=-x^4\Rightarrow x(1+x^3)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(x=-1\). Points \((0,0),(-1,-1)\).

\(f_{xx}=6x,\ f_{yy}=6y,\ f_{xy}=3\Rightarrow D=36xy-9\).

  • \((0,0)\): \(D=-9\lt0\) → saddle.
  • \((-1,-1)\): \(D=27\gt0\), \(f_{xx}=-6\lt0\) → local max, \(f=1\).

(b) Along \(y=0\), \(f(x,0)=x^3\to+\infty\) as \(x\to\infty\) and \(\to-\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\). So \(f\) has no absolute max and no absolute min (the local max \(1\) is beaten by e.g. \(f(2,0)=8\)).

Worked: 2022 S2 Q6 — the "happy function" \(2x^3+2y^3-3x^2y^2\), where \(D=0\) [5+6]

\(f_x=6x^2-6xy^2=6x(x-y^2)\), \(f_y=6y^2-6x^2y=6y(y-x^2)\).

From \(f_x=0\): \(x=0\) or \(x=y^2\). \(x=0\Rightarrow6y^2=0\Rightarrow y=0\). \(x=y^2\Rightarrow 6y(y-y^4)=6y^2(1-y^3)=0\Rightarrow y=0\) or \(y=1\). Points \((0,0),(1,1)\).

\(f_{xx}=12x-6y^2,\ f_{yy}=12y-6x^2,\ f_{xy}=-12xy\). At \((1,1)\): \(D=6\cdot6-144=-108\lt0\) → saddle. At \((0,0)\): \(D=0\) — inconclusive. Along \(y=0\): \(f(x,0)=2x^3\), positive for \(x\gt0\), negative for \(x\lt0\), and \(f(0,0)=0\) → \((0,0)\) is a saddle.

The marking scheme gave 3 of the 6 classification marks to the \(D=0\) analysis. The official solution originally wrote "not a local max/min nor a saddle" and crossed it out to "a saddle point" — neither max nor min is a saddle in this course's definition.

📄 Real exam question — 2025 S1 Q5 [13 marks]

In the plane, let \(S\) be the closed square region bounded by the lines \(x=0,\ x=1,\ y=0,\ y=1\). Consider the function \(f(x,y)=4x^2-3y^2+2xy\).

  • (a) (7 marks) Determine the maximum and minimum values of \(f\) on the boundary of \(S\).
  • (b) (4 marks) Determine the critical point(s) of \(f\) in the interior of \(S\).
  • (c) (2 marks) Determine the global maximum and minimum values of \(f\) on \(S\).
Full worked solution — 2025 S1 Q5 (with the marking scheme)

(a) Four edges:

  • (i) \(x=0,\ y\in[0,1]\): \(f=-3y^2\). Min \(-3\) at \((0,1)\), max \(0\) at \((0,0)\). [1 mark]
  • (ii) \(x=1,\ y\in[0,1]\): \(g(y)=4-3y^2+2y\), \(g'(y)=-6y+2=0\Rightarrow y=\tfrac13\), \(g(\tfrac13)=\tfrac{13}3\); \(g(0)=4,\ g(1)=3\). Max \(\tfrac{13}3\) at \((1,\tfrac13)\), min \(3\) at \((1,1)\). [2 marks]
  • (iii) \(y=0,\ x\in[0,1]\): \(f=4x^2\). Max \(4\) at \((1,0)\), min \(0\) at \((0,0)\). [1 mark]
  • (iv) \(y=1,\ x\in[0,1]\): \(h(x)=4x^2-3+2x\), \(h'(x)=8x+2=0\Rightarrow x=-\tfrac14\notin[0,1]\); \(h(0)=-3,\ h(1)=3\). Max \(3\) at \((1,1)\), min \(-3\) at \((0,1)\). [2 marks]

On the boundary of \(S\): max \(\tfrac{13}{3}\) at \((1,\tfrac13)\), min \(-3\) at \((0,1)\). [1 mark for conclusion]

(b) \(\nabla f=(8x+2y,\ -6y+2x)\) [1 mark]. Setting to zero: \(4x+y=0\) and \(x=3y\Rightarrow 12y+y=0\Rightarrow y=0,x=0\) [2 marks]. But \((0,0)\) is a corner, not in the interior of \(S\) [1 mark]. So there are no critical points of \(f\) in the interior.

(c) By the Extreme Value Theorem, global max \(\tfrac{13}{3}\) (at \((1,\tfrac13)\)) and global min \(-3\) (at \((0,1)\)). [1 mark for stating EVT; 1 mark for correct values]

Worked: 2024 S1 Q5 — \(f=2+2x+2y-x^2-y^2\) on the triangle \(x,y\ge0,\ y\le9-x\) [6+4+3]

(c) EVT: global max \(4\) at \((1,1)\); global min \(-61\) at \((0,9)\) and \((9,0)\).

Worked: 2023 S1 Q5 — \(f=3xe^y-x^3-e^{3y}\) [5+4+4]

(a) \(f_x=3e^y-3x^2=0\Rightarrow x^2=e^y\) ①. \(f_y=3xe^y-3e^{3y}=0\) ②. Substitute \(e^y=x^2\): \(3x^3-3x^6=3x^3(1-x^3)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(x=1\). \(x=0\Rightarrow e^y=0\): no solution. \(x=1\Rightarrow e^y=1\Rightarrow y=0\). Only critical point \((1,0)\).

(b) \(f_{xx}=-6x,\ f_{xy}=3e^y,\ f_{yy}=3xe^y-9e^{3y}\). At \((1,0)\): \(D=(-6)(-6)-9=27\gt0\), \(f_{xx}=-6\lt0\) → local max, \(f(1,0)=1\).

(c) No. \(f(x,0)=3x-x^3-1\to+\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\) (e.g. \(f(-3,0)=17\gt1\)). So \(f\) has no global maximum, even though its only critical point is a local max.

This is the famous "one critical point, it's a local max, but not global" example — impossible in 1-D, possible in 2-D.

Worked: 2024 S2 Q1(b) — max of \(T=\dfrac{100}{3+x^2+2y^2-4y}\) [4]

\(T\) is largest when the (positive) denominator is smallest [1]. Complete the square: \(3+x^2+2y^2-4y=x^2+2(y-1)^2+1\ge1\), with equality at \((0,1)\) [2]. So the maximum temperature is \(100\), at \((0,1)\) [1].

📄 Real exam question — 2023 S1 Q6 [12 marks]

The temperature at a point \((x,y)\) on a metal plate is \(T(x,y)=4x^2-4xy+y^2\). Epsilon, our pet ant, walks on the metal plate along a circle of radius 5 centred at the origin. Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the highest and lowest temperatures encountered by Epsilon.

Full worked solution — 2023 S1 Q6

Constraint \(g=x^2+y^2-25=0\). \(\nabla T=\lambda\nabla g\):

$$ 8x-4y=2\lambda x\ ①,\qquad -4x+2y=2\lambda y\ ②,\qquad x^2+y^2=25\ ③. $$

From ①: \(4x-2y=\lambda x\). But ② says \(-(4x-2y)=2\lambda y\), so \(-\lambda x=2\lambda y\), i.e. \(\lambda(x+2y)=0\).

  • \(\lambda=0\): ① gives \(y=2x\). ③: \(5x^2=25\Rightarrow x=\pm\sqrt5\): points \(\pm(\sqrt5,2\sqrt5)\). \(T=(2x-y)^2=0\).
  • \(x=-2y\): ③: \(5y^2=25\Rightarrow y=\pm\sqrt5\): points \(\pm(-2\sqrt5,\sqrt5)\). \(T=(2x-y)^2=(-5\sqrt5)^2=125\).

The circle is closed and bounded, so the extremes are among these. Highest temperature \(125\), lowest \(0\).

Spotting \(T=(2x-y)^2\) makes evaluation instant and shows \(T\ge0\) so \(0\) must be the min. (The official solution has a typo "\(y=\pm5\)" in the second case; it should be \(\pm\sqrt5\) — the values \(125\) and \(0\) are right.)

Worked: 2023 S2 Q2 — a line constraint (one candidate: justify it)

\(g=2x+4y-14\). \((4x-4,\ 16y-16)=\lambda(2,4)\Rightarrow 2(x-1)=\lambda=4(y-1)\Rightarrow x=2y-1\). Constraint: \(2(2y-1)+4y=14\Rightarrow y=2,\ x=3\). \(f(3,2)=6\).

Is it a min? The line is unbounded, so EVT doesn't apply. Official solution: pick another point on the constraint, \((7,0)\): \(f(7,0)=70\gt6\). "So at \((3,2)\) there is a minimum." Min value \(6\).

Type E — Lagrange multipliers, three variables 10–12 marks

Instances: 2025 S2 Q5 (12, \(xyz^2\) on \(x+y+z=20\)) · 2025 S1 Q6 (12, \(400xyz^2\) on unit sphere) · 2024 S2 Q5 (12, \(x-2y+2z\) on unit sphere → max \(3\) at \((\tfrac13,-\tfrac23,\tfrac23)\), min \(-3\)) · 2021 S2 Q6 (10, \(x+2y+z\) on \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\tfrac{27}2\) → max \(9\) at \((\tfrac32,3,\tfrac32)\)) · 2020 S2 Q7 (10, \(x+yz\) on the unit ball → max \(1\), min \(-1\)) · Tutorial 10 Q4, Q5.

📄 Real exam question — 2025 S1 Q6 [12 marks]

Epsilon, our pet ant, walks on the unit sphere centred at \((0,0,0)\). The temperature at a point \((x,y,z)\) on the sphere is \(T(x,y,z)=400xyz^2\). Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the highest and lowest temperatures encountered by Epsilon.

Full worked solution — 2025 S1 Q6 (with the marking scheme)

Formulation [2 marks]: max/min \(T=400xyz^2\) s.t. \(g=x^2+y^2+z^2-1=0\).

Lagrange equations [2 marks]: \(\nabla T=\lambda\nabla g\):

$$ 400yz^2=2\lambda x\ ①,\qquad 400xz^2=2\lambda y\ ②,\qquad 800xyz=2\lambda z\ ③. $$

Special cases [2 marks]: if \(x\), \(y\) or \(z=0\) then \(T=0\). If \(\lambda=0\) then at least one of \(x,y,z\) is \(0\), so again \(T=0\).

Main case \(\lambda,x,y,z\ne0\) [4 marks]:

  • ② ÷ ①: \(\dfrac{x}{y}=\dfrac{y}{x}\Rightarrow x^2=y^2\).
  • ③ ÷ ②: \(\dfrac{2y}{z}=\dfrac{z}{y}\Rightarrow z^2=2y^2\).
  • Constraint: \(y^2+y^2+2y^2=1\Rightarrow y^2=\tfrac14\), so \(x=\pm\tfrac12,\ y=\pm\tfrac12,\ z=\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\).

\(T=400\,xy\cdot\tfrac12=200\,xy\), and \(xy=\pm\tfrac14\).

Highest temperature \(50\) (when \(x,y\) have the same sign) [1 mark]; lowest \(-50\) (opposite signs) [1 mark]. Both beat the special-case value \(0\).

📄 Real exam question — 2025 S2 Q5 [12 marks]

Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the maximum value of the function \(f(x,y,z)=xyz^2\) on the plane defined by \(x+y+z=20\).

Full worked solution — 2025 S2 Q5

\(g=x+y+z-20\). \(\nabla f=(yz^2,\ xz^2,\ 2xyz)=\lambda(1,1,1)\):

$$ yz^2=\lambda\ ①,\qquad xz^2=\lambda\ ②,\qquad 2xyz=\lambda\ ③,\qquad x+y+z=20\ ④. $$

Case \(z=0\): then \(\lambda=0\) and \(f=0\). Case \(z\ne0\): ①=② gives \(yz^2=xz^2\Rightarrow y=x\). If \(y=0\) then \(x=0\), \(f=0\). Otherwise ①=③: \(yz^2=2xyz\Rightarrow z=2x\). ④: \(x+x+2x=20\Rightarrow x=5\): point \((5,5,10)\), \(f=5\cdot5\cdot100=2500\).

Comparing candidates (\(2500\) vs \(0\)): maximum value \(2500\) at \((5,5,10)\) — the official answer.

Strictly, on the whole plane \(xyz^2\) is unbounded (take \(x=y=t,\ z=20-2t\): \(f=t^2(20-2t)^2\to\infty\)). The intended reading is the octant \(x,y,z\ge0\) (a closed triangle where EVT applies and \(f=0\) on the edges). Write the answer \(2500\); if you have 20 seconds, add "restricting to \(x,y,z\ge0\), where the constraint set is closed and bounded". Never argue with the examiner in the exam, but never be wrong either.

Type F — Applied / geometric optimisation with Lagrange 12 marks

Instances: 2024 S1 Q6 (12, closed cylinder with fixed surface area) · 2022 S2 Q7 (12, cone inscribed in the unit sphere → \(h=\tfrac43,\ r=\tfrac{2\sqrt2}3\), \(V=\tfrac{32\pi}{81}\)) · Tutorial 10 Q7 (Cobb–Douglas), Q8(e).

📄 Real exam question — 2024 S1 Q6 [12 marks]

A closed cylinder has height \(h\) and a circular base of radius \(r\). The cylinder has a fixed surface area, \(A\). Use Lagrange multipliers to show that the volume is maximised by choosing \(r=\sqrt{\dfrac{A}{6\pi}},\ h=\sqrt{\dfrac{2A}{3\pi}}\).

Full worked solution — 2024 S1 Q6

Objective \(V(r,h)=\pi r^2h\). Constraint \(g(r,h)=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh-A=0\) (two discs + curved side). \(r,h\gt0\).

$$ \nabla V=(2\pi rh,\ \pi r^2),\qquad \nabla g=(4\pi r+2\pi h,\ 2\pi r). $$ $$ 2\pi rh=\lambda(4\pi r+2\pi h)\ ①,\qquad \pi r^2=2\pi\lambda r\ ②. $$

② with \(r\gt0\): \(\lambda=\tfrac r2\). Into ①: \(2\pi rh=\tfrac r2(4\pi r+2\pi h)=2\pi r^2+\pi rh\Rightarrow\pi rh=2\pi r^2\Rightarrow h=2r\).

Constraint: \(2\pi r^2+2\pi r(2r)=6\pi r^2=A\Rightarrow r=\sqrt{\dfrac{A}{6\pi}}\), and \(h=2r=2\sqrt{\dfrac{A}{6\pi}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{4A}{6\pi}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2A}{3\pi}}\) ✓.

Why a max: it is the only candidate with \(r,h\gt0\), and \(V\to0\) at the extremes (\(r\to0\), or \(h\to0\) i.e. \(r\to\sqrt{A/2\pi}\)), so the single interior candidate is the maximum.

"Show that" questions give you the answer — so the marks are entirely for the working. Show \(\lambda\) elimination and the \(h=2r\) relation explicitly.

Worked: 2022 S2 Q7 — cone inscribed in the unit sphere

With the apex at the top of the sphere and the base a distance \(h-1\) below the centre, the Pythagorean relation is \((h-1)^2+r^2=1\) [2 marks for this constraint]. Maximise \(f=\tfrac13\pi r^2h\).

\(\tfrac23\pi rh=2\lambda r\) ①, \(\tfrac13\pi r^2=2\lambda(h-1)\) ②. \(\lambda\ne0\) (else \(r=0\)). From ①, \(\lambda=\tfrac{\pi h}{3}\); into ②: \(r^2=2h(h-1)\). Constraint: \((h-1)^2+2h(h-1)=1\Rightarrow3h^2-4h=0\Rightarrow h=\tfrac43\) (\(h=0\) is degenerate). Then \(r^2=\tfrac83\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac89\Rightarrow r=\tfrac{2\sqrt2}{3}\). \(h=\tfrac43,\ r=\tfrac{2\sqrt2}{3}\) (volume \(\tfrac{32\pi}{81}\)).

The question said "Note: you must use the method of Lagrange multipliers." Substituting \(r^2=1-(h-1)^2\) and doing 1-D calculus gets the right numbers but not the method marks.

§6 · Traps & marks lost

Dividing by something that might be zero. Cancelling \(x\) from \(2xy=0\), or \(z^2\) from \(yz^2=xz^2\), silently deletes critical points / Lagrange candidates. The 2025 S1 Q6 marking scheme gave 2 of 12 marks purely for "consideration of these special cases" (\(\lambda=0\); \(x\), \(y\) or \(z=0\)). The 2025 S2 official solution writes "(assuming \(z\ne0\))" every time it divides — copy that habit.
Missing critical points. A system like \(x^2=e^y,\ xe^y=e^{3y}\) has branches that die (\(e^y=0\)) and branches that live. Write every branch and say which ones fail and why ("\(e^y=0\) has no solution").
A "critical point in the interior" that isn't. 2025 S1 Q5(b): \(\nabla f=\mathbf0\) gives \((0,0)\), which is a corner of the square — "\((0,0)\) is not in the interior of \(S\)" was worth 1 mark. Always test the inequalities of \(S\) strictly.
Forgetting to quote the EVT. 2025 S1 Q5(c): "1 mark for stating EVT; 1 mark for correct global max and global min". One sentence: "\(f\) is continuous and \(S\) is closed and bounded, so by the Extreme Value Theorem \(f\) attains a global max and min on \(S\)."
Forgetting the endpoints of an edge. On an edge, a 1-D function on \([p,q]\) can have its extremes at the ends — and often does (2025 S1 Q5 edge (iv): the stationary point \(x=-\tfrac14\) lies outside \([0,1]\), so only the endpoints count). Also: discard stationary points that fall outside the edge's interval.
Stopping at "\(D=0\)". 2022 S2 Q6(b) gave 3 of 6 marks for the cross-section argument at \((0,0)\). "\(D=0\) so it's a saddle" scores zero for that point; so does "\(D=0\) so there is no extremum".
Hessian arithmetic. \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\) — minus, squared. Compute \(f_{xx},f_{yy},f_{xy}\) as general formulas first (1 mark in 2022 S2), then substitute each point. With exponential factors, differentiate the simplified \(f_x\) (e.g. \(e^{-2x}(-2y^2-4x+8)\)) by the product rule, carefully.
Using \(f_{xx}\) when \(D\lt0\). A negative \(D\) is a saddle regardless of \(f_{xx}\). And \(D\gt0\) with a negative \(f_{xx}\) is a max, not a min — read the sign twice.
Lagrange without the comparison. Lagrange gives candidates, not answers. Evaluate \(f\) at every candidate (including the special-case ones) and state which is the max and which the min, with points if asked ("find the points where…", 2024 S2 Q5, 2021 S2 Q6). 2024 S2 Q5 gave 2 marks for the final two lines.
Formulation marks are real marks. 2024 S2 Q5: "3 marks for formulation of the Lagrange problem" (writing \(f\), \(g\), \(\nabla f\), \(\nabla g\)) + "3 marks for correct Lagrange equations" — that's half the question before any algebra. 2025 S1 Q6: 2 + 2. Write them out even though they feel obvious.
Unbounded constraint, single candidate. On a line or plane the EVT does not apply. Justify max/min by comparing with another feasible point (2023 S2 Q2 official solution: \(f(7,0)=70\gt6\)), or by an argument that \(f\to\infty\) far away.
Not using Lagrange when told to. "Use the method of Lagrange multipliers" / "you must use the method of Lagrange multipliers" (2022 S2 Q7). Substituting the constraint to reduce to one variable gives the numbers but not the method marks.
Domain restrictions. 2021 S2 Q5 defined \(f\) on \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{x=0\text{ or }y=0\}\). Your cases must respect it: "as \(x\ne0\) and \(y\ne0\), \(xy(4x-y)=0\Rightarrow y=4x\)" (official solution).
Decimals. No calculator: leave \(e^{-4}\), \(\sqrt5\), \(\tfrac{13}{3}\), \(2^{1/6}\) exact. Classification needs signs only (\(e^{\text{anything}}\gt0\)).
Marker-pleasing layout. (1) Box the critical points. (2) Table: point · \(f_{xx}\) · \(D\) · nature · value. (3) A final sentence that answers the exact question asked ("the highest temperature is 50 and the lowest is −50").

§7 · Self-check

1. At a critical point \(f_{xx}=-3,\ f_{yy}=-3,\ f_{xy}=1\). Nature?

\(D=9-1=8\gt0\), \(f_{xx}\lt0\) → local maximum.

2. At a critical point \(f_{xx}=2,\ f_{yy}=8,\ f_{xy}=4\). Nature?

\(D=16-16=0\) → test inconclusive; investigate with cross-sections or a sign argument.

3. At a critical point \(f_{xx}=5,\ f_{yy}=-1,\ f_{xy}=0\). Does it matter that \(f_{xx}\gt0\)?

No: \(D=-5\lt0\) → saddle. \(f_{xx}\) is only consulted when \(D\gt0\).

4. Find and classify the critical point of \(f=x^2+xy+y^2-3y\).

\(2x+y=0,\ x+2y-3=0\Rightarrow(-1,2)\). \(D=2\cdot2-1=3\gt0,\ f_{xx}=2\gt0\) → local min.

5. Solving \(\nabla f=\mathbf0\) you reach \(xy^2=x\). What do you write next?

\(x(y^2-1)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(y=\pm1\) — three branches, each fed into the other equation. Never "divide by \(x\)".

6. \(f=x^2y-y^3\). Find \(D(x,y)\) and classify the only critical point.

\(f_x=2xy,\ f_y=x^2-3y^2\Rightarrow(0,0)\) only. \(f_{xx}=2y,\ f_{yy}=-6y,\ f_{xy}=2x\Rightarrow D=-12y^2-4x^2\), so \(D(0,0)=0\). Along the \(y\)-axis \(f=-y^3\) takes both signs → saddle (a "monkey saddle").

7. What does the Extreme Value Theorem say, and when must you quote it?

A continuous function on a closed and bounded set attains a global max and a global min. Quote it in every "global max/min on \(S\)" question (1 mark in 2025 S1 Q5), and use it to justify Lagrange answers on circles, ellipses and spheres.

8. Region \(S=\{x^2+y^2\le4\}\); \(\nabla f=\mathbf0\) at \((3,1)\) and \((1,1)\). Which are candidates?

\((1,1)\) only: \(1+1=2\lt4\) (interior). \((3,1)\) has \(9+1=10\gt4\) — outside \(S\), discard.

9. How many boundary pieces and corners does a triangle region have, and what do you do on each?

3 edges, 3 corners. On each edge substitute the line, get \(g(t)\) on a closed interval, solve \(g'=0\) inside the interval, evaluate at the ends (the ends are the corners).

10. Geometrically, what does \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) say?

At a constrained extremum the level curve (surface) of \(f\) is tangent to the constraint \(g=0\), so their normals \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) are parallel.

11. Lagrange on a line constraint gives a single candidate. How do you know if it's a max or a min?

Evaluate \(f\) at any other point on the line: if it's larger, the candidate is the min (2023 S2 Q2: \(f(7,0)=70\gt f(3,2)=6\)).

12. Maximise \(3x+4y\) on \(x^2+y^2=25\) in your head.

Linear \(f\) on a circle: max \(=R\|\mathbf a\|=5\cdot5=25\) at \(5\cdot\tfrac{(3,4)}5=(3,4)\); min \(-25\) at \((-3,-4)\). (Show the Lagrange working in the exam.)

13. Why minimise distance² rather than distance?

\(\sqrt{\ }\) is increasing, so both have the same minimiser — but distance² has polynomial derivatives (no square roots, no quotient rule).

14. Does \(f=x^3+y^3+3xy\) have an absolute maximum on \(\mathbb R^2\)?

No — \(f(x,0)=x^3\to\infty\). Its local max \((-1,-1)\), value \(1\), is not global (2023 S2 Q1(b)).

15. You get \(\lambda(x+2y)=0\) in a Lagrange problem. What next?

Two cases: \(\lambda=0\) (go back to the original equations with \(\lambda=0\)) and \(x=-2y\) (substitute into the constraint). Both give candidates (2023 S1 Q6).

8.2 · Classifying with the Hessian — including \(D=0\)

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — three points, two natures: \(f(x,y)=x^4-2x^2+y^2-2y\)

\(f_x=4x^3-4x=4x(x-1)(x+1)=0\Rightarrow x=0,\pm1\). \(f_y=2y-2=0\Rightarrow y=1\). Points \((0,1),(1,1),(-1,1)\).

\(f_{xx}=12x^2-4,\ f_{yy}=2,\ f_{xy}=0\Rightarrow D=2(12x^2-4)\).

  • \((0,1)\): \(D=-8\lt0\) → saddle, \(f=-1\).
  • \((\pm1,1)\): \(D=16\gt0,\ f_{xx}=8\gt0\) → local minima, \(f=1-2+1-2=-2\).

Two minima separated by a saddle — the "double well". Sketching the \(x\)-cross-section \(x^4-2x^2\) confirms it.

Worked B — \(D=0\) twice, different answers: \(x^2+y^4\) and \(x^2-y^4\)

Both have \((0,0)\) as the only critical point, with \(f_{xx}=2,\ f_{yy}=\pm12y^2=0,\ f_{xy}=0\Rightarrow D(0,0)=0\): inconclusive.

  • \(f=x^2+y^4\ge0=f(0,0)\) everywhere → minimum (local and global).
  • \(g=x^2-y^4\): along \(y=0\), \(g=x^2\gt0\); along \(x=0\), \(g=-y^4\lt0\) → saddle.

STRETCH · 8 marks Q5. Let \(f(x,y)=(y-x^2)(y-3x^2)=y^2-4x^2y+3x^4\). (a) Show \((0,0)\) is the only critical point and \(D(0,0)=0\). (b) Show that along every straight line \(y=mx\) (and along \(x=0\)), \(f\) has a local min at the origin. (c) Show \((0,0)\) is nevertheless not a local minimum.

(c) Along the parabola \(y=2x^2\) (between the two parabolas \(y=x^2\), \(y=3x^2\)): \(f=(x^2)(-x^2)=-x^4\lt0=f(0,0)\) arbitrarily close to the origin. So it is not a local min — it is a saddle. Moral: checking straight lines is not enough to prove a minimum.

8.3 · Global extrema on closed bounded regions

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — rectangle: \(f=x^2+2y^2-2x-4y\) on \(0\le x\le3,\ 0\le y\le2\)

EVT applies (polynomial on a closed, bounded rectangle).

Interior: \(f_x=2x-2,\ f_y=4y-4\Rightarrow(1,1)\), inside ✓, \(f=-3\).

Edges:

  • \(y=0\): \(x^2-2x\) on \([0,3]\): stationary \(x=1\) (\(-1\)); ends \(0\), \(3\).
  • \(y=2\): \(x^2-2x+8-8=x^2-2x\): same values: \(-1\) at \((1,2)\), \(0\), \(3\) at \((3,2)\).
  • \(x=0\): \(2y^2-4y\) on \([0,2]\): stationary \(y=1\) (\(-2\)); ends \(0\).
  • \(x=3\): \(3+2y^2-4y\): stationary \(y=1\) (\(1\)); ends \(3\).

Compare: global max \(3\) at \((3,0)\) and \((3,2)\); global min \(-3\) at \((1,1)\).

Worked B — disk: \(f=2x^2+y^2-y\) on \(x^2+y^2\le1\)

Interior: \(4x=0,\ 2y-1=0\Rightarrow(0,\tfrac12)\), inside, \(f=-\tfrac14\).

Boundary \(x^2=1-y^2\), \(y\in[-1,1]\): \(f=2(1-y^2)+y^2-y=2-y^2-y\). \(\tfrac{d}{dy}=-2y-1=0\Rightarrow y=-\tfrac12\), \(x=\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\): \(f=2-\tfrac14+\tfrac12=\tfrac94\). Ends: \(y=1\): \((0,1)\), \(f=0\); \(y=-1\): \((0,-1)\), \(f=2\).

Global max \(\tfrac94\) at \((\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}2,-\tfrac12)\); global min \(-\tfrac14\) at \((0,\tfrac12)\).

Eliminating \(x^2\) is valid because \(f\) only involves \(x^2\). The range of \(y\) on the circle is \([-1,1]\) — its endpoints are genuine candidates.

Edges: \(y=0\): \(f=x\in[0,2]\). \(y=2\): \(f=2-x\in[0,2]\). \(x=0\): \(f=y\). \(x=2\): \(f=2-y\). All linear — extremes at corners: \(f(0,0)=0,\ f(2,0)=2,\ f(0,2)=2,\ f(2,2)=0\).

Global max \(2\) at \((2,0),(0,2)\); global min \(0\) at \((0,0),(2,2)\).

STANDARD · 8 marks Q2. Find the global max and min of \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-4x\) on the disk \(x^2+y^2\le9\).

Solution

EVT applies. Interior: \((2,0)\) (\(4\lt9\) ✓), \(f=-4\).

Boundary: \(x^2+y^2=9\Rightarrow f=9-4x,\ x\in[-3,3]\): max \(21\) at \((-3,0)\), min \(-3\) at \((3,0)\).

Global max \(21\) at \((-3,0)\); global min \(-4\) at \((2,0)\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 13 marks Q3. Let \(S\) be the closed square bounded by \(x=0,\ x=2,\ y=0,\ y=2\), and \(f(x,y)=2x^2-y^2+2xy-4x\). (a) Determine the max and min of \(f\) on the boundary of \(S\). [7] (b) Determine the critical point(s) of \(f\) in the interior of \(S\). [4] (c) Determine the global max and min of \(f\) on \(S\). [2]

Solution

(a)

  • \(y=0\): \(2x^2-4x\), \(x\in[0,2]\): stationary \(x=1\) → \(-2\); ends \(0,0\).
  • \(y=2\): \(2x^2-4+4x-4x=2x^2-4\): min \(-4\) at \((0,2)\), max \(4\) at \((2,2)\).
  • \(x=0\): \(-y^2\), \(y\in[0,2]\): max \(0\) at \((0,0)\), min \(-4\) at \((0,2)\).
  • \(x=2\): \(8-y^2+4y-8=4y-y^2\): stationary at \(y=2\) (an endpoint); values \(0\) at \((2,0)\), \(4\) at \((2,2)\).

Boundary: max \(4\) at \((2,2)\), min \(-4\) at \((0,2)\).

(b) \(f_x=4x+2y-4=0,\ f_y=2x-2y=0\Rightarrow x=y,\ 6x=4\Rightarrow(\tfrac23,\tfrac23)\), inside ✓, \(f=-\tfrac43\). (\(D=(4)(-2)-4=-12\lt0\): a saddle.)

(c) By EVT, comparing: global max \(4\) at \((2,2)\); global min \(-4\) at \((0,2)\).

STRETCH · 10 marks Q4. Find the global max and min of \(f(x,y)=x^2y\) on the disk \(x^2+y^2\le3\).

Solution

Interior: \(f_x=2xy=0,\ f_y=x^2=0\Rightarrow x=0\), any \(y\): a whole line of critical points \((0,y)\), all with \(f=0\).

Boundary: \(x^2=3-y^2\), \(y\in[-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]\): \(f=(3-y^2)y=3y-y^3\). Derivative \(3-3y^2=0\Rightarrow y=\pm1\), \(x=\pm\sqrt2\): \(f=2\) at \(y=1\), \(f=-2\) at \(y=-1\). Ends \(y=\pm\sqrt3\) (\(x=0\)): \(f=0\).

Global max \(2\) at \((\pm\sqrt2,1)\); global min \(-2\) at \((\pm\sqrt2,-1)\).

8.4 · Lagrange multipliers — two variables

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — linear \(f\) on a circle: \(f=x+2y\) on \(x^2+y^2=5\)

\(g=x^2+y^2-5\). \(\nabla f=(1,2)=\lambda(2x,2y)\): \(1=2\lambda x\) ①, \(2=2\lambda y\) ②.

① shows \(\lambda\ne0\) and \(x\ne0\). ②÷①: \(\dfrac{y}{x}=2\Rightarrow y=2x\). Constraint: \(5x^2=5\Rightarrow x=\pm1\).

\(f(1,2)=5\), \(f(-1,-2)=-5\). Circle closed and bounded ⇒ max \(5\) at \((1,2)\), min \(-5\) at \((-1,-2)\).

Worked B — a zero case that matters: \(f=x^2y\) on \(x^2+2y^2=6\)

\(\nabla f=(2xy,\ x^2)=\lambda(2x,\ 4y)\): \(2xy=2\lambda x\) ①, \(x^2=4\lambda y\) ②.

① \(\Rightarrow 2x(y-\lambda)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(\lambda=y\).

  • \(x=0\): constraint \(2y^2=6\Rightarrow y=\pm\sqrt3\): \(f=0\).
  • \(\lambda=y\): ② \(x^2=4y^2\). Constraint: \(4y^2+2y^2=6\Rightarrow y=\pm1,\ x=\pm2\). \(f=x^2y=4y\): \(4\) at \((\pm2,1)\), \(-4\) at \((\pm2,-1)\).

Max \(4\) at \((\pm2,1)\); min \(-4\) at \((\pm2,-1)\). The \(x=0\) candidates (value \(0\)) are neither — but you only know that after comparing.

WARM-UP · 6 marks Q1. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the minimum of \(x^2+y^2\) subject to \(3x+4y=25\).

Solution

\(2x=3\lambda,\ 2y=4\lambda\Rightarrow y=\tfrac43x\). Constraint: \(3x+\tfrac{16}3x=25\Rightarrow\tfrac{25}{3}x=25\Rightarrow x=3,\ y=4\). \(f=25\).

Minimum: far along the line \(x^2+y^2\to\infty\), so the only candidate is the min (or compare: \(f(\tfrac{25}{3},0)=\tfrac{625}{9}\gt25\)). Min \(25\) at \((3,4)\) — i.e. the line is at distance \(5\) from the origin.

STANDARD · 10 marks Q2. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the max and min of \(f(x,y)=x^2-y^2\) on the ellipse \(x^2+4y^2=4\).

Solution

\(2x=2\lambda x\) ①, \(-2y=8\lambda y\) ②. ① \(\Rightarrow x(1-\lambda)=0\).

  • \(x=0\): \(4y^2=4\Rightarrow(0,\pm1)\), \(f=-1\).
  • \(\lambda=1\): ② \(-2y=8y\Rightarrow y=0\Rightarrow x=\pm2\): \((\pm2,0)\), \(f=4\).

Max \(4\) at \((\pm2,0)\); min \(-1\) at \((0,\pm1)\).

EXAM-LEVEL · 12 marks Q3. The temperature on a plate is \(T(x,y)=2x^2+4xy-y^2\). Epsilon walks on the circle \(x^2+y^2=5\). Use Lagrange multipliers to find the highest and lowest temperatures Epsilon meets, and where.

Solution

\((4x+4y,\ 4x-2y)=\lambda(2x,2y)\Rightarrow 2x+2y=\lambda x\) ①, \(2x-y=\lambda y\) ②.

If \(x=0\), ① gives \(y=0\) — not on the circle; so \(x\ne0\). Eliminate \(\lambda\) by cross-multiplying ①·\(y\) = ②·\(x\): \((2x+2y)y=(2x-y)x\Rightarrow 2x^2-3xy-2y^2=0\Rightarrow(2x+y)(x-2y)=0\).

  • \(x=2y\): \(5y^2=5\Rightarrow(2,1),(-2,-1)\): \(T=8+8-1=15\).
  • \(y=-2x\): \(5x^2=5\Rightarrow(1,-2),(-1,2)\): \(T=2-8-4=-10\).

Circle closed and bounded ⇒ highest \(15\) at \(\pm(2,1)\); lowest \(-10\) at \(\pm(1,-2)\).

STRETCH · 10 marks Q4. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the points on the curve \(x^2y=16\) closest to the origin, and the minimum distance.

Solution

Minimise \(f=x^2+y^2\) s.t. \(g=x^2y-16=0\): \(2x=2\lambda xy\) ①, \(2y=\lambda x^2\) ②.

On the curve \(x\ne0\) (else \(0=16\)), so ① gives \(\lambda y=1\). ②: \(2y=\dfrac{x^2}{y}\Rightarrow x^2=2y^2\). Constraint: \(2y^3=16\Rightarrow y=2,\ x^2=8\Rightarrow x=\pm2\sqrt2\).

\(f=8+4=12\). Closest points \((\pm2\sqrt2,2)\), minimum distance \(\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3\) (the curve goes off to infinity, so this candidate is the min).

8.5 · Lagrange multipliers — three variables

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — linear on a sphere: \(f=2x+y-2z\) on \(x^2+y^2+z^2=9\)

\((2,1,-2)=\lambda(2x,2y,2z)\Rightarrow x=\tfrac1\lambda,\ y=\tfrac1{2\lambda},\ z=-\tfrac1\lambda\) (\(\lambda\ne0\) since \(2=2\lambda x\)).

Constraint: \(\tfrac1{\lambda^2}\big(1+\tfrac14+1\big)=9\Rightarrow\lambda^2=\tfrac14\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm\tfrac12\).

\(\lambda=\tfrac12\): \((2,1,-2)\), \(f=4+1+4=9\). \(\lambda=-\tfrac12\): \((-2,-1,2)\), \(f=-9\).

Max \(9\) at \((2,1,-2)\), min \(-9\) at \((-2,-1,2)\). Check: \(R\|\mathbf a\|=3\cdot3=9\) ✓.

Worked B — product on a plane: maximise \(xy^2z^3\) with \(x+y+z=6\), \(x,y,z\gt0\)

\((y^2z^3,\ 2xyz^3,\ 3xy^2z^2)=\lambda(1,1,1)\). All positive, so divide freely:

② ÷ ①: \(\dfrac{2x}{y}=1\Rightarrow y=2x\). ③ ÷ ①: \(\dfrac{3x}{z}=1\Rightarrow z=3x\). Constraint: \(6x=6\Rightarrow(1,2,3)\).

\(f=1\cdot4\cdot27=108\). On the edges of the closed triangle \(x,y,z\ge0\) the product is \(0\), so by EVT max \(=108\) at \((1,2,3)\).

Pattern: maximising \(x^ay^bz^c\) on \(x+y+z=S\) splits \(S\) in the ratio \(a:b:c\). Great for checking.

WARM-UP · 6 marks Q1. Find the max and min of \(x+y+z\) on the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=12\).

Solution

\(1=2\lambda x=2\lambda y=2\lambda z\Rightarrow x=y=z\) (\(\lambda\ne0\)). \(3x^2=12\Rightarrow x=\pm2\). Max \(6\) at \((2,2,2)\); min \(-6\) at \((-2,-2,-2)\).

STANDARD · 10 marks Q2. Maximise \(f=xyz\) subject to \(2x+3y+z=18\), with \(x,y,z\gt0\).

Solution

\(yz=2\lambda\) ①, \(xz=3\lambda\) ②, \(xy=\lambda\) ③. ①÷③: \(\tfrac zx=2\Rightarrow z=2x\). ②÷③: \(\tfrac zy=3\Rightarrow z=3y\Rightarrow y=\tfrac{2x}{3}\).

Constraint: \(2x+2x+2x=18\Rightarrow x=3,\ y=2,\ z=6\). Max \(f=36\) at \((3,2,6)\) (\(f=0\) on the edges of the closed triangle).

EXAM-LEVEL · 12 marks Q3. The temperature on the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=8\) is \(T=xy^2z\). Use Lagrange multipliers to find the highest and lowest temperatures.

Solution

\(y^2z=2\lambda x\) ①, \(2xyz=2\lambda y\) ②, \(xy^2=2\lambda z\) ③.

Special cases: if any of \(x,y,z\) is \(0\), \(T=0\); if \(\lambda=0\), ① forces \(y=0\) or \(z=0\), so \(T=0\).

Main case (all non-zero): ①÷③: \(\dfrac zx=\dfrac xz\Rightarrow x^2=z^2\). ② gives \(\lambda=xz\); ①: \(y^2z=2x^2z\Rightarrow y^2=2x^2\). Constraint: \(x^2+2x^2+x^2=8\Rightarrow x^2=2,\ y^2=4,\ z^2=2\).

\(T=y^2(xz)=4xz\), with \(xz=\pm2\). Highest \(8\) (\(x,z\) same sign, e.g. \((\sqrt2,2,\sqrt2)\)); lowest \(-8\) (opposite signs).

STRETCH · 10 marks Q4. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the points of the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=9\) closest to and farthest from \(P=(4,2,4)\), and the two distances.

Solution

Minimise/maximise \(f=(x-4)^2+(y-2)^2+(z-4)^2\): \(2(x-4)=2\lambda x\Rightarrow x(1-\lambda)=4\), similarly \(y(1-\lambda)=2,\ z(1-\lambda)=4\). So \((x,y,z)=\tfrac{1}{1-\lambda}(4,2,4)\).

Constraint: \(\dfrac{36}{(1-\lambda)^2}=9\Rightarrow1-\lambda=\pm2\): points \((2,1,2)\) and \((-2,-1,-2)\).

\(f(2,1,2)=4+1+4=9\) → closest \((2,1,2)\), distance \(3\). \(f(-2,-1,-2)=36+9+36=81\) → farthest \((-2,-1,-2)\), distance \(9\). (Check: \(|OP|=6\), radius \(3\): \(6-3=3\), \(6+3=9\) ✓.)

8.6 · Applied optimisation word problems

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — open-top box of volume 32 with least material

Q. An open-top rectangular box must hold \(32\ \text{m}^3\). Use Lagrange multipliers to find the dimensions that minimise the surface area.

Base \(x\times y\), height \(z\) (all \(\gt0\)). Minimise \(S=xy+2xz+2yz\) s.t. \(xyz=32\).

$$ y+2z=\lambda yz\ ①,\qquad x+2z=\lambda xz\ ②,\qquad 2x+2y=\lambda xy\ ③. $$

① \(\times x\) − ② \(\times y\): \(2xz-2yz=0\Rightarrow x=y\). ② \(\times y\) − ③ \(\times z\) with \(x=y\): \(xy+2yz-2xz-2yz=0\Rightarrow x^2=2xz\Rightarrow x=2z\).

Constraint: \((2z)(2z)z=32\Rightarrow z^3=8\Rightarrow z=2\). Base \(4\times4\), height \(2\); area \(16+16+16=48\ \text{m}^2\).

Worked B — largest rectangle in an ellipse

Q. A rectangle with sides parallel to the axes is inscribed in the ellipse \(\tfrac{x^2}8+\tfrac{y^2}2=1\). Find its largest possible area.

Corner \((x,y)\) in the first quadrant; area \(A=4xy\). Maximise \(xy\) s.t. \(g=\tfrac{x^2}8+\tfrac{y^2}2-1=0\): \(y=\tfrac{\lambda x}4\), \(x=\lambda y\Rightarrow\) multiply: \(xy=\tfrac{\lambda^2}{4}xy\Rightarrow\lambda=2\) (positive case) \(\Rightarrow x=2y\).

Constraint: \(\tfrac{4y^2}8+\tfrac{y^2}2=y^2=1\Rightarrow y=1,\ x=2\). Max area \(4\cdot2\cdot1=8\).

WARM-UP · 6 marks Q1. Positive numbers \(x,y\) satisfy \(2x+y=12\). Use Lagrange multipliers to maximise \(x^2y\).

Solution

\(2xy=2\lambda,\ x^2=\lambda\Rightarrow 2xy=2x^2\Rightarrow y=x\) (\(x\gt0\)). \(3x=12\Rightarrow(4,4)\). Max \(64\).

STANDARD · 10 marks Q2. A closed rectangular box has total surface area \(96\ \text{cm}^2\). Use Lagrange multipliers to find the largest volume.

Solution

Max \(V=xyz\) s.t. \(2(xy+yz+xz)=96\). \(yz=2\lambda(y+z)\), \(xz=2\lambda(x+z)\), \(xy=2\lambda(x+y)\).

Multiply the first by \(x\), second by \(y\) and subtract: \(0=2\lambda z(x-y)\Rightarrow x=y\) (\(\lambda,z\ne0\)). Similarly \(y=z\). Cube: \(6x^2=96\Rightarrow x=4\). Max volume \(64\ \text{cm}^3\) (a \(4\) cm cube).

EXAM-LEVEL · 12 marks Q3. A rectangular box lies in the first octant with three faces on the coordinate planes and the opposite vertex \((x,y,z)\) on the plane \(x+2y+3z=6\). Use Lagrange multipliers to find the maximum volume.

Solution

Max \(V=xyz\) s.t. \(x+2y+3z=6\), \(x,y,z\gt0\): \(yz=\lambda,\ xz=2\lambda,\ xy=3\lambda\Rightarrow x=2y,\ x=3z\). Constraint: \(3x=6\Rightarrow x=2,\ y=1,\ z=\tfrac23\).

Max volume \(2\cdot1\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac43\) (volume \(\to0\) on the edges of the feasible triangle).

STANDARD · 8 marks Q4. A rectangular paddock of area \(800\ \text{m}^2\) is built along a straight river; no fence is needed along the river. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the least length of fencing.

Solution

Sides \(x\) (perpendicular to the river, two of them) and \(y\) (parallel). Minimise \(L=2x+y\) s.t. \(xy=800\): \(2=\lambda y,\ 1=\lambda x\Rightarrow y=2x\). \(2x^2=800\Rightarrow x=20,\ y=40\). Least fencing \(80\) m (\(L\to\infty\) as \(x\to0\) or \(\infty\), so the candidate is the min).

EXAM-LEVEL · 10 marks Q5. A closed cylindrical can must have volume \(16\pi\ \text{cm}^3\). Use Lagrange multipliers to find the radius and height minimising the surface area, and the minimum area.

Solution

Minimise \(S=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh\) s.t. \(\pi r^2h=16\pi\): \(4\pi r+2\pi h=2\pi\lambda rh\) ①, \(2\pi r=\pi\lambda r^2\) ②. ② (\(r\gt0\)): \(\lambda=\tfrac2r\). ①: \(4\pi r+2\pi h=4\pi h\Rightarrow h=2r\).

\(\pi r^2(2r)=16\pi\Rightarrow r^3=8\Rightarrow r=2,\ h=4\). \(S=8\pi+16\pi=24\pi\ \text{cm}^2\). (Same \(h=2r\) shape as 2024 S1 Q6 — the dual problem.)

Question 1 — Directional derivatives [12 marks] · Ch 5

A scientist is studying the growth of a population of bacteria in a petri dish. The population function is given by $$P(x,y)=500e^{-(x^2+y^2)/100}$$ where \((x,y)\) represents the position in the petri dish. Let \(Q=(1,2)\).
4 marks(a) Determine the rate of increase of population of bacteria at \(Q\) in the direction \((1,1)\).
Worked solution
"Rate of change in the direction \(\mathbf u\)" = directional derivative \(P_{\mathbf u}=\nabla P\cdot \dfrac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}\) (formula sheet). Compute \(\nabla P\), evaluate at \(Q\), dot with the unit vector.
Partial derivatives (chain rule on the exponential): $$P_x=500e^{-(x^2+y^2)/100}\cdot\left(-\frac{2x}{100}\right)=-10x\,e^{-(x^2+y^2)/100},\qquad P_y=-10y\,e^{-(x^2+y^2)/100}.$$ At \(Q=(1,2)\): \(x^2+y^2=5\), so \(e^{-5/100}=e^{-1/20}\): $$\nabla P(1,2)=e^{-1/20}\,(-10,\,-20).$$ Unit direction: \(\mathbf u=(1,1)\), \(\|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt2\). $$P_{\mathbf u}(1,2)=e^{-1/20}(-10,-20)\cdot\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt2}=\frac{-30}{\sqrt2}\,e^{-1/20}=-15\sqrt2\,e^{-1/20}.$$
\(P_{\mathbf u}(Q)=-\dfrac{30}{\sqrt2}e^{-1/20}=-15\sqrt2\,e^{-1/20}\) — negative, so the population actually decreases in the direction \((1,1)\).
Forgetting to divide by \(\|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt2\) is the #1 lost mark on directional-derivative questions. \((1,1)\) is not a unit vector.
Leave \(e^{-1/20}\) exact — no calculator, and the marker wants the exact form. Say in words what the sign means ("population decreasing in that direction").
3 marks(b) Determine the direction in which the population increases most rapidly at \(Q\).
Worked solution
Steepest ascent is always in the direction of \(\nabla P\) at the point.
$$\nabla P(1,2)=10e^{-1/20}(-1,-2),\qquad 10e^{-1/20}\gt0,$$ so the direction of \(\nabla P(1,2)\) is that of \((-1,-2)\).
Direction \((-1,-2)\), i.e. unit vector \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(-1,-2)\) — pointing back toward the centre of the dish.
You may strip off a positive scalar factor when giving a direction. Never strip a negative one — that flips the direction to steepest descent. Intuition check: \(P\) peaks at the origin, so "uphill" from \((1,2)\) must point toward \((0,0)\). ✓
2 marks(c) Determine the maximum rate of increase of the population at \(Q\).
Worked solution
Maximum rate of increase \(=\|\nabla P\|\) at the point.
$$\|\nabla P(1,2)\|=10e^{-1/20}\,\|(-1,-2)\|=10e^{-1/20}\sqrt{1+4}=10\sqrt5\,e^{-1/20}.$$
Maximum rate \(=10\sqrt5\,e^{-1/20}\).
3 marks(d) In which direction(s) is the population not changing at \(Q\)?
Worked solution
\(P_{\mathbf u}=0\iff \mathbf u\perp\nabla P\). In 2-D there are exactly two such unit directions (tangent to the contour through \(Q\)).
We need \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2)\) with \((-1,-2)\cdot(u_1,u_2)=0\iff u_1=-2u_2\). Taking \(u_2=\mp1\): $$\mathbf u=(2,-1)\quad\text{or}\quad \mathbf u=(-2,1).$$ Check: \((-10,-20)\cdot(2,-1)=-20+20=0\). ✓
The directions \((2,-1)\) and \((-2,1)\) (unit: \(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2,-1)\)).
"Direction(s)" — plural. Giving only one of the two opposite vectors typically costs a mark.

Question 2 — Removable discontinuity [12 marks] · Ch 4

12 marks A function \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) has a removable discontinuity at \((a,b)\) if \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)\) exists but \(f\) is not continuous at \((a,b)\). Let $$g(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sin(9x^2+9y^2)}{6x^2+6y^2} & \text{if }(x,y)\ne(0,0)\\[2mm] 0 & \text{if }(x,y)=(0,0)\end{cases}$$ Does \(g\) have a removable discontinuity at \((0,0)\)? Explain your answer.
Worked solution
The expression depends on \(x,y\) only through \(x^2+y^2\) ⇒ switch to polar: \(r^2=x^2+y^2\), and \((x,y)\to(0,0)\iff r\to0^+\) regardless of \(\theta\). Then use the one-variable standard limit \(\dfrac{\sin u}{u}\to1\).
Step 1 — compute the limit. With \(x^2+y^2=r^2\): $$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g(x,y)=\lim_{r\to0^+}\frac{\sin(9r^2)}{6r^2}=\lim_{r\to0^+}\frac{\sin(9r^2)}{9r^2}\cdot\frac{9}{6}=\frac32\lim_{u\to0}\frac{\sin u}{u}=\frac32\cdot1=\frac32,$$ where \(u=9r^2\to0\). Because the expression has no \(\theta\) in it, the limit is the same along every path — so the two-variable limit genuinely exists and equals \(\tfrac32\).
Step 2 — compare with the function value. \(g(0,0)=0\ne\tfrac32\), so \(g\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\).
Step 3 — conclude using the definition given. The limit exists but \(g\) is not continuous there.
Yes. \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g=\tfrac32\) exists but \(\tfrac32\ne g(0,0)=0\), so \(g\) has a removable discontinuity at \((0,0)\) (redefining \(g(0,0)=\tfrac32\) would make it continuous).
Testing a couple of straight-line paths and getting \(\tfrac32\) each time does not prove the limit exists — paths can only prove non-existence. The polar substitution (no \(\theta\) left) is what proves existence, and it is where most of the 12 marks sit.
Write the full structure the marker is looking for: (i) limit value with justification, (ii) \(g(0,0)\), (iii) "limit exists and \(\ne g(0,0)\) ⇒ removable discontinuity" — explicitly quoting the definition in the question.

Question 3 — Contours [12 marks] · Ch 3

4 marks(a) Match each function \(f(x,y)\) in the table below with its contour map (labelled 1, 2, 3). Enter 0 in the table if there is no match. No justification is required.
Function \(f(x,y)=\)Enter 0, 1, 2 or 3
\(y-x\)
\((y^2-1)\,x\)
\(y^2+x^2-xy\)
\(y^2-x\)
Map (1): the lines \(x=0\) and \(y=\pm1\) drawn as contours, with hyperbola-like curves in each region between them (bracket shapes opening away from the \(y\)-axis in the middle strip, bunched curves above \(y=1\) and below \(y=-1\)). Map (2): a family of nested parabolas all opening to the right (" ((( " shapes), symmetric about the \(x\)-axis. Map (3): concentric ellipses tilted along the line \(y=x\).
Worked solution
Identify the contour shape of each \(f=k\): linear → parallel lines; \(x=y^2-k\) → parabolas; positive-definite quadratic → ellipses; a product that vanishes on lines → those lines appear as the zero contour.
  • \(y-x=k\) → parallel straight lines of slope 1. None of the maps shows parallel lines → 0.
  • \((y^2-1)x=k\): the \(k=0\) contour is \(x=0\) or \(y=\pm1\) — exactly the three straight lines in map 1 → 1.
  • \(x^2-xy+y^2=k\): quadratic form with \(\det\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac12\\-\frac12&1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac34\gt0\) ⇒ ellipses, tilted because of the \(xy\) term (major axis along \(y=x\)) → 3.
  • \(y^2-x=k\iff x=y^2-k\) → parabolas opening in the \(+x\) direction → 2.
\(y-x\to0\); \(\ (y^2-1)x\to1\); \(\ y^2+x^2-xy\to3\); \(\ y^2-x\to2\).
Start with the zero contour \(f=0\): it's the fastest fingerprint. \((y^2-1)x=0\) giving three lines is unmistakable.
8 marks(b) Consider the function \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) given by the formula $$f(x,y)=x^2y^2+2x^2y+x^2+1.$$ Sketch the contours \(f(x,y)=1\) and \(f(x,y)=5\).
Worked solution
Set \(f=k\) and factorise. Here \(x^2\) is a common factor and the bracket is a perfect square: \(x^2(y^2+2y+1)=x^2(y+1)^2\).
Simplify first: \(f(x,y)=x^2(y+1)^2+1\).

Contour \(f=1\): $$x^2(y+1)^2=0\iff x=0\ \text{ or }\ y=-1.$$ Two straight lines: the \(y\)-axis and the horizontal line \(y=-1\).

Contour \(f=5\): $$x^2(y+1)^2=4\iff x(y+1)=\pm2\iff y=\frac{2}{x}-1\ \text{ or }\ y=-\frac{2}{x}-1.$$ These are rectangular hyperbolas with asymptotes \(x=0\) and \(y=-1\) (the \(f=1\) lines). Key points: \(y=\tfrac2x-1\) crosses the \(x\)-axis at \((2,0)\) (branches in the regions \(x\gt0,y\gt-1\) and \(x\lt0,y\lt-1\)); \(y=-\tfrac2x-1\) crosses at \((-2,0)\) (branches in \(x\lt0,y\gt-1\) and \(x\gt0,y\lt-1\)).
\(f=1\): the lines \(x=0\) and \(y=-1\). \(\ f=5\): the four branches of \(y=\pm\tfrac2x-1\), rectangular hyperbolas asymptotic to those lines, crossing the \(x\)-axis at \((\pm2,0)\).
Marker's note on the official solution: "Highlight y-axis and y = −1 on graph for f = 1." Both lines must be clearly drawn and labelled as the \(f=1\) contour — on the same axes as the \(f=5\) curves. Also label the intercepts \((\pm2,0)\) and name the curves ("rectangular hyperbolae").
Taking the square root and writing only \(x(y+1)=2\) loses half the \(f=5\) contour. \(x^2(y+1)^2=4\) gives both signs.

Question 4 — Chain rule: distance between two bugs [12 marks] · Ch 5

12 marks Atom Ant is travelling on a path given by the parametric equations $$x_1=4\cos t,\qquad y_1=2\sin t.$$ Billy Bug is travelling on a path given by the parametric equations $$x_2=2\sin 2t,\qquad y_2=3\cos 2t.$$ Determine the rate at which the distance between Atom Ant and Billy Bug is changing at \(t=\pi\). Hint: The distance between Atom Ant and Billy Bug at time \(t\) is \(s=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\).
Worked solution
\(s\) depends on four intermediate variables \(x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2\), each a function of \(t\) → multivariable chain rule with a tree diagram: \(\dfrac{ds}{dt}=\sum \dfrac{\partial s}{\partial(\cdot)}\dfrac{d(\cdot)}{dt}\).
Tree: \(s\to x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2\to t\), so $$\frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_1}\frac{dx_1}{dt}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_2}\frac{dx_2}{dt}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial y_1}\frac{dy_1}{dt}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial y_2}\frac{dy_2}{dt}.$$ Partial derivatives of \(s\): $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_1}=\frac{-(x_2-x_1)}{s},\quad \frac{\partial s}{\partial x_2}=\frac{x_2-x_1}{s},\quad \frac{\partial s}{\partial y_1}=\frac{-(y_2-y_1)}{s},\quad \frac{\partial s}{\partial y_2}=\frac{y_2-y_1}{s}.$$ Positions at \(t=\pi\): \(x_1=4\cos\pi=-4,\ y_1=2\sin\pi=0,\ x_2=2\sin2\pi=0,\ y_2=3\cos2\pi=3\). So \(x_2-x_1=4,\ y_2-y_1=3,\ s=\sqrt{16+9}=5\). $$\frac{\partial s}{\partial x_1}=-\frac45,\quad \frac{\partial s}{\partial x_2}=\frac45,\quad \frac{\partial s}{\partial y_1}=-\frac35,\quad \frac{\partial s}{\partial y_2}=\frac35.$$ Velocities: \(\dfrac{dx_1}{dt}=-4\sin t,\ \dfrac{dx_2}{dt}=4\cos2t,\ \dfrac{dy_1}{dt}=2\cos t,\ \dfrac{dy_2}{dt}=-6\sin 2t\); at \(t=\pi\): \(0,\ 4,\ -2,\ 0\). $$\frac{ds}{dt}\Big|_{t=\pi}=\left(-\tfrac45\right)(0)+\left(\tfrac45\right)(4)+\left(-\tfrac35\right)(-2)+\left(\tfrac35\right)(0)=\frac{16}{5}+\frac65=\frac{22}{5}.$$
\(\dfrac{ds}{dt}\Big|_{t=\pi}=\dfrac{22}{5}\) — the distance is increasing at \(4.4\) units per unit time.
Draw the tree diagram and write the four-term chain rule before differentiating — the official solution does exactly this, and it earns method marks even if an arithmetic slip follows.
Shortcut sanity check: \(\dfrac{ds}{dt}=\dfrac{(\mathbf p_2-\mathbf p_1)\cdot(\mathbf v_2-\mathbf v_1)}{s}=\dfrac{(4,3)\cdot\big((4,0)-(0,-2)\big)}{5}=\dfrac{(4,3)\cdot(4,2)}{5}=\dfrac{22}{5}.\) ✓
\(\dfrac{d}{dt}\sin2t=2\cos2t\) — don't drop the inner factor 2 (so \(dx_2/dt=4\cos2t\), not \(2\cos2t\)).

Question 5 — Critical points & global maximum [13 marks] · Ch 6

Let \(f(x,y)=3xe^{y}-x^3-e^{3y}\).
5 marks(a) Determine all critical points of \(f(x,y)\).
Worked solution
Critical points: solve \(f_x=0\) and \(f_y=0\) simultaneously. Use one equation to eliminate a variable.
$$f_x=3e^y-3x^2=0\ \Rightarrow\ x^2=e^y\quad(1)\qquad\qquad f_y=3xe^y-3e^{3y}=0\quad(2)$$ Substitute \(e^y=x^2\) from (1) into (2) (note \(e^{3y}=(e^y)^3=x^6\)): $$3x(x^2)-3x^6=0\ \Rightarrow\ 3x^3(1-x^3)=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0\ \text{or}\ x=1.$$
  • \(x=0\): (1) gives \(e^y=0\) — no solution (\(e^y\gt0\) always).
  • \(x=1\): \(e^y=1\Rightarrow y=0\).
The only critical point is \((1,0)\).
Don't list \((0,\,\cdot)\) as a critical point. You must explicitly reject \(x=0\) because \(e^y=0\) is impossible — the marker looks for that sentence.
4 marks(b) Classify the critical point(s).
Worked solution
Second-derivative test: \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\). \(D\gt0,f_{xx}\lt0\) ⇒ local max; \(D\gt0,f_{xx}\gt0\) ⇒ local min; \(D\lt0\) ⇒ saddle.
$$f_{xx}=-6x,\qquad f_{xy}=f_{yx}=3e^y,\qquad f_{yy}=3xe^y-9e^{3y}.$$ $$D(1,0)=\begin{vmatrix}-6&3\\3&3-9\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}-6&3\\3&-6\end{vmatrix}=36-9=27\gt0,\qquad f_{xx}(1,0)=-6\lt0.$$
\((1,0)\) is a local maximum, with \(f(1,0)=3-1-1=1\).
4 marks(c) Does \(f\) attain a global maximum? Explain your answer.
Worked solution
To show "no global max", exhibit a path along which \(f\to+\infty\). Freeze one variable to get a one-variable function.
Set \(y=0\): \(f(x,0)=3x-x^3-1\). As \(x\to-\infty\), the \(-x^3\) term dominates and \(-x^3\to+\infty\), so $$\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x,0)=+\infty.$$ So \(f\) takes arbitrarily large values and has no global maximum. (Concretely, \(f(-3,0)=-9+27-1=17\gt1=f(1,0)\).)
No — \(f(x,0)\to+\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\), so \(f\) is unbounded above and attains no global maximum. The local max \((1,0)\) is not global.
A function with a single critical point that is a local max need not have a global max in two variables (unlike one-variable calculus). This question is built exactly to punish that assumption.

Question 6 — Lagrange multipliers on a circle [12 marks] · Ch 6

12 marks The temperature at a point \((x,y)\) on a metal plate is \(T(x,y)=4x^2-4xy+y^2\). Epsilon, our pet ant, walks on the metal plate along a circle of radius 5 centred at the origin. Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the highest and lowest temperatures encountered by Epsilon.
Worked solution
Optimise \(T\) on a closed curve \(g=0\) → Lagrange: solve \(\nabla T=\lambda\nabla g\) together with \(g=0\), then evaluate \(T\) at every candidate. The circle is closed and bounded, so both extremes exist and are among the candidates.
Set-up: \(g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-25=0\). $$\nabla T=\lambda\nabla g:\quad (8x-4y,\ -4x+2y)=\lambda(2x,\ 2y).$$ $$8x-4y=2\lambda x\quad(1)\qquad -4x+2y=2\lambda y\quad(2)\qquad x^2+y^2=25\quad(3)$$ Solve. From (1): \(4x-2y=\lambda x\). But (2) says \(-(4x-2y)=2\lambda y\), so \(-\lambda x=2\lambda y\), i.e. $$\lambda(x+2y)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=0\ \text{ or }\ x=-2y.$$ Case \(\lambda=0\): (1) gives \(y=2x\). Then (3): \(x^2+4x^2=25\Rightarrow x=\pm\sqrt5\), giving \((\sqrt5,2\sqrt5)\) and \((-\sqrt5,-2\sqrt5)\).
Case \(x=-2y\): (3): \(4y^2+y^2=25\Rightarrow y=\pm\sqrt5\), giving \((-2\sqrt5,\sqrt5)\) and \((2\sqrt5,-\sqrt5)\) (here \(\lambda=5\)).
Evaluate — note \(T=(2x-y)^2\):
T( √5, 2√5) = 5(4 − 8 + 4) = 0 T(−√5, −2√5) = 5(4 − 8 + 4) = 0 T( 2√5, −√5) = 5(16 + 8 + 1) = 125 T(−2√5, √5) = 5(16 + 8 + 1) = 125
Highest temperature \(=125\) (at \(\pm(2\sqrt5,-\sqrt5)\)); lowest temperature \(=0\) (at \(\pm(\sqrt5,2\sqrt5)\)).
Never divide by \(\lambda\) or by \(x\)/\(y\) — you lose the \(\lambda=0\) case, which here gives the minimum. Factor instead: \(\lambda(x+2y)=0\).
The official solution writes "\(y=\pm5\)" in the \(x=-2y\) case — a slip: \(5y^2=25\) gives \(y=\pm\sqrt5\) (they then use \(\sqrt5(2,-1)\), which is correct). Check each candidate satisfies \(x^2+y^2=25\).
State why the extremes exist: \(T\) is continuous and the circle is closed and bounded, so the max and min are attained — and by Lagrange they occur at the candidate points.

Question 7 — Hexbug: motion along a parabola [13 marks] · Ch 7

A hexbug moves on a table so that its position at time \(t\) is $$\mathbf r(t)=16\sin(\pi t)\,\mathbf i+4\cos(2\pi t)\,\mathbf j.$$
4 marks(a) Find the velocity and acceleration of the hexbug at time \(t\).
Worked solution
\(\mathbf v=\mathbf r'(t)\), \(\mathbf a=\mathbf v'(t)\), differentiate component-wise (chain rule brings out \(\pi\), \(2\pi\)).
$$\mathbf v(t)=16\pi\cos(\pi t)\,\mathbf i-8\pi\sin(2\pi t)\,\mathbf j,$$ $$\mathbf a(t)=-16\pi^2\sin(\pi t)\,\mathbf i-16\pi^2\cos(2\pi t)\,\mathbf j.$$
\(\mathbf v=16\pi\cos(\pi t)\,\mathbf i-8\pi\sin(2\pi t)\,\mathbf j,\quad \mathbf a=-16\pi^2\sin(\pi t)\,\mathbf i-16\pi^2\cos(2\pi t)\,\mathbf j.\)
5 marks(b) Show that the hexbug moves along a parabolic path. Give the cartesian equation of the path. Hint: \(\cos(2\theta)=1-2\sin^2\theta\).
Worked solution
Eliminate \(t\): express \(y\) through \(\sin(\pi t)\) using the double-angle hint, then replace \(\sin(\pi t)=x/16\).
\(x=16\sin(\pi t)\Rightarrow \sin(\pi t)=\dfrac{x}{16}\). With \(\theta=\pi t\): $$y=4\cos(2\pi t)=4\big(1-2\sin^2(\pi t)\big)=4\left(1-2\cdot\frac{x^2}{256}\right)=4-\frac{8x^2}{256}=4-\frac{x^2}{32}.$$ This is a parabola (quadratic in \(x\)), opening downward with vertex \((0,4)\).
\(y=4-\dfrac{x^2}{32}\), traversed for \(-16\le x\le16\) (since \(|16\sin\pi t|\le16\)).
Add the domain restriction \(-16\le x\le 16\): the bug only covers the arc from \((-16,-4)\) to \((16,-4)\), sweeping back and forth. It shows the marker you understand the parametrisation, not just the algebra.
4 marks(c) Determine when the hexbug first returns to its starting point.
Worked solution
Find \(\mathbf r(0)\), then the smallest \(t\gt0\) with \(\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r(0)\) — both components must match simultaneously.
Start: \(\mathbf r(0)=(0,4)\). Need $$16\sin(\pi t)=0\ \text{ and }\ 4\cos(2\pi t)=4\iff \sin(\pi t)=0\ \text{ and }\ \cos(2\pi t)=1.$$ \(\sin(\pi t)=0\iff t\in\mathbb Z\); \(\cos(2\pi t)=1\iff t\in\mathbb Z\). The smallest positive solution is \(t=1\). (Motion: \(0\to\tfrac12\) the bug goes out to \((16,-4)\); \(\tfrac12\to1\) it comes back to \((0,4)\), moving leftward.)
The hexbug first returns to its starting point \((0,4)\) at \(t=1\).
Solving only one component (e.g. \(x=0\)) isn't enough in general — show both conditions hold at the same \(t\).

Question 8 — Helix: length, work, potential [14 marks] · Ch 7

Let \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=-\tfrac12x\,\mathbf i-\tfrac12y\,\mathbf j+\tfrac14\mathbf k\) be a force field in \(\mathbb R^3\). Let \(C\) be the path parametrised by $$\mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\ \sin t,\ t),\qquad t\in[0,3\pi].$$
3 marks(a) Determine the length of \(C\).
Worked solution
Arc length \(L=\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\) (formula sheet).
$$\mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 1),\qquad \|\mathbf r'(t)\|=\sqrt{\sin^2t+\cos^2t+1}=\sqrt2.$$ $$L=\int_0^{3\pi}\sqrt2\,dt=3\sqrt2\,\pi.$$
\(L=3\sqrt2\,\pi\).
\(C\) is a helix of radius 1 above the circle \(x^2+y^2=1\), making \(1\tfrac12\) turns (\(3\pi/2\pi\)). Constant speed \(\sqrt2\) makes the integral trivial.
5 marks(b) Use the work formula \(W=\int_a^b\mathbf F(t)\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\) to determine the work done by \(\mathbf F\) on a particle as it moves along the curve \(C\) from \(t=0\) to \(t=3\pi\).
Worked solution
Substitute the parametrisation into \(\mathbf F\), dot with \(\mathbf r'(t)\), integrate in \(t\).
On \(C\): \(\mathbf F(t)=\left(-\tfrac12\cos t,\ -\tfrac12\sin t,\ \tfrac14\right)\). $$\mathbf F(t)\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=\tfrac12\sin t\cos t-\tfrac12\sin t\cos t+\tfrac14=\tfrac14.$$ $$W=\int_0^{3\pi}\tfrac14\,dt=\frac{3\pi}{4}.$$
\(W=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\).
3 marks(c) Determine a potential function for \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)\).
Worked solution
Find \(f\) with \(\nabla f=\mathbf F\): integrate each component and match up.
\(f_x=-\tfrac12x\Rightarrow f=-\tfrac14x^2+h(y,z)\). \(f_y=h_y=-\tfrac12y\Rightarrow h=-\tfrac14y^2+k(z)\). \(f_z=k'(z)=\tfrac14\Rightarrow k=\tfrac14z+C\). $$f(x,y,z)=-\frac{x^2}{4}-\frac{y^2}{4}+\frac{z}{4}\quad(+C).$$ Check: \(\nabla f=\left(-\tfrac x2,-\tfrac y2,\tfrac14\right)=\mathbf F\). ✓
\(f(x,y,z)=-\tfrac14x^2-\tfrac14y^2+\tfrac14z\).
The official solution just "guesses" \(f\) — acceptable only if you then verify \(\nabla f=\mathbf F\). Show that check line.
3 marks(d) Use (c) to check your answer for (b).
Worked solution
Fundamental theorem for line integrals: \(\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\).
Start \(\mathbf r(0)=(1,0,0)\); end \(\mathbf r(3\pi)=(\cos3\pi,\sin3\pi,3\pi)=(-1,0,3\pi)\). $$W=f(-1,0,3\pi)-f(1,0,0)=\left(-\tfrac14-0+\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)-\left(-\tfrac14\right)=\frac{3\pi}{4}.\ ✓$$
\(W=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\), agreeing with (b).
\(\cos3\pi=-1\), not \(1\): the helix ends on the opposite side of the circle. Using \((1,0,3\pi)\) happens to give the same answer here (the \(x^2\) term hides the sign), but you'd lose the mark for the wrong endpoint.

Question 1 — Critical points & absolute extrema [12 marks] · Ch 6

Let \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be given by \(f(x,y)=x^3+y^3+3xy\).
8 marks(a) Find all the critical points of \(f\) and classify them.
Worked solution
Solve \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\), then classify each point with \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\).
$$f_x=3x^2+3y=0\ \Rightarrow\ y=-x^2\quad(1)\qquad f_y=3y^2+3x=0\quad(2)$$ Substitute (1) into (2): $$3x^4+3x=3x(x^3+1)=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0\ \text{or}\ x=-1.$$ \(x=0\Rightarrow y=0\); \(x=-1\Rightarrow y=-(-1)^2=-1\). Critical points: \((0,0)\) and \((-1,-1)\).
Classify: \(f_{xx}=6x,\ f_{xy}=3,\ f_{yy}=6y\), so $$D(x,y)=\begin{vmatrix}6x&3\\3&6y\end{vmatrix}=36xy-9.$$
  • \(D(0,0)=-9\lt0\) ⇒ saddle point.
  • \(D(-1,-1)=36-9=27\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}(-1,-1)=-6\lt0\) ⇒ local maximum, \(f(-1,-1)=-1-1+3=1\).
\((0,0)\): saddle point. \(\ (-1,-1)\): local maximum (value 1).
\(x^3+1=0\) has only the real root \(x=-1\) — don't invent \(x=1\). Solving \(x^3=-1\) carelessly as \(x=\pm1\) creates a bogus point \((1,-1)\), which fails \(f_y=0\) (\(3+3=6\ne0\)). Always substitute candidates back.
4 marks(b) Does the function \(f\) have an absolute maximum? Does the function \(f\) have an absolute minimum? Justify your answers.
Worked solution
Unbounded domain \(\mathbb R^2\): look for a path along which \(f\to+\infty\) (kills absolute max) and one along which \(f\to-\infty\) (kills absolute min).
Set \(y=0\): \(f(x,0)=x^3\). $$\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x,0)=+\infty,\qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x,0)=-\infty.$$ So \(f\) takes arbitrarily large positive and negative values.
\(f\) has no absolute maximum and no absolute minimum — along the \(x\)-axis \(f=x^3\) is unbounded above and below. (The local max \(f(-1,-1)=1\) is beaten by, e.g., \(f(2,0)=8\).)
Answer both questions explicitly — two separate sentences, one for max and one for min. That's where the 4 marks split.

Question 2 — Lagrange with a linear constraint [12 marks] · Ch 6

12 marks Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to find the minimum value of $$f(x,y)=2x^2+8y^2-4x-16y$$ subject to the constraint \(2x+4y=14\).
Worked solution
One constraint → \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) plus \(g=0\). The constraint is a line (unbounded), so you must justify that the point found is a minimum.
Set-up: \(g(x,y)=2x+4y-14=0\). $$(4x-4,\ 16y-16)=\lambda(2,\ 4).$$ $$4x-4=2\lambda\Rightarrow \lambda=2(x-1)\quad(1)\qquad 16y-16=4\lambda\Rightarrow\lambda=4(y-1)\quad(2)\qquad 2x+4y=14\quad(3)$$ Solve: from (1),(2): \(2(x-1)=4(y-1)\Rightarrow x=2y-1\) (4). Into (3): $$2(2y-1)+4y-14=0\Rightarrow 8y=16\Rightarrow y=2,\ x=3\quad(\lambda=4).$$ Value: \(f(3,2)=18+32-12-32=6\).
Justify it is a minimum. On the constraint \(x=7-2y\): $$f(7-2y,\,y)=16y^2-64y+70=16(y-2)^2+6\ \ge\ 6,$$ with equality only at \(y=2\). So \(6\) is the minimum.
Minimum value \(=6\), attained at \((3,2)\).
The official solution justifies "minimum" by comparing with another constraint point: \(f(7,0)=70\gt6\). That's the minimum acceptable argument (there's only one candidate and \(f\) is larger elsewhere, and \(f\to\infty\) along the line). The completed-square line above is airtight and takes one line — use it.
Lagrange only produces candidates. On an unbounded constraint, the candidate could be a max, a min, or neither — you must say why it is a minimum.

Question 3 — Direction of maximum increase [12 marks] · Ch 5

Consider \(T(x,y)=10+6\cos x\cos y+3\cos2x+4\cos3y\) near the point \(P=(\pi/3,\pi/3)\).
8 marks(a) Find the direction of the maximum increase and the maximum decrease of the function \(T(x,y)\) at \(P\), in the \(xy\)-plane.
Worked solution
Max increase: direction of \(\nabla T(P)\). Max decrease: direction of \(-\nabla T(P)\). So everything hinges on computing \(\nabla T(P)\) exactly with the unit-circle values.
$$T_x=-6\sin x\cos y-6\sin2x,\qquad T_y=-6\cos x\sin y-12\sin3y.$$ At \(P\): \(\sin\tfrac\pi3=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \cos\tfrac\pi3=\tfrac12,\ \sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \sin\pi=0\). $$T_x(P)=-6\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\tfrac12-6\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}-3\sqrt3=-\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{2},$$ $$T_y(P)=-6\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}-12\cdot0=-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}.$$ $$\nabla T(P)=-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\,(3,\,1).$$
Maximum increase: direction of \(\nabla T(P)\), i.e. \((-3,-1)\) (unit \(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3,1)\)). Maximum decrease: direction of \(-\nabla T(P)\), i.e. \((3,1)\) (unit \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3,1)\)). The maximum rate of increase is \(\|\nabla T(P)\|=\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\sqrt{10}=\tfrac{3\sqrt{30}}{2}\).
The factor \(-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\) is negative, so the direction of \(\nabla T(P)\) is \((-3,-1)\), not \((3,1)\). Writing "max increase in direction \((3,1)\)" swaps the two answers.
\(\dfrac{d}{dy}4\cos3y=-12\sin3y\) — chain-rule factor 3; and \(\sin(3\cdot\tfrac\pi3)=\sin\pi=0\), so that term vanishes.
4 marks(b) Find the directional derivative of \(T(x,y)\) at \(P\), in the direction \(\mathbf u=(2,-3)\).
Worked solution
\(T_{\mathbf u}(P)=\nabla T(P)\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}\), \(\|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt{4+9}=\sqrt{13}\).
$$T_{\mathbf u}(P)=-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}(3,1)\cdot\frac{(2,-3)}{\sqrt{13}}=-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{6-3}{\sqrt{13}}=-\frac{9\sqrt3}{2\sqrt{13}}=-\frac{9\sqrt{39}}{26}.$$
\(T_{\mathbf u}(P)=-\dfrac{9\sqrt3}{2\sqrt{13}}\;\left(=-\dfrac{9\sqrt{39}}{26}\right)\).
In the official solution the marker has corrected the formula to include \(/\|\mathbf u\|\) — again, the normalisation is the point being tested.

3 marks(a) Match each function \(f(x,y)\) in the table below with its graph (labelled Figure 1, 2, 3). Enter 0 in the table if there is no match. No justification is required.
\(f(x,y)\)0, 1, 2, or 3
\(x^2+y^2\)
\(\exp(x)\,(\cos(y)-1)\)
\(x^3\)
\(x^2-y^2\)
\(\exp(x)\cos(y)\)
Figure 1: a surface that oscillates in waves along the \(y\)-direction, with the wave amplitude growing rapidly in one \(x\)-direction (peaks reaching \(z\approx7\), troughs below 0) and flattening to near 0 in the other. Figure 2: an upward-opening bowl (circular paraboloid) with vertex at the origin. Figure 3: a surface constant along \(y\) (a "sheet" of parallel straight rulings) whose cross-section in \(x\) is an S-shaped cubic, rising above and falling below \(z=0\).
Worked solution
Look for fingerprints: no \(y\)-dependence ⇒ a cylinder-type sheet; \(e^x\) ⇒ growth in one \(x\)-direction; \(\cos y\) ⇒ waves in \(y\); sign (can it go positive/negative?) separates look-alikes.
  • \(x^2+y^2\): circular paraboloid bowl → 2.
  • \(e^x(\cos y-1)\): since \(\cos y-1\le0\), this is never positive. Figure 1 goes up to \(z\approx7\) → not it. No match → 0.
  • \(x^3\): independent of \(y\), cubic cross-section → 3.
  • \(x^2-y^2\): saddle — no figure is a saddle → 0.
  • \(e^x\cos y\): waves in \(y\) whose amplitude \(e^x\) grows in \(x\), taking both signs → 1.

Figure 1 looks like it could be either exponential–cosine function. The deciding test is sign: \(e^x(\cos y-1)\le0\) everywhere, so it can't produce the positive peaks. (In the scanned official solution the \(x^2-y^2\) entry is a hastily written 0 that looks like "9".)
9 marks(b) Consider the surface $$z=\frac14x^2-\frac19y^2-x+\frac29y+\frac89.$$ Sketch the contours \(z=1\) and \(z=-4\). In addition, for each contour, describe what kind of curve it is.
Hint: If \(z=0\), then \(y=(8-3x)/2\) or \(y=(3x-4)/2\).
Worked solution
Opposite-sign squares ⇒ hyperbolas. Complete the square in \(x\) and \(y\) to find the centre, then divide to standard form. The \(z=0\) lines in the hint are the common asymptotes.
Complete the square. Write \(\tfrac89=1-\tfrac19\): $$z=\tfrac14(x^2-4x+4)-\tfrac19(y^2-2y+1)=\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}-\frac{(y-1)^2}{9}.$$ Asymptotes (\(z=0\)): \(\dfrac{x-2}{2}=\pm\dfrac{y-1}{3}\iff y-1=\pm\tfrac32(x-2)\), i.e. \(y=\tfrac{3x-4}{2}\) and \(y=\tfrac{8-3x}{2}\) — exactly the hint. ✓

Contour \(z=1\): $$\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}-\frac{(y-1)^2}{9}=1.$$ A horizontal hyperbola (opens left/right) centred at \((2,1)\), \(a=2,\ b=3\), vertices \((0,1)\) and \((4,1)\). (Crosses the \(x\)-axis at \(x=2\pm\tfrac{2\sqrt{10}}{3}\).)

Contour \(z=-4\): $$\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}-\frac{(y-1)^2}{9}=-4\iff \frac{(y-1)^2}{36}-\frac{(x-2)^2}{16}=1.$$ A vertical hyperbola (opens up/down) centred at \((2,1)\), semi-axes 6 (in \(y\)) and 4 (in \(x\)), vertices \((2,7)\) and \((2,-5)\). (Crosses the \(y\)-axis at \(y=1\pm3\sqrt5\).) Same asymptotes.
\(z=1\): horizontal hyperbola \(\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}-\frac{(y-1)^2}{9}=1\), centre \((2,1)\), vertices \((0,1),(4,1)\). \(\ z=-4\): vertical hyperbola \(\frac{(y-1)^2}{36}-\frac{(x-2)^2}{16}=1\), centre \((2,1)\), vertices \((2,7),(2,-5)\). Both have asymptotes \(y=\tfrac{3x-4}{2},\ y=\tfrac{8-3x}{2}\).
For full sketch marks: draw the dashed asymptotes first (from the hint), mark the centre \((2,1)\) and the vertices, then draw each hyperbola hugging the asymptotes. Label each curve with its \(z\)-value and name it ("hyperbola, opening left–right").
Positive vs negative level: \(z\gt0\) gives the hyperbola opening along \(x\) (the positive-square variable), \(z\lt0\) flips it to open along \(y\). Drawing both the same way is the classic error.

Question 5 — Arc length of a conical spiral [10 marks] · Ch 7

10 marks Compute the length of the curve in \(\mathbb R^3\) given by the parametric equations: $$x=e^{-t}\cos t,\quad y=e^{-t}\sin t,\quad z=e^{-t},\quad t\in[0,1].$$
Worked solution
Arc length \(L=\int_0^1\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\). Product rule on each component; factor out \(e^{-t}\) and let \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\) collapse the square root.
$$\mathbf r'(t)=\big(-e^{-t}(\cos t+\sin t),\ e^{-t}(\cos t-\sin t),\ -e^{-t}\big).$$ $$\|\mathbf r'(t)\|^2=e^{-2t}\Big[(\cos t+\sin t)^2+(\cos t-\sin t)^2+1\Big].$$ Expand: \((\cos t+\sin t)^2+(\cos t-\sin t)^2=2(\cos^2t+\sin^2t)=2\) (the \(\pm2\sin t\cos t\) cancel). So $$\|\mathbf r'(t)\|=e^{-t}\sqrt{2+1}=\sqrt3\,e^{-t}.$$ $$L=\int_0^1\sqrt3\,e^{-t}\,dt=\sqrt3\left[-e^{-t}\right]_0^1=\sqrt3\,(1-e^{-1})=\frac{\sqrt3\,(e-1)}{e}.$$
\(L=\sqrt3\left(1-\dfrac1e\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt3(e-1)}{e}\).
10 marks for a one-line integral means the marks are in the algebra: show the product-rule derivative, the expansion, and the cancellation of the cross terms explicitly.
Sign in the antiderivative: \(\int e^{-t}dt=-e^{-t}\). Getting \(\sqrt3(e^{-1}-1)\lt0\) should immediately alarm you — lengths are positive.

Question 6 — Sketch a path; work by a conservative field [14 marks] · Ch 7

A field \(\mathbf F(x,y)=(4x^3y,\ x^4)\) moves a particle along the curve given parametrically by $$x(t)=-4t^2+8t+1,\qquad y(t)=2t,\qquad t\in[0,2].$$
10 marks(a) Sketch the path of the particle.
Worked solution
Eliminate \(t\) to get a Cartesian equation, complete the square to find the vertex, then mark the start/end points and direction of travel for \(t\in[0,2]\).
Eliminate \(t\): \(t=\tfrac y2\), so $$x=-4\left(\tfrac y2\right)^2+8\cdot\tfrac y2+1=-y^2+4y+1=-(y^2-4y+4)+5=5-(y-2)^2.$$ A parabola opening in the \(-x\) direction (left), vertex \((5,2)\), axis \(y=2\).
Key points: \(t=0\): \((1,0)\) (start). \(t=1\): \((5,2)\) (vertex). \(t=2\): \((1,4)\) (end). Since \(y=2t\) increases, the particle moves upward along the arc. Only \(0\le y\le4\) is travelled (the rest of the parabola, e.g. its \(y\)-intercepts \(2\pm\sqrt5\), is not part of the path).
Arc of \(x=5-(y-2)^2\) from \((1,0)\) through the vertex \((5,2)\) to \((1,4)\), traversed upward (direction arrows).
For 10 marks the sketch must show: Cartesian equation, vertex, both endpoints labelled with their \(t\)-values, direction arrows, and the travelled portion emphasised (official solution highlights it and draws the rest of the parabola faintly).
4 marks(b) Determine the work done by the field.
Worked solution
Check conservative first (\(P_y=Q_x\)). If so, find a potential \(\varphi\) and use \(W=\varphi(\text{end})-\varphi(\text{start})\) — avoids a nasty polynomial integral.
\(P=4x^3y,\ Q=x^4\): \(\ \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=4x^3=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\) on all of \(\mathbb R^2\) ⇒ \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
Potential: \(\varphi_x=4x^3y\Rightarrow\varphi=x^4y+h(y)\). \(\varphi_y=x^4+h'(y)=x^4\Rightarrow h'=0\). Take \(\varphi(x,y)=x^4y\). $$W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\varphi(1,4)-\varphi(1,0)=1^4\cdot4-0=4.$$
\(W=4\).
Say "conservative on \(\mathbb R^2\) (simply connected), so path-independent" before using the potential — that sentence is what justifies ignoring the shape of the path you just sketched.
Brute force \(\int_0^2\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\) involves \((−4t^2+8t+1)^4\) — correct (it also gives 4) but a time sink with high error risk and no calculator.

Question 7 — Reverse-engineering from the gradient [14 marks] · Ch 5

Let \(f:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R\) be given by $$f(x,y,z)=axy^2+byz+cz^2x^3$$ where \(a,b,c\) are constants, and let \(P\) denote the point \((1,2,-1)\).
10 marks(a) Suppose the directional derivative at the point \(P\) attains its maximum value of 64 in the direction of the positive \(z\)-axis. Find the values \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\).
Worked solution
Two facts in one sentence: (i) max directional derivative occurs in the direction of \(\nabla f\) ⇒ \(\nabla f(P)\) is a positive multiple of \(\mathbf k=(0,0,1)\); (ii) the max value is \(\|\nabla f(P)\|=64\). Together: \(\nabla f(P)=(0,0,64)\).
$$\nabla f=\big(ay^2+3cz^2x^2,\ \ 2axy+bz,\ \ by+2czx^3\big).$$ At \(P=(1,2,-1)\): $$\nabla f(P)=(4a+3c,\ \ 4a-b,\ \ 2b-2c).$$ Set \(\nabla f(P)=(0,0,64)\): $$4a+3c=0\quad(1),\qquad 4a-b=0\quad(2),\qquad 2b-2c=64\iff b-c=32\quad(3).$$ From (2): \(b=4a\); from (3): \(c=b-32=4a-32\). Into (1): \(4a+3(4a-32)=0\Rightarrow16a=96\Rightarrow a=6\). Then \(b=24,\ c=-8\).
Check: \(\nabla f(P)=(24-24,\ 24-24,\ 48+16)=(0,0,64)\). ✓
\(a=6,\ b=24,\ c=-8\), so \(f(x,y,z)=6xy^2+24yz-8z^2x^3\).
Using only "\(f_z(P)=64\)" gives one equation (\(b-c=32\)) — not enough. The word maximum is what forces the \(x\)- and \(y\)-components of \(\nabla f(P)\) to be zero. That's where most of the 10 marks are.
\(\partial_x(cz^2x^3)=3cz^2x^2\) — at \(P\) this is \(3c(1)(1)=3c\) (the official working briefly writes \(8a+3c\) before correcting to \(4a+3c\); \(ay^2=4a\)).
4 marks(b) Let \(f\) denote the function in part (a). Determine the direction(s) in which \(f\) has zero slopes at \(P\).
Worked solution
Zero slope \(\iff f_{\mathbf u}(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\mathbf u=0\iff \mathbf u\perp\nabla f(P)\). In 3-D that's a whole plane of directions.
With \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\), \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\): $$(0,0,64)\cdot(u_1,u_2,u_3)=64u_3=0\iff u_3=0.$$
Every direction parallel to the \(xy\)-plane: \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2,0)\) with \(u_1^2+u_2^2=1\), i.e. \(\mathbf u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)\), \(\theta\in[0,2\pi)\).
In 2-D there are two zero-slope directions; in 3-D there are infinitely many (a full circle of unit vectors). Giving just \((1,0,0)\) and \((0,1,0)\) is incomplete.

Question 8 — A limit that fools every straight line [14 marks] · Ch 4

Let \(g:\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:\ y=-x^{3/5}\}\to\mathbb R\) be given by $$g(x,y)=\frac{2y^3x^2}{y^5+x^3}.$$
4 marks(a) Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{y\to0}g(\alpha y,y)\), where \(\alpha\in\mathbb R\).
Worked solution
Substitute the line \(x=\alpha y\), cancel powers of \(y\), then let \(y\to0\). Treat \(\alpha=0\) separately.
$$g(\alpha y,y)=\frac{2y^3\alpha^2y^2}{y^5+\alpha^3y^3}=\frac{2\alpha^2y^5}{y^3(y^2+\alpha^3)}=\frac{2\alpha^2y^2}{y^2+\alpha^3}.$$
  • \(\alpha=0\): \(g(0,y)=0\) for \(y\ne0\), so the limit is \(0\).
  • \(\alpha\ne0\): as \(y\to0\), numerator \(\to0\) and denominator \(\to\alpha^3\ne0\), so the limit is \(0\).
\(\displaystyle\lim_{y\to0}g(\alpha y,y)=0\) for every \(\alpha\in\mathbb R\).
10 marks(b) Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g(x,y)\) does not exist.
Worked solution
All straight lines give 0 (part a, plus the \(x\)-axis). To show non-existence you need a curved path giving a different value. Aim the path at the "danger curve" \(y=-x^{3/5}\) where the denominator vanishes: hug it closely so the denominator becomes as small as the numerator.
Path 1 — the \(x\)-axis \(y=0\) (not covered by \(x=\alpha y\)): $$\lim_{x\to0}g(x,0)=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{0}{x^3}=0.\qquad(*)$$ Path 2 — hug the excluded curve. The official solution uses \(y=-x^{3/5}\big(x^{4/5}+1\big)=-x^{3/5}-x^{7/5}\), which lies in the domain and passes through \((0,0)\). Parametrise it cleanly with \(x=t^5\) (\(t\to0^+\)), so \(x^{3/5}=t^3,\ x^{7/5}=t^7\): $$x=t^5,\qquad y=-t^3(1+t^4).$$ Numerator: \(2y^3x^2=2\big(-t^3(1+t^4)\big)^3t^{10}=-2t^{19}(1+t^4)^3.\)
Denominator: \(y^5+x^3=-t^{15}(1+t^4)^5+t^{15}=-t^{15}\big[(1+t^4)^5-1\big].\) $$g=\frac{-2t^{19}(1+t^4)^3}{-t^{15}\big[(1+t^4)^5-1\big]}=2(1+t^4)^3\cdot\frac{t^4}{(1+t^4)^5-1}.$$ With \(u=t^4\to0\): \(\dfrac{(1+u)^5-1}{u}=5+10u+10u^2+5u^3+u^4\to5\) (binomial expansion). Hence $$\lim_{t\to0^+}g=2\cdot1\cdot\frac15=\frac25.\qquad(**)$$ Conclusion: \((*)\) gives \(0\) and \((**)\) gives \(\tfrac25\). Two paths to \((0,0)\) give different limits, so the limit does not exist.
Along \(y=0\): limit \(0\). Along \(y=-x^{3/5}(x^{4/5}+1)\): limit \(\tfrac25\). Since \(0\ne\tfrac25\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g(x,y)\) does not exist.
The official solution reaches \(\tfrac25\) by L'Hôpital on \(\dfrac{x^{4/5}}{(x^{4/5}+1)^5-1}\). The substitution \(x=t^5\) + binomial expansion above avoids fractional powers entirely — less error-prone under exam pressure.
Why this path? The denominator \(y^5+x^3\) vanishes on \(y=-x^{3/5}\). Along \(y=-t^3+kt^7\) (\(x=t^5\)) the denominator shrinks like \(5kt^{19}\), exactly matching the numerator's \(t^{19}\), and the limit comes out as \(-\tfrac{2}{5k}\) — every \(k\ne0\) gives a different value. Official path is \(k=-1\).
Part (a) is a deliberate trap: "all lines through the origin give 0" does not mean the limit is 0. The structure of the question (4 marks lines, 10 marks non-existence) is the examiner telling you so.

What 2023 teaches

1 · The syllabus core is fixed — and it's all multivariable

Neither 2023 paper has an ODE question. Both papers hit the same six skills: gradient/directional derivative (S1 Q1, S2 Q3, S2 Q7), 2-D limits (S1 Q2, S2 Q8), contours/graphs (S1 Q3, S2 Q4), critical points + global extrema (S1 Q5, S2 Q1), Lagrange (S1 Q6, S2 Q2) and curves/line integrals (S1 Q7–8, S2 Q5–6). Master these six routines and you've covered ~90% of the marks.

2 · Recurring mark-savers

  • Normalise \(\mathbf u\) in every directional derivative (S1 Q1a, S2 Q3b).
  • Watch the sign of the scalar you pull out of \(\nabla f\) — it decides increase vs decrease (S2 Q3a).
  • Factor, never divide in Lagrange systems: \(\lambda(x+2y)=0\) keeps the \(\lambda=0\) case (S1 Q6).
  • Justify min/max on unbounded constraints (S2 Q2) and use a one-variable slice to disprove global extrema (S1 Q5c, S2 Q1b).
  • Paths prove non-existence only; polar with no \(\theta\) proves existence (S1 Q2 vs S2 Q8).
  • Check conservative first — a potential function turns a 20-minute integral into one subtraction (S1 Q8d, S2 Q6b).
  • Sketches: complete the square, draw asymptotes/lines, label vertices, intercepts, \(z\)-values, and direction of travel.

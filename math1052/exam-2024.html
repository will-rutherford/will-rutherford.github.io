S1 Question 1 — Directional derivative & steepest ascent [12 marks] · Ch 5

Panda Po is practising the art of levitation in a room where the temperature (in degrees Celsius) at a point \((x,y,z)\) is given by $$T(x,y,z)=\frac{xyz}{1+x^2+y^2+z^2}.$$ Panda Po rises to the point \(P=(1,1,1)\).
6 marks(a) Determine the rate of change of temperature (with respect to distance) at \(P\) in the direction of the origin.
Worked solution
"Rate of change with respect to distance in a direction" = directional derivative \(T_{\mathbf u}=\nabla T\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}\) (formula sheet). Two jobs: (1) the gradient at \(P\); (2) the direction vector from \(P\) to the origin, normalised.
Direction. From \(P=(1,1,1)\) to \(O=(0,0,0)\): \(\mathbf u=O-P=(-1,-1,-1)\), \(\|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt3\).
Gradient. Write \(T=(xyz)(1+x^2+y^2+z^2)^{-1}\) and use the product rule: $$T_x=\frac{yz}{1+x^2+y^2+z^2}-\frac{xyz\cdot 2x}{(1+x^2+y^2+z^2)^2}.$$ At \((1,1,1)\) the denominator \(1+1+1+1=4\): $$T_x(1,1,1)=\frac14-\frac{2}{16}=\frac18.$$ \(T\) is symmetric in \(x,y,z\) (swapping any two variables leaves it unchanged), and \(P\) has equal coordinates, so \(T_y(1,1,1)=T_z(1,1,1)=\tfrac18\). $$\nabla T(1,1,1)=\left(\tfrac18,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\right).$$ Directional derivative. $$T_{\mathbf u}(1,1,1)=\left(\tfrac18,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\right)\cdot\frac{(-1,-1,-1)}{\sqrt3}=-\frac{3}{8\sqrt3}=-\frac{\sqrt3}{8}.$$
\(T_{\mathbf u}(P)=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{8}\ \approx -0.217\) °C per unit distance — the temperature decreases as Po heads towards the origin.
Direction "towards the origin" is \(O-P=(-1,-1,-1)\), not \(P=(1,1,1)\). Using \((1,1,1)\) flips the sign and loses the answer marks. And normalise — a non-unit vector gives \(-\tfrac38\), which is wrong by a factor \(\sqrt3\).
Saying "by symmetry" is accepted by the marker (the official solution does exactly this) — but say why it's symmetric. It saves computing \(T_y,T_z\) separately.
3 marks(b) Po moves in the direction of maximum temperature increase. Determine the unit vector in this direction.
Worked solution
Maximum increase ⇔ direction of \(\nabla T\). Unit vector = \(\nabla T/\|\nabla T\|\).
$$\nabla T(1,1,1)=\tfrac18(1,1,1),\qquad \hat{\mathbf u}=\frac{(1,1,1)}{\sqrt3}.$$
\(\hat{\mathbf u}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)=\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right).\)
Justify in one sentence: "\(T_{\mathbf u}=\|\nabla T\|\cos\theta\) is largest when \(\theta=0\), i.e. \(\mathbf u\) parallel to \(\nabla T\)." Then normalise.
3 marks(c) Determine the rate at which the temperature rises at \(P\) in the direction obtained in part (b).
Worked solution
In the gradient direction the directional derivative equals \(\|\nabla T\|\) (since \(\cos 0=1\)).
$$T_{\hat{\mathbf u}}=\nabla T\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=\|\nabla T(1,1,1)\|=\sqrt{3\cdot\tfrac1{64}}=\frac{\sqrt3}{8}.$$
Maximum rate \(=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{8}\approx0.217\) °C per unit distance.
Consistency check: (a) and (c) are negatives of each other because the direction to the origin, \(-(1,1,1)/\sqrt3\), is exactly the steepest-descent direction here.

S1 Question 2 — Does the limit exist? [12 marks] · Ch 4

Consider the function on \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\) defined by $$f(x,y)=\frac{x^3y}{2x^6+y^2}.$$
4 marks(a) What value does \(f(x,y)\) approach as \((x,y)\) approaches \((0,0)\) along a straight line \(y=mx\), where \(m\in\mathbb R\) is a constant?
Worked solution
Path limit: substitute the path, simplify to one variable, let \(x\to0\). Treat the special case \(m=0\) separately if the algebra divides by \(m\).
$$f(x,mx)=\frac{x^3(mx)}{2x^6+m^2x^2}=\frac{mx^4}{x^2(2x^4+m^2)}=\frac{mx^2}{2x^4+m^2}.$$ If \(m\ne0\): as \(x\to0\), numerator \(\to0\) and denominator \(\to m^2\ne0\), so \(f\to0\).
If \(m=0\): \(f(x,0)=\dfrac{0}{2x^6}=0\) for all \(x\ne0\), so \(f\to0\).
Along every line \(y=mx\): \(f(x,y)\to0\).
The official solution explicitly adds "(Note: if \(m=0\) …)". Covering the degenerate case is what "justify" means here.
4 marks(b) What value does \(f(x,y)\) approach as \((x,y)\) approaches \((0,0)\) along a parabola \(y=kx^2\), where \(k\in\mathbb R\) is a constant?
Worked solution $$f(x,kx^2)=\frac{x^3(kx^2)}{2x^6+k^2x^4}=\frac{kx^5}{x^4(2x^2+k^2)}=\frac{kx}{2x^2+k^2}.$$ If \(k\ne0\): as \(x\to0\), \(f\to\dfrac{0}{k^2}=0\). If \(k=0\): \(f(x,0)=0\).
Along every parabola \(y=kx^2\): \(f(x,y)\to0\).
Agreement along lines and parabolas does not prove the limit exists. The question is setting you up: the "balance" path is where the two denominator terms have the same power.
4 marks(c) Does \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) exist? Explain your answer.
Worked solution
Pick the path that makes the denominator terms the same order: \(2x^6\) and \(y^2\) match when \(y^2\sim x^6\), i.e. \(y=x^3\). Then use the two-path test.
Along \(y=x^3\): $$f(x,x^3)=\frac{x^3\cdot x^3}{2x^6+x^6}=\frac{x^6}{3x^6}=\frac13\quad(x\ne0).$$ So \(f\to\tfrac13\) along \(y=x^3\), but \(f\to0\) along \(y=mx\) (part (a)).
Two paths into \((0,0)\) give different limits (\(\tfrac13\ne0\)), so \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) does not exist.
Finding the path: set \(y=kx^n\) and choose \(n\) so numerator and denominator have equal degree. Here \(x^3\cdot x^n\) vs \(x^6 + x^{2n}\): \(n=3\) gives \(x^6/x^6\). More generally \(y=kx^3\) gives \(f=k/(2+k^2)\), a different value for each \(k\) — overwhelming evidence.
Write the conclusion sentence naming both paths and both values. "Limit DNE" with no comparison loses the explanation marks.

S1 Question 3 — Contour sketch & minimum [12 marks] · Ch 3

Consider the surface $$z=4y^2-8y+x^2+2x+6.$$
8 marks(a) Sketch the contour which passes through \((x,y)=(-1,2)\). Label all \(x\)-intercepts and \(y\)-intercepts of this contour.
Worked solution
Contour through a point: (1) evaluate \(z\) at the point to get the level \(c\); (2) set \(z=c\) and complete the square in each variable; (3) identify the conic (centre, semi-axes); (4) find intercepts by setting \(x=0\) and \(y=0\).
Level. \(z(-1,2)=4(4)-8(2)+1-2+6=16-16-1+6=5.\)
Complete the square in \(4y^2-8y+x^2+2x+6=5\): $$4(y^2-2y)+(x^2+2x)+1=0$$ $$4\big[(y-1)^2-1\big]+\big[(x+1)^2-1\big]+1=0$$ $$(x+1)^2+4(y-1)^2=4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{(x+1)^2}{4}+\frac{(y-1)^2}{1}=1.$$ This is an ellipse, centre \((-1,1)\), semi-axis \(2\) in the \(x\)-direction (major axis parallel to the \(x\)-axis, length 4) and semi-axis \(1\) in the \(y\)-direction (minor axis, length 2). Vertices: \((-3,1),(1,1),(-1,0),(-1,2)\).
\(x\)-intercepts (\(y=0\)): \(\dfrac{(x+1)^2}{4}+1=1\Rightarrow (x+1)^2=0\Rightarrow x=-1\). The ellipse just touches the \(x\)-axis at \((-1,0)\).
\(y\)-intercepts (\(x=0\)): \(4y^2-8y+6=5\Rightarrow 4y^2-8y+1=0\), $$y=\frac{8\pm\sqrt{64-16}}{8}=\frac{8\pm4\sqrt3}{8}=1\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\;\approx\;1.87,\ 0.13.$$
Ellipse \(\dfrac{(x+1)^2}{4}+(y-1)^2=1\), centre \((-1,1)\). \(x\)-intercept: \((-1,0)\) only (tangent). \(y\)-intercepts: \(\left(0,\,1\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\).
The single \(x\)-intercept is where students lose marks: they expect two, get \((x+1)^2=0\), and panic. It is a tangency — label it and say so. Also: the major axis is along \(x\) (the coefficient 4 sits on the \(y\)-term, making \(y\) the short direction).
On your sketch mark the centre, the four vertices and all intercepts with exact values. Exact \(1\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) — don't only give decimals (no calculator anyway).
4 marks(b) Find the minimum value of \(z\) on the surface.
Worked solution
A sum of squares plus a constant has minimum = the constant, attained where every square is zero. Reuse the completed square from (a).
$$z=4(y-1)^2-4+(x+1)^2-1+6=4(y-1)^2+(x+1)^2+1.$$ Both squares are \(\ge0\) and both vanish at \((x,y)=(-1,1)\), so \(z\ge1\) with equality there.
Minimum value \(z=1\), at \((x,y)=(-1,1)\) (the vertex of the elliptic paraboloid).
Cross-check with calculus: \(z_x=2x+2=0,\ z_y=8y-8=0\Rightarrow(-1,1)\); \(D=2\cdot8-0=16>0,\ z_{xx}>0\) ⇒ minimum. Either argument earns the marks; completing the square is faster.

S1 Question 4 — Related rates (chain rule) [12 marks] · Ch 5

At a certain instant the legs of a right angled triangle have lengths 2 cm and 4 cm, and they are increasing at the rates of 1 cm/s and 2 cm/s respectively.
5 marks(a) How fast is the area of the triangle changing?
Worked solution
A quantity depends on two variables that both depend on \(t\) → chain rule \(\dfrac{dA}{dt}=\dfrac{\partial A}{\partial x}\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\dfrac{\partial A}{\partial y}\dfrac{dy}{dt}\) (formula sheet). Define variables first.
Let the legs be \(x\) and \(y\) (cm). At the instant: \(x=2,\ y=4,\ \dfrac{dx}{dt}=1,\ \dfrac{dy}{dt}=2\).
Area \(A=\tfrac12xy\), so \(A_x=\tfrac12y,\ A_y=\tfrac12x\): $$\frac{dA}{dt}=\tfrac12y\frac{dx}{dt}+\tfrac12x\frac{dy}{dt}=\tfrac12(4)(1)+\tfrac12(2)(2)=2+2=4.$$
\(\dfrac{dA}{dt}=4\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}\) (increasing).
Units matter — area rate is cm²/s. Drawing the labelled triangle (as the official solution does) sets up the variables in 5 seconds.
7 marks(b) How fast is the perimeter of the triangle changing?
Worked solution
Perimeter includes the hypotenuse \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) (Pythagoras) — that's the only non-trivial derivative.
$$P=x+y+\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\qquad P_x=1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\quad P_y=1+\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}.$$ At \((2,4)\): \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5\). $$\frac{dP}{dt}=\Big(1+\frac{2}{2\sqrt5}\Big)(1)+\Big(1+\frac{4}{2\sqrt5}\Big)(2)=3+\frac{2+8}{2\sqrt5}=3+\frac{5}{\sqrt5}=3+\sqrt5.$$
\(\dfrac{dP}{dt}=3+\sqrt5\ \text{cm/s}\ (\approx5.24\ \text{cm/s}).\)
Don't treat the hypotenuse's rate as \(1+2=3\) or "\(\sqrt{1^2+2^2}\)". Rates don't combine by Pythagoras — only lengths do. Differentiate \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) properly: \(\tfrac{1}{2}(x^2+y^2)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x\).
Simplify surds fully: \(\tfrac{10}{\sqrt{20}}=\tfrac{10}{2\sqrt5}=\sqrt5\).

S1 Question 5 — Global extrema on a triangle [13 marks] · Ch 6

Let \(f(x,y)=2+2x+2y-x^2-y^2\). Let \(S\) be the closed triangular region in the first quadrant bounded by the lines \(x=0,\ y=0,\ y=9-x\).
6 marks(a) Determine the maximum and minimum values of \(f\) on the boundary of \(S\).
Worked solution
Closed bounded region → extreme values occur at interior critical points or on the boundary (Extreme Value Theorem). Boundary: parametrise each edge as a one-variable function, find its critical points and its endpoints (the corners).
Edge 1: \(x=0,\ 0\le y\le9\). \(g(y)=f(0,y)=2+2y-y^2\). \(g'(y)=2-2y=0\Rightarrow y=1\). $$f(0,0)=2,\qquad f(0,1)=3,\qquad f(0,9)=2+18-81=-61.$$ Edge 2: \(y=0,\ 0\le x\le9\). \(f(x,0)=2+2x-x^2\) — identical by symmetry (\(f\) is symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\)): $$f(0,0)=2,\qquad f(1,0)=3,\qquad f(9,0)=-61.$$ Edge 3: \(y=9-x,\ 0\le x\le9\). $$h(x)=2+2x+2(9-x)-x^2-(9-x)^2=2+18-x^2-(81-18x+x^2)=-2x^2+18x-61.$$ \(h'(x)=-4x+18=0\Rightarrow x=\tfrac92,\ y=\tfrac92\): $$f\!\left(\tfrac92,\tfrac92\right)=2+9+9-\tfrac{81}{4}-\tfrac{81}{4}=20-\tfrac{81}{2}=-\tfrac{41}{2}.$$ Endpoints of this edge are the corners \((0,9),(9,0)\): value \(-61\).
Candidate(0,0)(0,1), (1,0)(9/2, 9/2)(0,9), (9,0)
\(f\)23−41/2−61
On the boundary: maximum \(3\) at \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\); minimum \(-61\) at \((0,9)\) and \((9,0)\).
Don't forget the corners. Each edge's endpoints must be tested even though \(g'\ne0\) there — the global minimum \(-61\) lives only at corners.
4 marks(b) Determine the critical point(s) of \(f\) in the interior of \(S\).
Worked solution
Critical point: solve \(f_x=0\) and \(f_y=0\) simultaneously; check it lies inside \(S\).
$$f_x=2-2x=0,\qquad f_y=2-2y=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(x,y)=(1,1).$$ \((1,1)\) is interior: \(x>0,\ y>0,\ x+y=2\lt9\). Value \(f(1,1)=2+2+2-1-1=4\).
(Classification, for completeness: \(f_{xx}=-2,\ f_{yy}=-2,\ f_{xy}=0\Rightarrow D=4>0,\ f_{xx}\lt0\): local maximum.)
One interior critical point: \((1,1)\), with \(f(1,1)=4\).
3 marks(c) Determine the global maximum and minimum values of \(f\) on \(S\).
Worked solution
Compare the interior value with the boundary extremes; largest wins, smallest loses.
Candidates: interior \(4\); boundary max \(3\), boundary min \(-61\).
Global maximum \(4\) at \((1,1)\); global minimum \(-61\) at \((0,9)\) and \((9,0)\).
Open the question with the justification sentence the official solution writes first: "Max & min are achieved at a critical point in the interior of the region or on the boundary of the region." That sentence is a method mark.
Intuition: \(f=4-(x-1)^2-(y-1)^2\) is a downward paraboloid with peak 4 at \((1,1)\); the farthest points of \(S\) from \((1,1)\) are the corners \((9,0),(0,9)\) at squared distance \(65\), giving \(4-65=-61\). ✓

S1 Question 6 — Lagrange: the optimal closed cylinder [12 marks] · Ch 6

12 marks A closed cylinder has height \(h\) and a circular base of radius \(r\). The cylinder has a fixed surface area, \(A\). Use Lagrange multipliers to show that the volume is maximised by choosing $$r=\sqrt{\frac{A}{6\pi}},\qquad h=\sqrt{\frac{2A}{3\pi}}.$$
Worked solution
"Maximise \(V\) subject to fixed area" → Lagrange: \(\nabla V=\lambda\nabla g\) plus the constraint. Closed means two circular ends: \(A=2\pi rh+2\pi r^2\).
Set up. Maximise \(V(r,h)=\pi r^2h\) subject to \(g(r,h)=2\pi rh+2\pi r^2=A\), with \(r>0,\ h>0\). $$\nabla V=(2\pi rh,\ \pi r^2),\qquad \nabla g=(2\pi h+4\pi r,\ 2\pi r).$$ Lagrange equations \(\nabla V=\lambda\nabla g\): $$\text{(1)}\ \ 2\pi rh=\lambda(2\pi h+4\pi r),\qquad \text{(2)}\ \ \pi r^2=\lambda(2\pi r),\qquad \text{(3)}\ \ 2\pi rh+2\pi r^2=A.$$ From (2): \(r\ne0\) (otherwise \(V=0\), and a cylinder needs \(r>0\)), so divide by \(\pi r\): \(\ r=2\lambda.\)
Into (1): \(2\pi(2\lambda)h=\lambda(2\pi h+8\pi\lambda)\). Since \(\lambda=r/2\ne0\), divide by \(\lambda\): $$4\pi h=2\pi h+8\pi\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ 2\pi h=8\pi\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ h=4\lambda.$$ Into (3): $$A=2\pi(2\lambda)(4\lambda)+2\pi(2\lambda)^2=16\pi\lambda^2+8\pi\lambda^2=24\pi\lambda^2\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=\sqrt{\frac{A}{24\pi}}\ (\lambda>0).$$ Therefore $$r=2\sqrt{\frac{A}{24\pi}}=\sqrt{\frac{4A}{24\pi}}=\sqrt{\frac{A}{6\pi}},\qquad h=4\sqrt{\frac{A}{24\pi}}=\sqrt{\frac{16A}{24\pi}}=\sqrt{\frac{2A}{3\pi}}.$$ Why it's a maximum. On the constraint, \(h=\dfrac{A-2\pi r^2}{2\pi r}\), so \(V=\tfrac12 r(A-2\pi r^2)\) for \(0\lt r\lt\sqrt{A/(2\pi)}\). \(V\to0\) at both ends of this interval and \(V>0\) in between, so the only critical point must be the maximum.
\(r=2\lambda,\ h=4\lambda,\ \lambda=\sqrt{A/(24\pi)}\) ⇒ \(r=\sqrt{\dfrac{A}{6\pi}},\ h=\sqrt{\dfrac{2A}{3\pi}}\) — note \(h=2r\): the best closed can is as tall as it is wide.
Using the open cylinder area \(2\pi rh+\pi r^2\) gives a different answer (\(h=r\)). Read "closed" — two ends.
"Show that" questions: you must reach the given expressions by algebra, not substitute them in and check. And justify each division (\(r\ne0\), \(\lambda\ne0\)) — dividing by something that might be zero is a classic lost mark.
Expected check: \(h/r=\sqrt{\tfrac{2A}{3\pi}\cdot\tfrac{6\pi}{A}}=\sqrt4=2\). A quick ratio like this catches algebra slips before you hand in.

S1 Question 7 — Speed and distance on a space curve [13 marks] · Ch 7

A jacaranda flower tumbles to the ground along the curve $$\mathbf r(t)=t^2\cos(t)\,\mathbf i+t^2\sin(t)\,\mathbf j+(16-2t)\,\mathbf k.$$
8 marks(a) Determine the speed of the jacaranda flower at \(t=\pi\).
Worked solution
Speed \(=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\). Differentiate each component (product rule on \(t^2\cos t\), \(t^2\sin t\)), then square and add — expect the cross terms to cancel.
$$\mathbf r'(t)=\big(2t\cos t-t^2\sin t,\ \ 2t\sin t+t^2\cos t,\ \ -2\big).$$ Square and add: $$\begin{aligned}\|\mathbf r'(t)\|^2&=(2t\cos t-t^2\sin t)^2+(2t\sin t+t^2\cos t)^2+4\\ &=4t^2\cos^2t-4t^3\sin t\cos t+t^4\sin^2t+4t^2\sin^2t+4t^3\sin t\cos t+t^4\cos^2t+4\\ &=4t^2(\cos^2t+\sin^2t)+t^4(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)+4\\ &=t^4+4t^2+4=(t^2+2)^2.\end{aligned}$$ So \(\|\mathbf r'(t)\|=t^2+2\) (positive, so no \(\pm\)). At \(t=\pi\):
Speed at \(t=\pi\): \(\ \pi^2+2\) (≈ 11.87 units per unit time).
Don't plug \(t=\pi\) in before simplifying — you'll still get the right number, but the simplification \((t^2+2)^2\) is what makes part (b) trivial. Also: speed is a scalar; the velocity vector \(\mathbf r'(\pi)=(-2\pi,-\pi^2,-2)\) is not the answer.
Check: \(\|\mathbf r'(\pi)\|^2=4\pi^2+\pi^4+4=(\pi^2+2)^2\). ✓
5 marks(b) Determine the distance travelled by the jacaranda flower in the time interval \(0\le t\le2\).
Worked solution
Distance travelled = arc length \(L=\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\) (formula sheet).
$$L=\int_0^2(t^2+2)\,dt=\left[\frac{t^3}{3}+2t\right]_0^2=\frac83+4=\frac{20}{3}.$$
Distance \(=\dfrac{20}{3}\) units.

S1 Question 8 — Conservative field & work along a spiral [14 marks] · Ch 7

Let \(\mathbf F(x,y)=(3x^2\sin y+y^2e^{xy})\,\mathbf i+(1+x^3\cos y+(xy+1)e^{xy})\,\mathbf j\) be a force field in \(\mathbb R^2\). Let \(C\) be the path parametrised by $$\mathbf r(t)=(t\cos t,\ t\sin t),\qquad t\in\left[0,\tfrac\pi2\right].$$
4 marks(a) Is \(\mathbf F\) a conservative field? Explain your answer.
Worked solution
\(\mathbf F=P\,\mathbf i+Q\,\mathbf j\) on all of \(\mathbb R^2\) (simply connected, \(P,Q\) have continuous partials): conservative ⇔ \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\).
\(P=3x^2\sin y+y^2e^{xy}\), \(Q=1+x^3\cos y+(xy+1)e^{xy}\). $$\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=3x^2\cos y+2ye^{xy}+xy^2e^{xy}.$$ $$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}=3x^2\cos y+\underbrace{y\,e^{xy}}_{\text{from }(xy+1)'}+\underbrace{(xy+1)\,y\,e^{xy}}_{\text{from }(e^{xy})'}=3x^2\cos y+2ye^{xy}+xy^2e^{xy}.$$
\(P_y=Q_x\) everywhere on \(\mathbb R^2\), and \(\mathbb R^2\) is simply connected, so \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
\(\partial_y(y^2e^{xy})\) needs the product rule: \(2ye^{xy}+y^2\cdot xe^{xy}\). Missing either term makes the test "fail" and you'll wrongly conclude non-conservative. Expand both sides fully and compare term by term.
3 marks(b) Sketch the path \(C\). Indicate the starting point and the direction of travel.
Worked solution
Recognise the form: \((t\cos t,t\sin t)\) is a point at polar angle \(\theta=t\) and distance \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=t\) from the origin — an Archimedean spiral \(r=\theta\).
\(x^2+y^2=t^2\), so the distance from the origin equals the angle. Key points:
\(t\)0\(\pi/4\)\(\pi/2\)
\((x,y)\)\((0,0)\)\(\left(\tfrac{\pi\sqrt2}{8},\tfrac{\pi\sqrt2}{8}\right)\approx(0.56,0.56)\)\((0,\tfrac\pi2)\)
A spiral arc in the first quadrant starting at the origin (\(t=0\)), sweeping anticlockwise while moving outward, ending at \((0,\pi/2)\) on the positive \(y\)-axis.
Label start, end, and an arrow for direction — that's the 3 marks. Mention "distance from origin \(=t\) = angle" to justify the shape.
7 marks(c) Determine the work done by \(\mathbf F\) on a particle as it moves along the path \(C\) from \(t=0\) to \(t=\pi/2\).
Worked solution
Conservative field ⇒ find a potential \(f\) with \(\nabla f=\mathbf F\), then use the Fundamental Theorem for line integrals: \(W=f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\). Direct \(\int\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf r'\,dt\) here would be a nightmare.
Potential. Integrate \(P\) in \(x\): $$f=\int(3x^2\sin y+y^2e^{xy})\,dx=x^3\sin y+ye^{xy}+g(y).$$ Differentiate in \(y\) and match \(Q\): $$f_y=x^3\cos y+e^{xy}+xye^{xy}+g'(y)=1+x^3\cos y+(xy+1)e^{xy}\ \Rightarrow\ g'(y)=1,\ g(y)=y.$$ $$f(x,y)=x^3\sin y+ye^{xy}+y\quad(+\text{const}).$$ Endpoints. \(\mathbf r(0)=(0,0)\), \(\mathbf r(\pi/2)=\big(\tfrac\pi2\cos\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2\sin\tfrac\pi2\big)=(0,\tfrac\pi2)\). $$W=f\!\left(0,\tfrac\pi2\right)-f(0,0)=\left(0+\tfrac\pi2e^{0}+\tfrac\pi2\right)-0=\pi.$$
\(W=\pi\).
Always check your potential: \(f_x=3x^2\sin y+y\cdot ye^{xy}\) ✓. The "\(+y\)" in \(f\) comes from the lone "1" in \(Q\) — easy to drop.
Integrating \(y^2e^{xy}\) with respect to \(x\): \(y\) is a constant, so \(\int y^2e^{xy}\,dx=y^2\cdot\dfrac{e^{xy}}{y}=ye^{xy}\). Don't write \(y^2e^{xy}/x\).

S2 Question 1 — Isotherm sketch & maximum temperature [12 marks] · Ch 3

A thin metal plate is located in the \(xy\)-plane. The temperature \(T\) at a point \((x,y)\) on the metal plate is given by $$T(x,y)=\frac{100}{3+x^2+2y^2-4y}.$$
8 marks(a) Sketch the contour of \(T\) which passes through the point \((x,y)=(2,1)\). Label the \(x\)-intercepts and \(y\)-intercepts of the contour.
Worked solution
Level through a point: evaluate \(T\) there, set \(T=c\), clear the fraction, complete the square → conic.
Level. \(T(2,1)=\dfrac{100}{3+4+2-4}=\dfrac{100}{5}=20.\)
Contour \(T=20\): \(\dfrac{100}{3+x^2+2y^2-4y}=20\iff 3+x^2+2(y^2-2y)=5.\)
Complete the square in \(y\): \(x^2+2\big[(y-1)^2-1\big]+3=5\), so $$x^2+2(y-1)^2=4\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{(y-1)^2}{2}=1.$$ An ellipse centred at \((0,1)\), semi-axes \(2\) (along \(x\)) and \(\sqrt2\) (along \(y\)).
\(x\)-intercepts (\(y=0\)): \(\dfrac{x^2}{4}+\dfrac12=1\Rightarrow x^2=2\Rightarrow x=\pm\sqrt2.\)
\(y\)-intercepts (\(x=0\)): \((y-1)^2=2\Rightarrow y=1\pm\sqrt2.\)
Ellipse \(\dfrac{x^2}{4}+\dfrac{(y-1)^2}{2}=1\), centre \((0,1)\); \(x\)-intercepts \((\pm\sqrt2,0)\); \(y\)-intercepts \((0,1\pm\sqrt2)\approx(0,2.41),(0,-0.41)\). It passes through \((2,1)\) ✓.
Marking scheme: 1 mark finding the level through \((2,1)\) · 3 marks correct working to the ellipse equation · 2 marks \(x,y\)-intercepts · 2 marks graph.
\(1-\sqrt2\lt0\): the lower \(y\)-intercept is below the \(x\)-axis. Your sketch must dip below the axis there, otherwise it contradicts your own intercepts.
4 marks(b) Determine the maximum temperature on the metal plate.
Worked solution
\(T=\dfrac{100}{D(x,y)}\) with \(D>0\): \(T\) is largest where the denominator \(D\) is smallest. Complete the square in \(D\).
$$D(x,y)=3+x^2+2y^2-4y=x^2+2(y-1)^2+1\ \ge 1,$$ with equality only at \(x=0,\ y=1\). Since \(D\ge1>0\) everywhere, \(T\) is defined on the whole plate and $$T\le\frac{100}{1}=100,\quad\text{attained at }(0,1).$$
Maximum temperature \(=100\) (°), at the point \((0,1)\).
Marking scheme: 1 mark "T is maximised when the denominator is minimised" · 2 marks complete the square and find the point · 1 mark the value 100. Write that first sentence — it's a free mark.

S2 Question 2 — Limits: DNE and polar [12 marks] · Ch 4

In each of the following cases, evaluate the limit, if it exists. If the limit does not exist, explain why.
6 marks(a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,0)}\frac{(x-1)^2\ln(y+2)}{(x-1)^2+y^2}.\)
Worked solution
Denominator \(\to0\) with the quadratic-ratio shape \(\dfrac{(x-1)^2}{(x-1)^2+y^2}\) → suspect DNE. Use the two-path test with paths through \((1,0)\): the vertical line \(x=1\) and the horizontal line \(y=0\).
Path 1: \(x=1\), \(y\to0\). $$\lim_{y\to0}\frac{0\cdot\ln(y+2)}{0+y^2}=\lim_{y\to0}\frac{0}{y^2}=0.$$ Path 2: \(y=0\), \(x\to1\). $$\lim_{x\to1}\frac{(x-1)^2\ln2}{(x-1)^2}=\ln2.$$
The limit does not exist: along \(x=1\) the function tends to \(0\), along \(y=0\) it tends to \(\ln2\ne0\).
Marking scheme: 4 marks correct working choosing two or more paths through \((1,0)\) giving different values · 2 marks the conclusion sentence.
Paths must go through the target point \((1,0)\), not the origin. \(y=x\) is not a path to \((1,0)\)! Shift first: with \(X=x-1\), the expression is \(\dfrac{X^2\ln(y+2)}{X^2+y^2}\) as \((X,y)\to(0,0)\), and "lines" are \(y=mX=m(x-1)\) (e.g. \(y=x-1\) gives \(\tfrac12\ln2\)).
6 marks(b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{e^{x^2+y^2}-1}{x^2+y^2}.\)
Worked solution
The expression depends only on \(x^2+y^2\) → polar coordinates \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) make \(\theta\) vanish, reducing to a one-variable limit. Then L'Hôpital (0/0).
With \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), \((x,y)\to(0,0)\iff r\to0^+\) (for every \(\theta\)): $$\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{e^{x^2+y^2}-1}{x^2+y^2}=\lim_{r\to0}\frac{e^{r^2}-1}{r^2}\quad\left(\tfrac00\right).$$ L'Hôpital: $$\lim_{r\to0}\frac{2re^{r^2}}{2r}=\lim_{r\to0}e^{r^2}=1.$$ The result does not depend on \(\theta\), so it is the same along every approach.
\(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{e^{x^2+y^2}-1}{x^2+y^2}=1.\)
Marking scheme: 3 marks setting up polar coordinates · 3 marks correct working and conclusion. Write "for all \(\theta\)" — it is the reason polar proves existence.
Equivalent one-liner: put \(s=x^2+y^2\to0^+\); \(\lim_{s\to0}\frac{e^s-1}{s}=1\) (the derivative of \(e^s\) at 0).

S2 Question 3 — Tangent line by implicit differentiation [12 marks] · Ch 5

12 marks Let \(C\) be the curve defined by the equation $$y\ln x+xe^y-e^2=0,\quad\text{and } x>0.$$ Show that the equation of the tangent line to the curve \(C\) at the point \(x=1\) is $$y=-\left(1+\frac{2}{e^2}\right)x+3+\frac{2}{e^2}.$$
Worked solution
Curve given implicitly by \(F(x,y)=0\) → slope \(y'=-F_x/F_y\) (formula sheet). Need the point first: find \(y\) at \(x=1\).
Step 1 — the point. At \(x=1\): \(y\ln1+1\cdot e^y-e^2=0\Rightarrow e^y=e^2\Rightarrow y=2\). Point \((1,2)\).
Step 2 — set up. \(F(x,y)=y\ln x+xe^y-e^2\), the curve is the level set \(F=0\), and $$\frac{dy}{dx}(1,2)=-\frac{F_x(1,2)}{F_y(1,2)}.$$ Step 3 — partials. $$F_x=\frac yx+e^y\ \Rightarrow\ F_x(1,2)=2+e^2,\qquad F_y=\ln x+xe^y\ \Rightarrow\ F_y(1,2)=0+e^2=e^2\ne0.$$ $$\frac{dy}{dx}(1,2)=-\frac{2+e^2}{e^2}=-\left(1+\frac{2}{e^2}\right).$$ Step 4 — tangent line. $$y-2=-\left(1+\frac2{e^2}\right)(x-1)\ \Rightarrow\ y=-\left(1+\frac2{e^2}\right)x+\left(1+\frac2{e^2}\right)+2=-\left(1+\frac2{e^2}\right)x+3+\frac2{e^2}.$$
Point \((1,2)\), slope \(-\left(1+\dfrac2{e^2}\right)\), giving \(y=-\left(1+\dfrac2{e^2}\right)x+3+\dfrac2{e^2}\) as required. ∎
Marking scheme: 2 marks finding \(y=2\) · 2 marks setting up \(F=0\) and writing the formula for \(dy/dx(1,2)\) · 5 marks correct partials and slope · 3 marks the tangent line. Notice 5 of 12 are for the partial derivatives — show them.
\(F_x\) of \(y\ln x\) is \(y/x\) (treat \(y\) as constant); \(F_y\) of \(xe^y\) is \(xe^y\). Mixing these up is the common slip. And note \(F_y(1,2)\ne0\) — that's what guarantees the tangent isn't vertical.

S2 Question 4 — Gradient & directional derivative (trig) [12 marks] · Ch 5

Consider the function \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) given by $$f(x,y)=10+6\cos x\cos y+3\cos2x+4\cos4y.$$
3 marks(a) Determine \(\nabla f(x,y)\).
Worked solution
\(\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)\). Chain rule on \(\cos2x\) and \(\cos4y\) (the inner-derivative factors 2 and 4).
$$f_x=-6\sin x\cos y-6\sin2x,\qquad f_y=-6\cos x\sin y-16\sin4y.$$
\(\nabla f(x,y)=\big(-6\sin x\cos y-6\sin2x,\ \ -6\cos x\sin y-16\sin4y\big).\)
Marking scheme: 1 mark writing \(\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)\) · 2 marks correct partials.
\(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}4\cos4y=-16\sin4y\), not \(-4\sin4y\). Forgetting the inner factor is the #1 slip on this question.
6 marks(b) Determine the directional derivative \(f_{\mathbf v}(\pi/4,\pi/4)\), where \(\mathbf v=2\mathbf i+\mathbf j\).
Worked solution
\(f_{\mathbf v}=\nabla f\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}\) (formula sheet). Evaluate the gradient at \((\pi/4,\pi/4)\) using exact trig values.
Values: \(\sin\frac\pi4=\cos\frac\pi4=\frac{\sqrt2}2\), \(\sin\frac\pi2=1\), \(\sin\pi=0\). $$f_x\!\left(\tfrac\pi4,\tfrac\pi4\right)=-6\cdot\tfrac12-6\cdot1=-9,\qquad f_y\!\left(\tfrac\pi4,\tfrac\pi4\right)=-6\cdot\tfrac12-16\cdot0=-3.$$ \(\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt5\). $$f_{\mathbf v}\!\left(\tfrac\pi4,\tfrac\pi4\right)=(-9,-3)\cdot\frac{(2,1)}{\sqrt5}=\frac{-18-3}{\sqrt5}=-\frac{21}{\sqrt5}.$$
\(f_{\mathbf v}(\pi/4,\pi/4)=-\dfrac{21}{\sqrt5}=-\dfrac{21\sqrt5}{5}\approx-9.39.\)
Marking scheme: 1 mark formula · 3 marks correct \(f_x,f_y\) at the point · 2 marks correct slope (the final dot product).
\(\mathbf v=(2,1)\) is not a unit vector — divide by \(\sqrt5\). Answer \(-21\) (unnormalised) is marked wrong.
3 marks(c) Determine the direction of greatest increase of \(f\) at the point \((\pi/4,\pi/4)\).
Worked solution
Greatest increase ⇔ direction of \(\nabla f\) at that point.
$$\nabla f\!\left(\tfrac\pi4,\tfrac\pi4\right)=(-9,-3),\qquad\text{unit direction }\ \frac{(-9,-3)}{\sqrt{90}}=\frac{(-3,-1)}{\sqrt{10}}.$$
Direction of \(\nabla f=(-9,-3)\), i.e. unit vector \(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3,1)\). (Maximum rate \(=\|\nabla f\|=3\sqrt{10}\).)
The MS accepts "the direction of the gradient vector \((-9,-3)\)" for all 3 marks. Giving the unit vector as well costs 10 seconds and protects you if the question had said "unit vector".

S2 Question 5 — Lagrange on a sphere [12 marks] · Ch 6

12 marks Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to find the minimum and maximum values of the function $$f(x,y,z)=x-2y+2z$$ on the sphere defined by $$x^2+y^2+z^2=1.$$
Worked solution
Extremise \(f\) on a constraint surface \(g=0\) → \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) + constraint: four equations in \(x,y,z,\lambda\). The sphere is closed and bounded, so max and min exist and are among the solutions.
Formulation. \(g(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-1=0\). $$\nabla f=(1,-2,2),\qquad\nabla g=(2x,2y,2z).$$ Lagrange equations \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\): $$1=2\lambda x,\qquad -2=2\lambda y,\qquad 2=2\lambda z,\qquad x^2+y^2+z^2=1.$$ The first equation forces \(\lambda\ne0\) and \(x\ne0\); similarly \(y\ne0,\ z\ne0\). So $$\lambda=\frac1{2x}=-\frac1y=\frac1z.$$ From \(\frac1{2x}=-\frac1y\): \(y=-2x\). From \(-\frac1y=\frac1z\): \(z=-y=2x\).
Constraint: \(x^2+4x^2+4x^2=9x^2=1\Rightarrow x=\pm\tfrac13\).
Point\(\lambda\)\(f=x-2y+2z\)
\(\left(\tfrac13,-\tfrac23,\tfrac23\right)\)\(\tfrac32\)\(\tfrac13+\tfrac43+\tfrac43=3\)
\(\left(-\tfrac13,\tfrac23,-\tfrac23\right)\)\(-\tfrac32\)\(-\tfrac13-\tfrac43-\tfrac43=-3\)
Maximum \(3\) at \(\left(\tfrac13,-\tfrac23,\tfrac23\right)\); minimum \(-3\) at \(\left(-\tfrac13,\tfrac23,-\tfrac23\right)\).
Marking scheme: 3 marks formulation (\(f\), \(g\), both gradients) · 3 marks correct Lagrange equations · 4 marks correct working (solving the system) · 2 marks the solutions. Formulating cleanly is worth a quarter of the question.
Sanity check with geometry: a linear function on the unit sphere has max \(=\|(1,-2,2)\|=3\), attained at the unit vector \((1,-2,2)/3\). Exactly matches.
Justify dividing by \(x,y,z\): "\(1=2\lambda x\) implies \(x\ne0\)". And state which point is max and which is min — just listing \(\pm3\) doesn't answer the question.

S2 Question 6 — Chain rule with new variables [12 marks] · Ch 5

Suppose \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) is a function of \(x\) and \(y\) whose partial derivatives are $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=x^2y\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=x^2.$$
8 marks(a) Let \(x=u^2\) and \(y=uv^2\), where \(u>0\) and \(v>0\). Determine \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}\) in terms of \(u\) and \(v\).
Worked solution
\(f\) depends on \(u\) through both \(x\) and \(y\) → tree diagram \(f\to(x,y)\to(u,v)\): \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}\).
\(\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}=2u,\qquad \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}=v^2.\) $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}=x^2y\cdot2u+x^2\cdot v^2.$$ Now substitute \(x=u^2,\ y=uv^2\) (so everything is in \(u,v\)): $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}=(u^2)^2(uv^2)(2u)+(u^2)^2v^2=2u^6v^2+u^4v^2=u^4v^2(1+2u^2).$$
\(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}=2u^6v^2+u^4v^2=u^4v^2\,(1+2u^2).\)
Marking scheme: 5 marks chain rule and correct working · 3 marks putting the answer in terms of \(u\) and \(v\). Leaving \(x^2y\cdot2u+x^2v^2\) un-substituted forfeits 3 marks.
4 marks(b) Evaluate \(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}\) at \((4,1)\).
Worked solution
The marking scheme reads \((4,1)\) as the point \((x,y)=(4,1)\) — so first convert it to \((u,v)\) using \(u>0,\ v>0\).
\(x=u^2=4\Rightarrow u=2\) (since \(u>0\)). \(\ y=uv^2=1\Rightarrow 2v^2=1\Rightarrow v=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\) (since \(v>0\)). $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\Big(2,\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\Big)=2^4\left(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^2(1+2\cdot2^2)=16\cdot\tfrac12\cdot9=72.$$
\(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}=72\) at \((x,y)=(4,1)\), i.e. \((u,v)=(2,\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2})\).
Marking scheme: 2 marks finding \((u,v)\) · 2 marks correct working and value. The conditions \(u>0,\ v>0\) in the question are there precisely so the square roots are unique — cite them.
Ambiguity alert: "\((4,1)\)" could be read as \((u,v)=(4,1)\), which gives \(4^4\cdot1\cdot(1+32)=8448\). The official MS uses \((x,y)=(4,1)\to72\). On the day, state your interpretation in a sentence (the exam instructions invite this: "If you believe there is missing or incorrect information … state this"). The ugly 8448 is also a hint that \((x,y)\) was intended.

S2 Question 7 — Velocity & path length [10 marks] · Ch 7

At time \(t\), the position of a particle moving in the plane is given by $$\mathbf r(t)=\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right)-1,\ \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right)\right),\quad t\in[0,2].$$
4 marks(a) Determine the velocity of the particle at time \(t\).
Worked solution
Velocity \(\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf r'(t)\), componentwise; chain rule gives the factor \(\pi/4\).
$$\mathbf v(t)=\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}=\left(-\frac\pi4\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}4\right),\ \frac\pi4\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}4\right)\right).$$
\(\mathbf v(t)=\dfrac\pi4\left(-\sin\dfrac{\pi t}{4},\ \cos\dfrac{\pi t}{4}\right).\)
Marking scheme: 1 mark formula \(\mathbf v=d\mathbf r/dt\) · 3 marks correct velocity vector. The constant \(-1\) differentiates to 0.
6 marks(b) Determine the length of the path travelled by the particle.
Worked solution
Length \(=\int_0^2\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\) (formula sheet).
$$\|\mathbf v(t)\|=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi^2}{16}\sin^2\tfrac{\pi t}4+\tfrac{\pi^2}{16}\cos^2\tfrac{\pi t}4}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi^2}{16}}=\frac\pi4\quad(\text{constant speed}).$$ $$L=\int_0^2\frac\pi4\,dt=\frac\pi4\cdot2=\frac\pi2.$$
Path length \(=\dfrac\pi2\).
Geometry check: \((x+1)^2+y^2=1\) — a unit circle centred at \((-1,0)\). The angle \(\pi t/4\) runs from \(0\) to \(\pi/2\): a quarter circle from \((0,0)\) to \((-1,1)\), length \(\tfrac14\cdot2\pi\cdot1=\tfrac\pi2\). ✓
Marking scheme: 1 mark the arc-length formula · 3 marks correct working for the speed · 2 marks correct working and answer.

S2 Question 8 — Choose A, B to make F conservative; work [18 marks] · Ch 7

Let \(\mathbf F(x,y)=(5x^2y+Ay^2+xy^2)\,\mathbf i+(Bx^3+3yx+yx^2)\,\mathbf j\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are real numbers, be a force field in \(\mathbb R^2\). Let \(C\) be the path parametrised by $$\mathbf r(t)=(t\cos t,\ t\sin t),\quad t\in\left[0,\tfrac\pi2\right].$$
6 marks(a) Determine the values of \(A\) and \(B\) such that \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
Worked solution
State the test (\(\mathbf F=P\mathbf i+Q\mathbf j\) on \(\mathbb R^2\) is conservative iff \(P_y=Q_x\)), then match coefficients of like terms — it must hold for all \((x,y)\).
\(P=5x^2y+Ay^2+xy^2,\quad Q=Bx^3+3yx+yx^2.\) $$P_y=5x^2+2Ay+2xy,\qquad Q_x=3Bx^2+3y+2xy.$$ \(P_y=Q_x\) for all \(x,y\) ⇔ \(5x^2+2Ay=3Bx^2+3y\). Compare coefficients: $$x^2:\ 5=3B\Rightarrow B=\tfrac53,\qquad y:\ 2A=3\Rightarrow A=\tfrac32.$$
\(A=\tfrac32,\ B=\tfrac53\); then \(\mathbf F=\left(5x^2y+\tfrac32y^2+xy^2\right)\mathbf i+\left(\tfrac53x^3+3xy+x^2y\right)\mathbf j.\)
Marking scheme: 2 marks stating the conservative condition · 3 marks correct partial derivatives and finding \(A,B\) · 1 mark writing out \(\mathbf F\) with the values. That last line is a free mark — write it.
6 marks(b) Using the values \(A\) and \(B\) found in part (a), find a potential function \(f(x,y)\) such that \(\mathbf F=\nabla f\).
Worked solution
Integrate \(P\) w.r.t. \(x\) (constant of integration is \(g(y)\)), then differentiate w.r.t. \(y\) and match \(Q\) to find \(g\).
$$f=\int\left(5x^2y+\tfrac32y^2+xy^2\right)dx=\frac53x^3y+\frac32xy^2+\frac12x^2y^2+g(y).$$ $$f_y=\frac53x^3+3xy+x^2y+g'(y)\overset{!}{=}\frac53x^3+3xy+x^2y\ \Rightarrow\ g'(y)=0\Rightarrow g=K.$$
\(f(x,y)=\dfrac53x^3y+\dfrac32xy^2+\dfrac12x^2y^2+K\) (any constant \(K\)).
Marking scheme: 5 marks correct working · 1 mark the correct potential. Verify: \(f_x=5x^2y+\tfrac32y^2+xy^2=P\) ✓.
6 marks(c) Determine the work done by \(\mathbf F\) on a particle that moves along the path \(C\) from \(t=0\) to \(t=\pi/2\).
Worked solution
Fundamental Theorem for line integrals: \(W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\mathbf r(\pi/2))-f(\mathbf r(0))\). Only endpoints matter.
Endpoints (same spiral as S1 Q8): \(\mathbf r(0)=(0,0)\), \(\mathbf r(\pi/2)=\left(\tfrac\pi2\cos\tfrac\pi2,\ \tfrac\pi2\sin\tfrac\pi2\right)=\left(0,\tfrac\pi2\right)\). $$W=f\!\left(0,\tfrac\pi2\right)-f(0,0)=K-K=0,$$ because every term of \(f\) contains a factor \(x\), which is 0 at both endpoints.
\(W=0\).
Marking scheme: 3 marks correct endpoints · 3 marks correct evaluation of the work. Zero work is not a mistake — both endpoints lie on the \(y\)-axis where \(f\equiv K\).
Don't try \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt\) directly — polynomial × trig mess, no calculator. Once you have a potential, the path shape is irrelevant.

What 2024 teaches

1 · The syllabus in 2024 was 100% multivariable

Neither paper asked a single ODE question. Every mark came from Chapters 3–7. Both papers share the same 8-slot skeleton:

The same path \(\mathbf r(t)=(t\cos t,t\sin t),\ t\in[0,\pi/2]\) appeared in both papers' Q8. Know its endpoints \((0,0)\to(0,\pi/2)\) and its shape (Archimedean spiral) cold.

2 · Patterns that repeat

  • Completing the square does triple duty: contour shape, intercepts, and the extreme value (S1 Q3b min \(z=1\); S2 Q1b max \(T=100\)). One algebra step, two parts.
  • Limits come in pairs: one that fails (find two paths — for \(x^3y/(2x^6+y^2)\) the killer is \(y=x^3\)), one that exists (polar, \(\theta\) disappears). Always name both paths and both values.
  • Speed simplifies: examiners design \(\|\mathbf r'\|\) to collapse (\((t^2+2)^2\); constant \(\pi/4\)). If your speed is ugly, re-check the derivative.
  • Work questions are really potential-function questions: \(P_y=Q_x\) → integrate → endpoints. The line integral itself is never computed directly.
  • "Show that" (S1 Q6, S2 Q3): reach the given answer by algebra, justify each division, then box the given result.

3 · Where the marks actually are (from the S2 marking scheme)

  • Writing the formula before using it is typically 1 mark (\(\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)\), \(f_{\mathbf v}=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\), \(L=\int\|\mathbf r'\|\), conservative test).
  • Formulation in Lagrange is 3/12 — define \(f\), \(g\) and both gradients explicitly.
  • Conclusion sentences are marked separately (2/6 in the DNE limit).
  • Final form matters: "in terms of \(u\) and \(v\)" was 3/8 on its own.

4 · Time plan (120 minutes, 100 marks ≈ 1.2 min/mark)

Question 1 — Quadratic approximation [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 4 · Limits, partials & approximation

Consider the function \(f(x,y)=x^2y-y^3+\ln x\).
5 marksa) Find the quadratic approximation for \(f(x,y)\) at the point \((1,1)\).
Worked solution
"Quadratic approximation" → the formula-sheet \(Q(x,y)\). You need six numbers: \(f,\ f_x,\ f_y,\ f_{xx},\ f_{xy},\ f_{yy}\), all at \((1,1)\).
Write the formula first (it earns the method mark): $$Q(x,y)=f(1,1)+f_x(1,1)(x-1)+f_y(1,1)(y-1)+\tfrac12f_{xx}(1,1)(x-1)^2+f_{xy}(1,1)(x-1)(y-1)+\tfrac12f_{yy}(1,1)(y-1)^2.$$ Compute the ingredients: $$\begin{aligned} f(1,1)&=1-1+\ln1=0\\ f_x&=2xy+\tfrac1x &&\Rightarrow f_x(1,1)=3\\ f_y&=x^2-3y^2 &&\Rightarrow f_y(1,1)=-2\\ f_{xx}&=2y-\tfrac1{x^2} &&\Rightarrow f_{xx}(1,1)=1\\ f_{xy}&=2x &&\Rightarrow f_{xy}(1,1)=2\\ f_{yy}&=-6y &&\Rightarrow f_{yy}(1,1)=-6 \end{aligned}$$ Substitute:
$$Q(x,y)=3(x-1)-2(y-1)+\tfrac12(x-1)^2+2(x-1)(y-1)-3(y-1)^2.$$
The official solution writes \(f_x=2x+1/x\) and \(f_{xx}=2-1/x^2\) — that is \(f_x\) with \(y=1\) already substituted. It gives the right numbers here only because \(y=1\) at the base point. In general differentiate first, substitute last, or \(f_{xy}\) comes out wrong.
Watch the \(\tfrac12\)'s: they go on \(f_{xx}\) and \(f_{yy}\) only — the mixed term has coefficient \(f_{xy}\), not \(\tfrac12f_{xy}\) (because \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) both contribute).
5 marksb) Using part a), derive an approximate value for \(f(1.1,1)\).
Worked solution
Plug the point into \(Q\). With \(y=1\) every \((y-1)\) term vanishes.
$$f(1.1,1)\approx Q(1.1,1)=3(0.1)-2(0)+\tfrac12(0.1)^2+2(0.1)(0)-3(0)^2=0.3+0.005.$$
\(f(1.1,1)\approx0.305\)
Sanity check the official solution adds: the true value is \(1.21-1+\ln1.1=0.305310\ldots\) — the quadratic term captured the curvature of \(\ln x\) nicely. No calculator is needed: \(0.1^2/2=0.005\).

Question 2 — Contours that are hyperbolas [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 3 · Surfaces, contours & cross-sections

Consider the surface $$z=\frac{2x}{x^2-y^2}.$$
5 marksa) For any \(z_0\neq0\), show that the contours \(z=z_0\) of the surface are hyperbolas.
Worked solution
"Show the contours are conics" → set \(z=z_0\), clear the fraction, complete the square, and massage into standard form \(\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2}=1\).
Set \(z=z_0\) (with \(x^2\ne y^2\), where the surface is defined): $$z_0(x^2-y^2)=2x\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z_0x^2-2x-z_0y^2=0.$$ Complete the square in \(x\) (divide the \(x\)-terms by \(z_0\neq0\)): $$z_0\Big(x^2-\tfrac{2}{z_0}x\Big)-z_0y^2=0\ \Longrightarrow\ z_0\Big(x-\tfrac1{z_0}\Big)^2-\tfrac1{z_0}-z_0y^2=0.$$ Move the constant across and multiply by \(z_0\): $$z_0^2\Big(x-\tfrac1{z_0}\Big)^2-z_0^2y^2=1\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{\big(x-\frac1{z_0}\big)^2}{(1/z_0)^2}-\frac{y^2}{(1/z_0)^2}=1.$$
This is the standard form of a hyperbola (difference of two squares \(=1\)), centred at \(\big(\tfrac1{z_0},0\big)\), opening left–right, with \(a=b=\tfrac1{|z_0|}\) and asymptotes \(y=\pm\big(x-\tfrac1{z_0}\big)\). Hence every contour \(z=z_0\neq0\) is a hyperbola. \(\blacksquare\)
State explicitly why \(z_0\ne0\) matters: you divide by it when completing the square (and \(z_0=0\) gives the line \(x=0\), not a hyperbola). Markers like to see the final line named: "difference of squares equal to 1 ⇒ hyperbola".
5 marksb) Sketch the contour for \(z=1\). Be sure to indicate the hyperbola's center, and asymptotes.
Worked solution
Substitute \(z_0=1\) into part a)'s standard form, then read off centre, vertices (\(\pm a\) from the centre along the opening axis) and asymptotes (slopes \(\pm b/a\) through the centre).
$$z_0=1:\qquad (x-1)^2-y^2=1.$$
  • Centre \((1,0)\); opens left–right (the \(x\)-term is positive).
  • Vertices \(a=1\): \((0,0)\) and \((2,0)\).
  • Asymptotes (\(b/a=1\)): \(y=x-1\) and \(y=-(x-1)=1-x\).
Horizontal hyperbola \((x-1)^2-y^2=1\), centre \((1,0)\), vertices \((0,0)\) and \((2,0)\), asymptotes \(y=\pm(x-1)\).
Fine print the official solution skips: the surface is undefined where \(x^2=y^2\). The left vertex \((0,0)\) lies on \(y=\pm x\), so it is not actually on the contour — draw it as an open circle. It won't cost you marks to omit, but it's the kind of detail that separates a 7 from a 6 on a "justify" question.
Marks are for labels: centre, both asymptote equations, and the vertices/intercepts. A beautiful unlabeled curve scores little.

Question 3 — Continuity at the origin [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 4 · Limits & continuity

10 marksConsider the function \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) defined as $$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}4 & \text{if }(x,y)=(0,0)\\[2pt] \dfrac{x^3y}{x^6+y^2} & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ Is \(f\) continuous at the point \((0,0)\)?
Worked solution
Continuity at \((0,0)\) needs \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) to exist and to equal \(f(0,0)=4\). To show "no", use the two-path test. The powers \(x^6\) and \(y^2\) are balanced when \(y\sim x^3\) — that's the path to try.
Path 1: along \(y=0\) (with \(x\ne0\)): $$f(x,0)=\frac{0}{x^6}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lim_{x\to0}f(x,0)=0.$$ Path 2: along \(y=x^3\): $$f(x,x^3)=\frac{x^3\cdot x^3}{x^6+x^6}=\frac{x^6}{2x^6}=\frac12\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lim_{x\to0}f(x,x^3)=\frac12.$$ The limits along two different paths disagree \((0\ne\tfrac12)\), so \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) does not exist.
No — \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\), because the limit there does not exist (two paths give \(0\) and \(\tfrac12\)).
Straight lines \(y=mx\) all give limit \(0\) here \(\big(\frac{mx^4}{x^6+m^2x^2}=\frac{mx^2}{x^4+m^2}\to0\big)\). Stopping after lines and concluding "limit \(=0\), and \(0\ne4\) so discontinuous" gets the right yes/no for the wrong reason — the limit doesn't exist at all. Match the degrees: \(y^2\) vs \(x^6\) ⇒ try \(y=x^3\).
The value \(f(0,0)=4\) is a red herring: once the limit fails to exist, continuity fails regardless of the defined value. Say that sentence explicitly.

Question 4 — Chain rule "show that" [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 5 · Chain rule & PDE identities

10 marksConsider the differentiable function \(z=f(x,y)=f(x^2-y^2)\). Show that $$x\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}+y\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=0.$$
Worked solution
\(f\) is a function of one inner variable. Name it: \(u=x^2-y^2\), so \(z=f(u)\). Tree: \(z\to u\to(x,y)\). Then \(\partial z/\partial x=\dfrac{df}{du}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\), etc.
Let \(u=x^2-y^2\), so \(z=f(u)\) with \(\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}=2x,\ \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}=-2y\). By the chain rule $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=\frac{df}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=2x\,f'(u),\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=\frac{df}{du}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=-2y\,f'(u).$$ Hence $$x\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}+y\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=x(-2y)f'(u)+y(2x)f'(u)=-2xyf'(u)+2xyf'(u)=0,$$ as required. \(\blacksquare\)
\(z_x=2xf'(u),\ z_y=-2yf'(u)\) ⇒ \(xz_y+yz_x=0\).
Note the question pairs \(x\) with \(\partial z/\partial y\) (not \(\partial z/\partial x\)). Misread it as \(xz_x+yz_y\) and you get \(2(x^2-y^2)f'\ne0\) — then you'll waste time hunting a "mistake".
Write \(f'(u)\) or \(\dfrac{df}{du}\), never \(f_x\): \(f\) is a one-variable function here. Introducing the letter \(u\) and drawing the tree diagram is what the marker is looking for.

Question 5 — Tangent lines by implicit differentiation [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 5 · Implicit differentiation

10 marksConsider the curve $$y\cos x=x^2+y^2.$$ Find the equation(s) of the tangent line(s) to the curve at \(x=0\).
Worked solution
Curve given implicitly → write it as \(F(x,y)=0\) and use the formula-sheet \(y'=-F_x/F_y\). The "(s)" warns you: find every \(y\) on the curve with \(x=0\) first.
Let \(F(x,y)=y\cos x-x^2-y^2\), so the curve is \(F=0\). $$F_x=-y\sin x-2x,\qquad F_y=\cos x-2y,\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}=\frac{y\sin x+2x}{\cos x-2y}.$$ Points with \(x=0\): \(y\cos0=0+y^2\Rightarrow y=y^2\Rightarrow y(y-1)=0\Rightarrow y=0\) or \(y=1\). So two points: \((0,0)\) and \((0,1)\).
Slopes: $$\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{(0,0)}=\frac{0+0}{1-0}=0,\qquad \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{(0,1)}=\frac{0+0}{1-2}=0.$$ (Both denominators \(F_y=1\) and \(F_y=-1\) are non-zero, so the formula is valid at both points.) Point–slope form \(y-y_0=m(x-x_0)\):
Two tangent lines: \(y=0\) (at \((0,0)\)) and \(y=1\) (at \((0,1)\)).
The most common loss: finding only \(y=1\) (dividing \(y=y^2\) by \(y\)) and losing the \(y=0\) point. Never divide by a variable that can be zero — factor instead.
Check \(F_y\ne0\) at each point and say so; if \(F_y=0\) the tangent would be vertical and the formula breaks.

Question 6 — Recover the gradient from a directional derivative [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 5 · Gradient & directional derivatives

10 marksThe directional derivative of an unknown function \(f(x,y,z)\) at the point \((3,-2,1)\) in the direction of \(\mathbf v=(2,1,-2)\) is \(-5\). In addition, \(\|\nabla f(3,-2,1)\|=5\).
Find \(\nabla f(3,-2,1)\).
Worked solution
Use the geometric form of the dot product: \(f_{\mathbf v}=\nabla f\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta\). Knowing \(f_{\mathbf v}\) and \(\|\nabla f\|\) pins down the angle \(\theta\) between \(\nabla f\) and \(\mathbf v\).
At \((3,-2,1)\): $$f_{\mathbf v}=\nabla f\cdot\frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}=\frac{\|\nabla f\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta}{\|\mathbf v\|}=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta.$$ Given \(f_{\mathbf v}=-5\) and \(\|\nabla f\|=5\): $$-5=5\cos\theta\ \Longrightarrow\ \cos\theta=-1\ \Longrightarrow\ \theta=\pi.$$ So \(\nabla f\) points exactly opposite to \(\mathbf v\), with length 5. Since \(\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{4+1+4}=3\), $$\nabla f(3,-2,1)=-5\,\frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}=-\frac53(2,1,-2).$$
\(\nabla f(3,-2,1)=\left(-\tfrac{10}{3},\,-\tfrac53,\,\tfrac{10}{3}\right)\).
The official solution has a typo: it writes \(-\frac53(2\mathbf i-\mathbf j-2\mathbf k)\), flipping the sign of the \(\mathbf j\)-component. With \(\mathbf v=(2,1,-2)\) the answer is \(-\frac53(2\mathbf i+\mathbf j-2\mathbf k)\). Check your answer: its length should be 5 and its dot with \(\mathbf v/3\) should be \(-5\) — both hold for \((-\frac{10}3,-\frac53,\frac{10}3)\).
Key insight to state in words: \(|f_{\mathbf v}|\le\|\nabla f\|\) always, with equality only when \(\mathbf v\) is parallel to \(\nabla f\). "Directional derivative = −‖∇f‖ ⇒ direction of steepest descent."

Question 7 — Critical points & classification [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 6 · Optimisation

Consider the function $$f(x,y)=xy-x^3-y^2.$$
5 marksa) Find all the critical points of \(f\).
Worked solution
Critical points: solve \(f_x=0\) and \(f_y=0\) simultaneously. Use the linear equation to eliminate a variable.
$$f_x=y-3x^2,\qquad f_y=x-2y.$$ From \(f_y=0\): \(x=2y\). Substitute into \(f_x=0\): $$y-3(2y)^2=y-12y^2=y(1-12y)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ y=0\ \text{or}\ y=\tfrac1{12}.$$ \(y=0\Rightarrow x=0\); \(\ y=\tfrac1{12}\Rightarrow x=\tfrac16\).
Critical points: \((0,0)\) and \(\left(\tfrac16,\tfrac1{12}\right)\).
The official solution says "\(f_x=0\Rightarrow x=2y\)" — it means \(f_y\). Label which equation you use; markers follow your logic.
5 marksb) Choose one of these points, and classify it as a local minimum, local maximum, or saddle point.
Worked solution
Second-derivative test: \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2\). \(D\lt0\) saddle; \(D\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}\gt0\) min; \(D\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}\lt0\) max; \(D=0\) inconclusive.
$$f_{xx}=-6x,\quad f_{yy}=-2,\quad f_{xy}=1,\qquad D(x,y)=(-6x)(-2)-1^2=12x-1.$$
  • \((0,0)\): \(D=-1\lt0\) ⇒ saddle point.
  • \(\left(\tfrac16,\tfrac1{12}\right)\): \(D=12\cdot\tfrac16-1=1\gt0\) and \(f_{xx}=-1\lt0\) ⇒ local maximum (value \(f=\tfrac1{432}\)).
Either answer earns the marks: \((0,0)\) is a saddle; \(\left(\tfrac16,\tfrac1{12}\right)\) is a local maximum.
"Choose one" — doing both costs 30 seconds and protects you if you slip on one. \((0,0)\) is the quickest (\(D=-1\), done).

Question 8 — Lagrange multipliers on an ellipse [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 6 · Lagrange multipliers

10 marksConsider an elliptical train track, whose coordinates \((x,y)\) satisfy the equation $$\frac{x^2}{4}+y^2=1.$$ The height of the track at any point on the loop is given by the function \(f(x,y)=x^2+2y^4\). Use the method of Lagrange Multipliers to find a point where the track is at its lowest.
Worked solution
Optimise \(f\) subject to \(g(x,y)=\frac{x^2}4+y^2=1\) (closed, bounded curve ⇒ a min exists). Solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) together with \(g=1\), handling the "\(=0\) or \(\lambda=\ldots\)" cases separately. Compare \(f\) at all candidates.
$$\nabla f=(2x,\ 8y^3),\qquad \nabla g=\left(\tfrac x2,\ 2y\right).$$ \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) gives $$2x=\lambda\frac x2\ \Longrightarrow\ x(4-\lambda)=0,\qquad 8y^3=2\lambda y\ \Longrightarrow\ 2y(4y^2-\lambda)=0.$$ Case 1: \(x=0\). Constraint: \(y^2=1\Rightarrow y=\pm1\). \(f(0,\pm1)=2\).
Case 2: \(\lambda=4\). Then \(8y^3=8y\Rightarrow y(y^2-1)=0\).
  • 2a: \(y=0\Rightarrow x^2=4\Rightarrow x=\pm2\). \(f(\pm2,0)=4\).
  • 2b: \(y^2=1\Rightarrow x^2/4=0\Rightarrow x=0\) — same points as Case 1.
Candidate\((0,\pm1)\)\((\pm2,0)\)
\(f\)24
The track is lowest at \((0,1)\) (or equally \((0,-1)\)), where the height is \(f=2\). (Highest at \((\pm2,0)\), height 4.)
Cancelling \(x\) from \(2x=\lambda x/2\) to get "\(\lambda=4\)" loses the \(x=0\) branch — which is exactly where the minimum lives. Always factor: \(x(4-\lambda)=0\).
The question says "Use the method of Lagrange Multipliers" — substituting \(x^2=4-4y^2\) and minimising in one variable would find the answer but may not get full method marks. Write \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) explicitly.

Question 9 — Motorcycle on a polar curve [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 7 · Parametrised curves, speed & arc length

A speeding motorcycle traces out a polar curve of the form $$r(\theta)=2\cos\theta,$$ where \(r\) is in metres, and \(\theta\) is in radians. During its journey, it is observed that \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}=3\) radians per second.
5 marksa) Show that the motorcycle's speed is a constant 6 metres per second.
Worked solution
Speed \(=\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\). First turn the polar curve into a Cartesian position vector \(\mathbf r=(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\), then differentiate with the chain rule \(\dfrac{d}{dt}=\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\).
Position: $$\mathbf r=r\cos\theta\,\mathbf i+r\sin\theta\,\mathbf j=2\cos^2\theta\,\mathbf i+2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf j=(1+\cos2\theta)\,\mathbf i+\sin2\theta\,\mathbf j.$$ Velocity (chain rule): $$\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}=\big(-2\sin2\theta\,\mathbf i+2\cos2\theta\,\mathbf j\big)\frac{d\theta}{dt}.$$ Speed: $$\left\|\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\right\|=2\left|\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right|\sqrt{\sin^22\theta+\cos^22\theta}=2\left|\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right|=2\times3=6.$$
Speed \(=6\) m/s for every \(\theta\) — constant, as required. \(\blacksquare\)
The official solution keeps \(-4\cos\theta\sin\theta\) and \(2(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)\) and grinds through \((\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)^2=1\). Spotting the double-angle forms \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta\), \(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\cos2\theta\) up front makes it a two-line job. Either route is full marks.
Geometric check: \(r=2\cos\theta\) is the circle \((x-1)^2+y^2=1\) of radius 1; a point at polar angle \(\theta\) is at central angle \(2\theta\), turning at \(6\) rad/s, so speed \(=1\times6\).
5 marksb) How far does the motorcycle travel in 5 seconds?
Worked solution
"How far does it travel" = arc length (distance along the path), \(L=\int_a^b\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt\) — formula sheet.
$$L=\int_{t_0}^{t_0+5}\|\mathbf r'(t)\|\,dt=\int_{t_0}^{t_0+5}6\,dt=6\times5.$$
\(L=30\) metres.
Distance travelled ≠ displacement. The bike laps the unit circle (circumference \(2\pi\approx6.28\)) almost 5 times, so its displacement is small — but the question asks how far it travels.

Question 10 — Conservative field & line integral [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 7 · Conservative fields & potentials

Consider the field $$\mathbf F=y\,\mathbf i+\left(x+2e^{2y}\right)\mathbf j.$$
5 marksa) Show that \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
Worked solution
Two acceptable routes: (i) the test \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\) on all of \(\mathbb R^2\); (ii) constructively find a potential \(f\) with \(\nabla f=\mathbf F\). Doing (ii) also sets up part b).
\(P=y,\ Q=x+2e^{2y}\). Test: \(P_y=1=Q_x\), and \(P,Q\) have continuous partials on all of \(\mathbb R^2\) (simply connected) ⇒ conservative.
Potential: integrate \(P\) in \(x\): $$f=\int y\,dx=xy+g(y).$$ Match \(f_y\) with \(Q\): $$f_y=x+g'(y)=x+2e^{2y}\ \Longrightarrow\ g'(y)=2e^{2y}\ \Longrightarrow\ g(y)=e^{2y}\ (+C).$$
\(\mathbf F=\nabla f\) with \(f(x,y)=xy+e^{2y}\), so \(\mathbf F\) is conservative.
Check it: \(f_x=y\) ✓, \(f_y=x+2e^{2y}\) ✓. One line of checking catches a wrong \(g(y)\).
5 marksb) Use this result or otherwise to evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\), where \(C\) is the straight line segment from \((1,0)\) to \((1,1)\).
Worked solution
Conservative field ⇒ fundamental theorem for line integrals: \(\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\).
$$\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=f(1,1)-f(1,0)=(1+e^2)-(0+e^0)=1+e^2-1.$$
\(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=e^2\).
Cross-check "otherwise": \(\mathbf r(s)=(1,s),\ 0\le s\le1\), \(\mathbf r'=(0,1)\), so \(\int_0^1 Q(1,s)\,ds=\int_0^1(1+2e^{2s})\,ds=1+(e^2-1)=e^2\) ✓.
The official solution writes \(f(1,1)-f(0,1)\) — a typo for \(f(1,0)\) (the start point). End minus start, with the right coordinates; swapping them flips the sign.

Question 1 — Linear approximation in 3 variables [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 4 · Linear approximation

Let \(f(x,y,z)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\).
6 marks(a) Determine the linear approximation of the function \(f(x,y,z)\) at \((3,2,6)\).
Worked solution
Linear approximation = formula-sheet \(P\), extended with a \(z\)-term: \(L=f+f_x(x-a)+f_y(y-b)+f_z(z-c)\).
$$L(x,y,z)=f(3,2,6)+f_x(3,2,6)(x-3)+f_y(3,2,6)(y-2)+f_z(3,2,6)(z-6).\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ $$f(3,2,6)=\sqrt{9+4+36}=\sqrt{49}=7.\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ $$f_x=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}\cdot2x=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}},\quad f_y=\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}},\quad f_z=\frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}$$ so \(f_x=\tfrac37,\ f_y=\tfrac27,\ f_z=\tfrac67\) at \((3,2,6)\). [3 — correct derivatives & correct values]
$$L(x,y,z)=7+\tfrac37(x-3)+\tfrac27(y-2)+\tfrac67(z-6).\quad\textbf{[1]}$$
Symmetry saves time: once you have \(f_x\), write \(f_y,f_z\) by swapping letters. Note \(\nabla f=\dfrac{(x,y,z)}{\|(x,y,z)\|}\) — the unit radial vector.
4 marks(b) Use the linear approximation to show that $$\sqrt{(3.02)^2+(1.97)^2+(5.99)^2}\approx7-\frac{0.06}{7}.$$
Worked solution
"Show that" → substitute \((3.02,1.97,5.99)\) into \(L\), displaying each increment.
$$\sqrt{3.02^2+1.97^2+5.99^2}\approx L(3.02,1.97,5.99)=7+\tfrac37(0.02)+\tfrac27(-0.03)+\tfrac67(-0.01)\quad\textbf{[2]}$$ $$=7+\frac{0.06}{7}-\frac{0.06}{7}-\frac{0.06}{7}=7-\frac{0.06}{7}.\quad\textbf{[2]}$$
\(\sqrt{(3.02)^2+(1.97)^2+(5.99)^2}\approx7-\dfrac{0.06}{7}\) \(\blacksquare\)
Signs of the increments: \(1.97-2=-0.03\) and \(5.99-6=-0.01\). Every term here is \(\pm0.06/7\) — a designed-clean question; if your terms don't simplify, you've slipped a sign.

Question 2 — Partial derivative from the definition; continuity [12 marks]

Topic: Ch 4 · Limits, continuity & partial derivatives

Let $$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2} & \text{if }(x,y)\neq(0,0)\\[4pt] 0 & \text{if }(x,y)=(0,0)\end{cases}$$
5 marks(a) Determine \(f_x(0,0)\). If it does not exist, explain why.
Worked solution
A piecewise function at the "special" point ⇒ you must use the limit definition \(f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}\). The quotient-rule formula is invalid at \((0,0)\).
$$f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(0+h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}\quad\textbf{[3]}\qquad=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0)-0}{h}\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ For \(h\ne0\), \(f(h,0)=\dfrac{h\cdot0}{h^2+0}=0\), so $$f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{0-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}0=0.\quad\textbf{[1]}$$
\(f_x(0,0)=0\) (it exists).
Writing the definition correctly is worth 3 of the 5 marks. Many students differentiate \(\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\) with the quotient rule and "substitute \((0,0)\)" — that gives \(\frac00\) and zero marks for method.
7 marks(b) Determine whether \(f(x,y)\) is continuous at \((0,0)\).
Worked solution
Numerator and denominator are both degree 2 ⇒ straight lines \(y=mx\) will give \(m\)-dependent limits. Two-path test.
Claim: \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\) because \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f(x,y)\) does not exist.
Along \(x=0\): \(f(0,y)=\dfrac{0}{y^2}=0\ \Rightarrow\ L_1=0.\quad\textbf{[3]}\)
Along \(y=x\): \(f(x,x)=\dfrac{x^2}{x^2+x^2}=\dfrac12\ \Rightarrow\ L_2=\dfrac12.\quad\textbf{[3]}\)
Since \(L_1\ne L_2\), the limit does not exist; hence \(f\) is not continuous at \((0,0)\). [1]
Not continuous at \((0,0)\) — the limit along \(x=0\) is \(0\) but along \(y=x\) is \(\tfrac12\).
The punchline of (a)+(b): \(f_x(0,0)\) (and \(f_y(0,0)\)) can exist even though \(f\) is not continuous. Partial derivatives only look along the axes. This contrast is exactly why the question pairs the two parts.
The final sentence ("since \(L_1\ne L_2\) the limit DNE, hence not continuous") carries its own mark. Don't stop at two numbers.

Question 3 — Temperature: directional derivative & steepest descent [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 5 · Gradient & directional derivatives

The temperature (in degrees Celsius) at a point \((x,y)\) on a metal plate in the \(xy\)-plane is $$T(x,y)=\frac{xy}{1+x^2+y^2}.$$
5 marks(a) Find the rate of change of temperature at \((1,1)\) in the direction \(2\mathbf i-\mathbf j\).
Worked solution
Rate of change in a given direction = directional derivative \(T_{\mathbf u}=\nabla T\cdot\dfrac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}\). Normalise \((2,-1)\) — its length is \(\sqrt5\).
Write \(T=xy(1+x^2+y^2)^{-1}\) (product rule): $$T_x=\frac{y}{1+x^2+y^2}-\frac{2x^2y}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}\ \Rightarrow\ T_x(1,1)=\frac13-\frac29=\frac19,\quad\textbf{[2]}$$ $$T_y=\frac{x}{1+x^2+y^2}-\frac{2xy^2}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}\ \Rightarrow\ T_y(1,1)=\frac13-\frac29=\frac19.\quad\textbf{[2]}$$ $$T_{\mathbf u}(1,1)=\left(\tfrac19,\tfrac19\right)\cdot\frac{(2,-1)}{\sqrt5}=\frac{2-1}{9\sqrt5}.\quad\textbf{[1]}$$
\(T_{\mathbf u}(1,1)=\dfrac{1}{9\sqrt5}=\dfrac{\sqrt5}{45}\) °C per unit distance.
Forgetting to divide by \(\|(2,-1)\|=\sqrt5\) gives \(\frac19\) — wrong. The formula sheet shows \(\mathbf u/\|\mathbf u\|\) for a reason.
5 marks(b) An ant at \((1,1)\) is finding it too hot at \((1,1)\). In which direction should the ant walk for the temperature to drop most rapidly? Determine the rate at which the temperature is dropping in this direction.
Worked solution
Steepest descent is in the direction \(-\nabla T\); the rate of change in that direction is \(-\|\nabla T\|\).
Walk in the direction of \(-\nabla T(1,1)\) [2], i.e. \(-\left(\tfrac19,\tfrac19\right)=\left(-\tfrac19,-\tfrac19\right)\), or equivalently the direction \((-1,-1)\). [1]
Rate of change in that direction: $$-\|\nabla T(1,1)\|=-\sqrt{\tfrac1{81}+\tfrac1{81}}=-\frac{\sqrt2}{9}.\quad\textbf{[2]}$$
Direction \(-\mathbf i-\mathbf j\) (unit: \(-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\mathbf i+\mathbf j)\)). The temperature drops at \(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{9}\) °C per unit distance (directional derivative \(=-\sqrt2/9\)).
Answer both forms of the "rate" wording: "the rate of change is \(-\sqrt2/9\)", i.e. "it is dropping at \(\sqrt2/9\)". The marker accepted the negative; say what the sign means.

5 marks(a) Match a function \(f(x,y)\) in the table below with its graph (labelled 1, 2, 3). Enter 0 in the table if there is no match. No justification is required.
Function \(f(x,y)=\)Enter 0, 1, 2 or 3

(Described from the scan — the paper shows three 3-D surface plots.) Graph 1: a U-shaped valley/trough — flat-bottomed, identical cross-sections all along one horizontal axis. Graph 2: a single smooth bell-shaped bump peaking over the origin and flattening to 0 in every direction. Graph 3: a saddle ("Pringle") — rises in one direction, falls in the perpendicular one.

Match by cross-sections and which variables appear: a function of one variable only is a "cylinder" (same profile along the missing axis); a radially symmetric decaying exponential is a bump; \(x^2-y^2\) is the classic saddle.

  • \(x^4\): no \(y\) ⇒ same flat-bottomed U-profile for every \(y\) ⇒ trough ⇒ 1.
  • \(e^{-x^2-y^2}\): max 1 at origin, \(\to0\) in all directions, circular contours ⇒ bump ⇒ 2.

The contour \(f=1\) is \(y=x+\frac1x\): two branches (first and third quadrants) with asymptotes \(x=0\) and \(y=x\), turning points \((1,2)\) and \((-1,-2)\).

Question 5 — Chain rule: channel capacity [10 marks]

Topic: Ch 5 · Multivariable chain rule

10 marksThe capacity, \(C\), of a communication channel, such as a telephone line, to carry information depends on the ratio of the signal strength, \(S\), to the noise, \(N\). For some positive constant \(k\), $$C=k\ln\left(1+\frac SN\right).$$ Suppose that the signal and noise are given as a function of time, \(t\) in seconds, by $$S(t)=4+\cos(4\pi t)\qquad N(t)=2+\sin(2\pi t).$$ Determine \(\dfrac{dC}{dt}\) one second after transmission started. Is the capacity increasing or decreasing at that instant?
Worked solution
\(C\) depends on \(S,N\), each depending on \(t\) ⇒ tree diagram \(C\to(S,N)\to t\), formula-sheet chain rule \(\dfrac{dC}{dt}=\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial S}\dfrac{dS}{dt}+\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial N}\dfrac{dN}{dt}\).
Tree: \(C\) branches to \(S\) and \(N\); each of \(S\), \(N\) branches to \(t\). $$\frac{dC}{dt}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}\frac{dS}{dt}+\frac{\partial C}{\partial N}\frac{dN}{dt}\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ $$=\underbrace{\frac{k}{1+\frac SN}\cdot\frac1N\cdot\big(-4\pi\sin4\pi t\big)}_{\textbf{[3]}}+\underbrace{\frac{k}{1+\frac SN}\cdot\left(-\frac{S}{N^2}\right)\cdot\big(2\pi\cos2\pi t\big)}_{\textbf{[3]}}.$$ At \(t=1\): \(S=4+\cos4\pi=5,\ N=2+\sin2\pi=2,\ \sin4\pi=0,\ \cos2\pi=1\), and \(1+\frac SN=\frac72\). So $$\left.\frac{dC}{dt}\right|_{t=1}=\frac{k}{7/2}\cdot\frac12\cdot0+\frac{k}{7/2}\left(-\frac54\right)(1)(2\pi)=-\frac{2k}{7}\cdot\frac54\cdot2\pi.\quad\textbf{[2]}$$
\(\left.\dfrac{dC}{dt}\right|_{t=1}=-\dfrac{5\pi k}{7}\). Since \(k\gt0\), this is negative: the capacity is decreasing at \(t=1\). [1]
Simplify \(\dfrac{k}{1+S/N}=\dfrac{kN}{N+S}\) if it helps: \(\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial S}=\dfrac{k}{N+S}\), \(\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial N}=-\dfrac{kS}{N(N+S)}\). At \(t=1\): \(\frac k7\) and \(-\frac{5k}{14}\).
"Is it increasing or decreasing?" is its own mark — you must use \(k\gt0\) to conclude the sign. Leaving it as \(-5\pi k/7\) without the sentence drops a mark.

Question 6 — The "happy function": critical points [11 marks]

Topic: Ch 6 · Critical points & the degenerate case

The function \(f(x,y)=2x^3+2y^3-3x^2y^2\) is called the "happy function" (you can see a smile on the contour diagram of \(f(x,y)\) when you turn your head clockwise by \(\pi/4\)).
(Described from the scan.) A contour diagram on \(-2\le x,y\le2\): nested closed-looking contours in the square region between the origin and \((1,1)\); two contour lines cross at \((1,1)\) (an X-shaped crossing) and contours pass through/near the origin; dense contours sweep out to the upper-left and lower-right, forming the "smile" when the picture is rotated.
5 marks(a) Determine all critical points of the happy function.
Worked solution
Solve \(f_x=f_y=0\). Factor both equations, then case-split on the factors.
$$f_x=6x^2-6xy^2=6x(x-y^2),\qquad f_y=6y^2-6x^2y=6y(y-x^2).\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ Set \(6x(x-y^2)=0\) ①, \(6y(y-x^2)=0\) ②.
From ①: \(x=0\) or \(x=y^2\).
  • \(x=0\) into ②: \(6y^2=0\Rightarrow y=0\). Point \((0,0)\). [2]
  • \(x=y^2\) into ②: \(6y(y-y^4)=6y^2(1-y^3)=0\Rightarrow y=0\) or \(y^3=1\Rightarrow y=1\). Points \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). [2]
Critical points: \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\).
\(y^3=1\) has only one real root, \(y=1\). Don't invent \(y=-1\) (that's \(y^2=1\)).
6 marks(b) Classify the critical points.
Worked solution
Hessian test first. If \(D=0\) the test is inconclusive — then examine \(f\) directly near the point (restrict to a line through it and look for a sign change).
$$f_{xx}=12x-6y^2,\quad f_{yy}=12y-6x^2,\quad f_{xy}=-12xy\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ $$D(x,y)=(12x-6y^2)(12y-6x^2)-(-12xy)^2.\quad\textbf{[2 with the next line]}$$ \((1,1)\): \(D=(12-6)(12-6)-144=36-144=-108\lt0\) ⇒ saddle point.
\((0,0)\): \(D=0\cdot0-0=0\) ⇒ test inconclusive. Examine \(f\) directly: \(f(0,0)=0\), and along \(y=0\), \(f(x,0)=2x^3\), which is \(\gt0\) for \(x\gt0\) and \(\lt0\) for \(x\lt0\). So every neighbourhood of the origin contains points above and below \(f(0,0)\): \((0,0)\) is neither a local max nor a local min — it is a saddle point. [3]
\((1,1)\): saddle point (\(D=-108\)). \((0,0)\): \(D=0\), but \(f(x,0)=2x^3\) changes sign ⇒ saddle point.
The marker's own solution first wrote "not a local max/local min nor a saddle" and crossed it out in red, replacing it with "a saddle point". Lesson: a critical point that is neither max nor min is a saddle (in MATH1052's definition). And "\(D=0\) so inconclusive" alone scores 0 of those 3 marks — you must do the follow-up analysis.
Best follow-up lines for \(D=0\): the axes \(x=0\), \(y=0\), and \(y=\pm x\). Here \(y=0\) wins immediately. The contour diagram agrees: contours cross at \((1,1)\) — the visual signature of a saddle.

Question 7 — Lagrange: biggest cone in a sphere [12 marks]

Topic: Ch 6 · Lagrange multipliers (applied)

12 marksA cone with base radius \(r\) and height \(h\) is inscribed in the unit sphere. Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to determine the radius and height of the cone for which the volume of the cone is maximum. You may assume that the volume of the cone is \(\pi r^2h/3\). Note: you must use the method of Lagrange multipliers.
A circle (cross-section of the unit sphere, centre O) with an inscribed triangle: apex C at the top of the circle, base AB a horizontal chord below O with midpoint D. The height \(h\) runs from C down to D; the base radius \(r=DB\); a dashed radius of length 1 joins O to the base rim.
Worked solution
Objective \(V=\frac13\pi r^2h\); the constraint comes from the right triangle O–D–(rim): the centre is at distance \(h-1\) from the base, so \((h-1)^2+r^2=1\). Then \(\nabla V=\lambda\nabla g\).
Set-up. Apex at the top of the sphere, so the centre O is distance \(h-1\) above the base. Pythagoras in triangle O–D–B: \((h-1)^2+r^2=1\). [2] $$f(r,h)=\tfrac13\pi r^2h,\qquad g(r,h)=(h-1)^2+r^2-1=0.$$ Lagrange: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\): $$\left(\tfrac23\pi rh,\ \tfrac13\pi r^2\right)=\lambda\big(2r,\ 2(h-1)\big)\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ $$\tfrac23\pi rh=2\lambda r\ \ ①,\qquad \tfrac13\pi r^2=2\lambda(h-1)\ \ ②.$$ Assume \(\lambda\ne0\) and \(r\ne0\) (otherwise \(r=0\) and the volume is \(0\), not a maximum). [1] From ①, \(\lambda=\frac13\pi h\). Substituting into ②: $$\tfrac13\pi r^2=\tfrac23\pi h(h-1)\ \Longrightarrow\ r^2=2h(h-1).\quad\textbf{[2]}$$ (The marker's version: ② ÷ ① gives \(\dfrac{r}{2h}=\dfrac{h-1}{r}\).) Substitute into the constraint: $$(h-1)^2+2h(h-1)=1\ \Longrightarrow\ h^2-2h+1+2h^2-2h-1=0\ \Longrightarrow\ 3h^2-4h=0\ \Longrightarrow\ h(3h-4)=0.$$ \(h=0\) is a degenerate cone (volume 0), so \(h=\tfrac43\). [4 for algebra & correct answer] Then $$r^2=2\cdot\tfrac43\left(\tfrac43-1\right)=\tfrac83\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac89\ \Longrightarrow\ r=\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}.\quad\textbf{[2]}$$
Maximum volume when \(h=\dfrac43\) and \(r=\dfrac{2\sqrt2}{3}\), giving \(V_{\max}=\tfrac13\pi\cdot\tfrac89\cdot\tfrac43=\dfrac{32\pi}{81}\).
Justify it's a maximum (the official solution doesn't, but "justify all working" invites it): on the feasible set \(0\le h\le2\), \(V=0\) at both ends (\(h=0\): no height; \(h=2\): \(r=0\)), \(V\gt0\) in between and continuous, so the single interior candidate must be the max.
"You must use the method of Lagrange multipliers" — substituting \(r^2=2h-h^2\) into \(V\) and differentiating in one variable is correct maths but will not earn the method marks. Use it only as a private check.

Question 8 — Asteroid on a space curve [12 marks]

Topic: Ch 7 · Parametrised curves, velocity & speed

The planet Earth can be described as a unit sphere centered at \((0,0,0)\). An asteroid is approaching from the point \(P=(0,4,3)\) along the path $$\mathbf r(t)=\big((4-t)\sin(t),\ (4-t)\cos(t),\ 3-t\big)$$ at time \(t\).
6 marks(a) Determine the position of the asteroid when it hits Earth.
Worked solution
"Hits the surface" ⇒ substitute the path into the surface equation \(x^2+y^2+z^2=1\) and solve for \(t\). Take the first (smallest positive) time.
Earth: \(x^2+y^2+z^2=1\). [1] Substitute: [1] $$(4-t)^2\sin^2t+(4-t)^2\cos^2t+(3-t)^2=1\ \Longrightarrow\ (4-t)^2+(3-t)^2=1$$ $$16-8t+t^2+9-6t+t^2-1=0\ \Longrightarrow\ 2t^2-14t+24=0\ \Longrightarrow\ t^2-7t+12=0\ \Longrightarrow\ (t-3)(t-4)=0.$$ So \(t=3\) or \(t=4\); the asteroid first hits Earth at \(t=3\). [3] $$\mathbf r(3)=(1\cdot\sin3,\ 1\cdot\cos3,\ 0)=(\sin3,\ \cos3,\ 0).\quad\textbf{[1]}$$
Impact at \((\sin3,\ \cos3,\ 0)\) — on the equator, at \(t=3\).
\(t=4\) is when the path would exit the sphere (point \((0,0,-1)\)) — physically irrelevant, since it has already hit. Say "first" and pick \(t=3\).
Leave \(\sin3,\cos3\) exact — no calculator, and the marker expects exact form. (Check: \(\mathbf r(0)=(0,4,3)=P\) ✓.)
6 marks(b) Determine the asteroid's velocity and speed on impact.
Worked solution
Velocity \(=\mathbf r'(t)\) (product rule on each component), evaluated at \(t=3\); speed \(=\|\mathbf r'(3)\|\).
$$\mathbf r'(t)=\big(-\sin t+(4-t)\cos t,\ \ -\cos t-(4-t)\sin t,\ \ -1\big)\quad\textbf{[2]}$$ $$\mathbf r'(3)=\big(\cos3-\sin3,\ -\sin3-\cos3,\ -1\big).$$ $$\|\mathbf r'(3)\|=\sqrt{(\cos3-\sin3)^2+(\cos3+\sin3)^2+1}\quad\textbf{[2]}$$ $$=\sqrt{(\cos^23-2\sin3\cos3+\sin^23)+(\cos^23+2\sin3\cos3+\sin^23)+1}=\sqrt{1+1+1}.\quad\textbf{[2]}$$
Velocity \(\mathbf r'(3)=(\cos3-\sin3,\ -\sin3-\cos3,\ -1)\); speed \(=\sqrt3\).
The cross terms \(\mp2\sin3\cos3\) cancel — expect such cancellation in a no-calculator paper. If your speed has leftover \(\sin3\cos3\), recheck the product rule signs.

Question 9 — Potential function, curve sketch, work [13 marks]

Topic: Ch 7 · Conservative fields, curves & work

Let \(\mathbf F=P\mathbf i+Q\mathbf j\) be a force field, where \(P=3+2xy\) and \(Q=x^2-3y^2\).
1 mark(a) Show that \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\).
Worked solution $$\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=2x\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}=2x,$$ so they are equal. [1]
\(P_y=Q_x=2x\).
5 marks(b) Find a function \(f\) such that \(\mathbf F=\nabla f\).
Worked solution
Since \(P_y=Q_x\) on all of \(\mathbb R^2\), \(\mathbf F\) is conservative. Integrate \(P\) w.r.t. \(x\), add an unknown \(g(y)\), differentiate w.r.t. \(y\) and match with \(Q\).
\((f_x,f_y)=(3+2xy,\ x^2-3y^2)\). [1] $$f=\int(3+2xy)\,dx=3x+x^2y+g(y)\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ $$f_y=x^2+g'(y)\quad\textbf{[1]}\qquad\text{must equal}\ x^2-3y^2\ \Longrightarrow\ g'(y)=-3y^2,\ g(y)=-y^3.\quad\textbf{[1]}$$
\(f(x,y)=3x+x^2y-y^3\) (plus any constant). [1]
Writing \(g\) as a constant \(C\) instead of a function \(g(y)\) is the classic error — you lose the \(-y^3\) term. The "+ constant" only goes on at the very end.
3 marks(c) Let \(C\) be the curve given by \(\mathbf r(t)=(e^t\sin t)\,\mathbf i+(e^t\cos t)\,\mathbf j\), where \(0\le t\le\pi\). Sketch the curve \(C\).
Worked solution
Spot the polar structure: \(x=R\sin t,\ y=R\cos t\) with \(R=e^t\). So distance from the origin grows like \(e^t\) while the angle (measured from the positive \(y\)-axis, clockwise) is \(t\) — an expanding spiral. Plot key points \(t=0,\frac\pi2,\pi\).
$$x^2+y^2=e^{2t}\sin^2t+e^{2t}\cos^2t=e^{2t}\ \Longrightarrow\ \|\mathbf r\|=e^t.\quad\textbf{[1]}$$ So as \(t\) increases from \(0\) to \(\pi\), the distance from the origin increases from \(1\) to \(e^\pi\). [1]
\(t\)0\(\pi/2\)\(\pi\)
\(\mathbf r(t)\)\((0,1)\)\((e^{\pi/2},0)\)\((0,-e^\pi)\)

(Axes not to equal scale: \(e^\pi\approx23.1\), \(e^{\pi/2}\approx4.8\).)

A clockwise spiral in the right half-plane (\(x=e^t\sin t\ge0\)), starting at \((0,1)\), crossing the positive \(x\)-axis at \((e^{\pi/2},0)\) and ending at \((0,-e^\pi)\), moving steadily away from the origin. [1 for the sketch]
Mark the start and end points and the direction of travel — part (d) needs exactly those two endpoints.
4 marks(d) Determine the work done by the force field \(\mathbf F\) on a particle that moves along the curve \(C\).
Worked solution
\(\mathbf F=\nabla f\) (part b) ⇒ path independence: \(W=\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\). Never grind the spiral integral.
$$W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=\Big[f(x,y)\Big]_{(0,1)\ \textbf{[1]}}^{(0,-e^\pi)\ \textbf{[1]}}=\Big[3x+x^2y-y^3\Big]_{(0,1)}^{(0,-e^\pi)}\ \textbf{[1]}$$ $$=\big(0+0-(-e^\pi)^3\big)-\big(0+0-1\big)=e^{3\pi}+1.$$
\(W=e^{3\pi}+1\). [1]
\(-(-e^\pi)^3=+e^{3\pi}\): two minus signs. And the start value \(f(0,1)=-1\) is subtracted, giving \(+1\). Sign slips here are the only way to lose these marks.

What 2022 teaches

Patterns across both papers

  • No ODEs at all. Both 2022 papers are 100% multivariable calculus. The Ch 3–7 material is the exam; ODEs (Ch 1–2) are insurance, not the core.
  • Every question is one recognisable routine. Quadratic/linear approx · two-path test · chain-rule identity or applied \(dC/dt\) · directional derivative/steepest descent · critical points + Hessian · Lagrange · curve velocity/speed/arc length · conservative field → potential → \(f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\). Those 8 routines = 100% of both papers.
  • The two-path test appeared in both semesters (S1 Q3 with the \(y=x^3\) curve; S2 Q2 with \(y=x\)). Always match the degrees before choosing a path.
  • Lagrange appeared in both — and both times the winning case was the one students cancel away (\(x=0\) in S1; \(r\ne0\) must be argued in S2). Factor, never divide.
  • "Show conservative" → find the potential in both papers, then use it for the line integral in one line.
  • Degenerate Hessian (\(D=0\)) showed up in S2 Q6 worth 3 marks: restrict to a line and show a sign change.

Marker's-eye lessons (from the MS scan)

  • Writing the correct formula / definition earns marks before any numbers (3/5 for the limit definition of \(f_x\); 1 mark for the chain-rule template; 1 for the linear-approx template).
  • Sketch marks are almost all for labels: asymptotes and turning points (4/5 in S2 Q4b).
  • Yes/no conclusions ("decreasing", "not continuous", "saddle") carry a separate mark — write the sentence.
  • "You must use Lagrange multipliers" is enforced: substitution earns nothing for method.

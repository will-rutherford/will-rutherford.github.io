§1 · Concepts in depth

Rusty on the quadratic formula with a negative discriminant, \(i^2=-1\), the product rule, or \(\int x^{k}\,dx\)? Skim the Ground Floor (complex numbers, differentiation rules) first. Chapter 1's integrating factor is also used inside reduction of order below.

What we are solving

A linear second-order ODE (the summary's "L2O") is one that can be written

$$ y''+p(x)\,y'+q(x)\,y=r(x). $$

"Linear" means \(y\), \(y'\), \(y''\) appear only to the first power and are never multiplied together or fed into a function like \(\sin y\) or \(e^{y}\). The words you must be able to use precisely:

  • Homogeneous (HL2O): \(r(x)=0\). Example: \(y''-5y'+4y=0\).
  • Inhomogeneous / forced (IL2O): \(r(x)\ne0\). Example: \(y''+y'-2y=x^2\). The function \(r\) is the forcing term.
  • Constant coefficients: \(ay''+by'+cy=r(x)\) with \(a,b,c\) constants. This is the case with a complete recipe; almost every exam and tutorial question is of this type.

The independent variable may be \(x\) or \(t\) (time, in mechanics questions). Nothing changes except the letter. Dots mean time derivatives: \(\dot y=dy/dt\), \(\ddot y=d^2y/dt^2\) (tutorial 3 Q6–7 use this notation).

🧭 Why a second-order ODE has TWO arbitrary constants

To undo two derivatives you integrate twice, and each integration costs one constant. The simplest example, \(y''=0\), gives \(y'=A\) and then \(y=Ax+B\). The same holds in general: the general solution of a second-order ODE is a two-parameter family. So:

  • "Find the general solution" means keep both constants and state them: "\(y=Ae^{4x}+Be^{x},\ A,B\in\mathbb R\)".
  • An initial value problem (IVP) gives two conditions, normally \(y(x_0)\) and \(y'(x_0)\) (position and velocity), which fix both constants.

Physically: a mass on a spring needs where it starts and how fast it's moving. Knowing only the position isn't enough.

🧭 Superposition: why solutions can be added

Write \(L[y]=ay''+by'+cy\). Differentiation is linear, so \(L[c_1y_1+c_2y_2]=c_1L[y_1]+c_2L[y_2]\). This one line gives the whole structure of the chapter:

  1. Superposition principle (summary p 2): if \(y_1,y_2\) solve \(L[y]=0\), so does every \(c_1y_1+c_2y_2\).
  2. If \(y_1,y_2\) are linearly independent (\(y_2\ne k\,y_1\)), then \(c_1y_1+c_2y_2\) is the general homogeneous solution. Two solutions that are multiples of each other (e.g. \(e^{2x}\) and \(5e^{2x}\)) count as one.
  3. Inhomogeneous structure: if \(y_P\) is any one solution of \(L[y]=r\), every solution is $$ y=y_H+y_P,\qquad y_H=c_1y_1+c_2y_2 \text{ (the complementary function).} $$ Why: if \(y\) and \(y_P\) both solve \(L[y]=r\), then \(L[y-y_P]=r-r=0\), so \(y-y_P\) is a homogeneous solution.
  4. Forcing superposition: if \(L[y_{P1}]=r_1\) and \(L[y_{P2}]=r_2\), then \(L[y_{P1}+y_{P2}]=r_1+r_2\). So you can split a sum of forcings (e.g. \(3e^{x}+x\) in T03 Q7(i)) and handle each piece separately.

🧭 Why we try \(y=e^{\lambda x}\): the characteristic equation

For \(ay''+by'+cy=0\) we need a function whose derivatives are multiples of itself, so that \(ay''\), \(by'\) and \(cy\) can cancel. The exponential is exactly that: \((e^{\lambda x})'=\lambda e^{\lambda x}\), \((e^{\lambda x})''=\lambda^2e^{\lambda x}\). Substituting:

$$ (a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c)\,e^{\lambda x}=0 \iff a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0\quad(\text{since }e^{\lambda x}\ne0). $$

This quadratic is the characteristic equation. Differential equation → algebra. Read it straight off the ODE: \(y''\to\lambda^2\), \(y'\to\lambda\), \(y\to1\). The discriminant \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\) splits into three cases (summary Recipe #4):

\(\Delta=b^2-4ac\)RootsGeneral solution \(y_H\)
\(\Delta\gt0\)two real \(\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2\)\(c_1e^{\lambda_1x}+c_2e^{\lambda_2x}\)
\(\Delta=0\)one repeated \(\mu=-\dfrac{b}{2a}\)\((c_1+c_2x)\,e^{\mu x}\)
\(\Delta\lt0\)complex pair \(\alpha\pm\beta i\), with \(\alpha=-\dfrac{b}{2a},\ \beta=\dfrac{\sqrt{4ac-b^2}}{2a}\)\(e^{\alpha x}\big(c_1\cos\beta x+c_2\sin\beta x\big)\)

🧭 Why the repeated root gets an extra factor \(x\)

If \(\Delta=0\) the exponential trick gives only one solution, \(e^{\mu x}\), but we need two independent ones. The fix is reduction of order (summary Recipe #3): try \(y=u(x)e^{\mu x}\). Substituting into \(ay''+by'+cy=0\) and using \(a\mu^2+b\mu+c=0\) and \(2a\mu+b=0\) (both true for a double root) leaves just \(a\,u''e^{\mu x}=0\), so \(u''=0\), giving \(u=c_1+c_2x\). Hence $$ y=(c_1+c_2x)\,e^{\mu x}. $$ The summary notes this was obtained "using ROO". You'll see the full derivation in §8.5 Worked A.

🧭 Why complex roots become cos and sin

If \(\lambda=\alpha\pm\beta i\), the two exponential solutions are \(e^{(\alpha+\beta i)x}\) and \(e^{(\alpha-\beta i)x}\). By Euler's formula \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\):

$$ e^{(\alpha\pm\beta i)x}=e^{\alpha x}\big(\cos\beta x\pm i\sin\beta x\big). $$

Adding and subtracting these (superposition) and rescaling gives the two real solutions \(e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x\) and \(e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x\). So the answer contains no \(i\) at all:

$$ \boxed{y=e^{\alpha x}\big(A\cos\beta x+B\sin\beta x\big)} $$

Read the picture off it: \(\cos/\sin\) means oscillation at angular frequency \(\beta\), and \(e^{\alpha x}\) is the envelope: it decays if \(\alpha\lt0\), grows if \(\alpha\gt0\), and stays constant if \(\alpha=0\) (pure oscillation, e.g. \(y''+3y=0\to A\cos\sqrt3x+B\sin\sqrt3x\), T03 Q2b).

Presentation that earns full marks: (1) write \(\lambda=\alpha\pm\beta i\) with the numbers, (2) say "so \(\alpha=\ldots,\ \beta=\ldots\)", (3) write \(y=e^{\alpha x}(A\cos\beta x+B\sin\beta x),\ A,B\in\mathbb R\). Never leave \(e^{(1+2i)x}\) in a final answer, and don't write \(\cos(\alpha\pm\beta i)x\).

🧭 Undetermined coefficients: the forcing tells you the shape of \(y_P\)

To find one particular solution of \(ay''+by'+cy=r(x)\), guess a \(y_P\) with the same shape as \(r\), leave its coefficients unknown, substitute, and match coefficients (summary Recipe #5). This works because polynomials, exponentials and sin/cos form families that are closed under differentiation: differentiating \(x^2\) gives lower powers, differentiating \(e^{kx}\) gives \(e^{kx}\), and differentiating \(\cos\omega x\) gives \(\sin\omega x\) and back. So \(L[\text{trial}]\) stays in the same family and can be matched against \(r\).

  • \(r\) a polynomial of degree \(n\) → trial is a full polynomial of degree \(n\): all lower powers and the constant included.
  • \(r=Ce^{kx}\) → trial \(Ae^{kx}\).
  • \(r\) contains \(\cos\omega x\) or \(\sin\omega x\) → trial \(A\cos\omega x+B\sin\omega x\) (both, always).
  • products (e.g. \(xe^{x}\), \(e^{2x}\cos x\)) → product of the trials: \((ax+b)e^{x}\), \(e^{2x}(A\cos x+B\sin x)\).

🧭 The overlap (resonance) rule, and why it's needed

If your trial is already a solution of the homogeneous equation, then \(L[\text{trial}]=0\) and it can never equal \(r\ne0\). You'll see this as a contradiction like "\(0\cdot A=6\)". The fix:

Modification rule. If any term of the trial appears in \(y_H\), multiply the whole trial by \(x\). If it still overlaps (this happens with a repeated root), multiply by \(x\) again (\(x^2\)). Never go higher than \(x^2\) for a second-order ODE.

Why \(x\) works: it's the same mechanism as the repeated root. Substituting \(y_P=u(x)e^{kx}\) when \(k\) is a root makes the \(u\) term cancel, leaving an equation in \(u',u''\) only, whose polynomial solution is one degree higher. This is exactly the Poster B method ("let \(y_{P}=u(x)e^{Bx}\) … hope that the coefficient of \(u\) vanishes"). Tutorial 3 Q9 is the same idea for polynomials: if \(c=0\), the constant \(1=e^{0x}\) is a homogeneous solution, so a polynomial forcing of degree \(n\) needs a trial of degree \(n+1\).

In mechanics this is resonance: forcing an undamped spring at its natural frequency \(\omega\) gives \(y_P\propto t\cos\omega t\) or \(t\sin\omega t\), an oscillation whose amplitude grows without bound.

🧭 Reduction of order: when you're handed one solution

For variable-coefficient equations like \(2x^2y''-3xy'-3y=0\) (2020 S2 Q3) there's no characteristic equation. If you are given (or can guess) one solution \(y_1\), set \(y=u\,y_1\). The \(u\)-terms cancel (because \(y_1\) is a solution) and you're left with a first-order linear ODE for \(v=u'\):

$$ u''+u'\left(p+\frac{2y_1'}{y_1}\right)=0\qquad\text{where the ODE is in standard form } y''+p\,y'+q\,y=0. $$

That's summary Recipe #3 verbatim ("enlist little sister to find \(y_1\)"). Solve for \(v\) (separable, or by integrating factor), integrate once to get \(u\), and \(y_2=u\,y_1\). Guessing \(y_1=x^n\) for an equation with \(x^2y''\), \(xy'\), \(y\) terms reduces to a quadratic in \(n\).

🧭 The spring–mass–damper: the physics behind the three cases

Summary p 3 sets it up. A spring of natural length \(L\) is stretched a distance \(s\) by a mass \(m\). Measure \(x\) = displacement below the equilibrium position (positive x-axis pointing down).

  • Hooke: spring force \(=-k(s+x)\). At equilibrium the spring balances the weight: \(ks=mg\), so \(k=mg/s\). This is how you get \(k\) from "the mass stretches the spring by …".
  • Newton's 2nd law: \(mx''=mg-k(s+x)-\beta x'=-kx-\beta x'\). The weight cancels against \(ks\), which is why we measure from equilibrium: $$ mx''+\beta x'+kx=0\qquad(\beta\ge0\text{ damping constant, N s m}^{-1}). $$
  • Undamped (\(\beta=0\)): \(x''+\omega^2x=0\), \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\), so \(x=c_1\cos\omega t+c_2\sin\omega t=R\cos(\omega t-\phi)\) with amplitude \(R=\sqrt{c_1^2+c_2^2}\). This is simple harmonic motion, period \(2\pi/\omega\).
\(\Delta=\beta^2-4mk\)NameMotionSummary's picture
\(\gt0\)Overdamped\(c_1e^{\lambda_1t}+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\), both \(\lambda\lt0\): creeps back and crosses equilibrium at most oncedoor that takes ages to close
\(=0\)Critically damped\((c_1+c_2t)e^{\mu t}\), \(\mu=-\beta/2m\): fastest return with no oscillationdoor that closes in the shortest time
\(\lt0\)Underdamped\(e^{\alpha t}(c_1\cos\omega_d t+c_2\sin\omega_d t)\), \(\alpha=-\beta/2m\lt0\): oscillation inside a decaying envelopechef's swinging doors

With every coefficient positive (\(m,\beta,k\gt0\)), all three cases decay to 0. In every case the real part of each root is negative (tutorial 3 Q6).

🧭 Forced oscillations: transient + steady state

Add an external force \(F(t)\): \(mx''+\beta x'+kx=F(t)\). The solution is still \(x=x_H+x_P\), but now each piece has a physical name:

  • \(x_H\) = transient. With damping (\(\beta\gt0\)) it decays to 0, so the initial conditions are eventually forgotten.
  • \(x_P\) = steady state. For \(F=F_0\cos\gamma t\) it is an oscillation at the driving frequency \(\gamma\), \(x_P=A\cos\gamma t+B\sin\gamma t\), with amplitude \(\sqrt{A^2+B^2}\).
  • Undamped and driven at \(\gamma=\omega\) → overlap → \(x_P\propto t\sin\omega t\): resonance, where the amplitude grows linearly forever.
Same letter, two meanings. The course summary uses \(\beta\) for both the imaginary part of a complex root (\(\alpha\pm\beta i\)) and the damping constant (\(mx''+\beta x'+kx=0\)). In a spring question, write the roots with numbers (e.g. \(\lambda=-4\pm2i\)) so the two never get confused. The imaginary part in the damped case is \(\omega_d=\sqrt{4mk-\beta^2}/(2m)\), which is not the damping constant.

§2 · Key methods & when to use them

None of this is on the exam formula sheet (the sheet only covers the multivariable topics). Every recipe below must come from memory. They are short, so drill them until they're automatic.

Method 1 · Homogeneous, constant coefficients (Recipe #4)

$$ ay''+by'+cy=0\ \longrightarrow\ a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0 $$
Any "solve / find the general solution" with constant coefficients and \(r=0\), and always as step 1 of an inhomogeneous problem (the complementary function).
  1. Write the characteristic equation (read off: \(y''\to\lambda^2,\ y'\to\lambda,\ y\to1\)).
  2. Solve: factorise if you can, otherwise \(\lambda=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\).
  3. Pick the form from \(\Delta\): distinct → \(c_1e^{\lambda_1x}+c_2e^{\lambda_2x}\); repeated → \((c_1+c_2x)e^{\mu x}\); complex → \(e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos\beta x+c_2\sin\beta x)\).
  4. State "\(c_1,c_2\in\mathbb R\)".

Mini-example. \(y''+6y'+13y=0\): \(\lambda^2+6\lambda+13=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\frac{-6\pm\sqrt{36-52}}{2}=-3\pm2i\Rightarrow y=e^{-3x}(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)\).

Method 2 · Initial value problems

"Solve the IVP", "given \(y(0)=\ldots,\ y'(0)=\ldots\)", "released from rest", "starts at equilibrium with velocity …".
  1. Find the full general solution first (\(y_H+y_P\) if forced).
  2. Differentiate the general solution to get \(y'\) (product rule on \(e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x\) and \(xe^{\mu x}\)).
  3. Substitute both conditions → two linear equations in \(c_1,c_2\) → solve.
  4. Write the final \(y\) with numbers, then check \(y(0)\) quickly.

Shortcuts at \(x=0\) (\(y'(0)\) with no product-rule working): distinct: \(y(0)=c_1+c_2,\ y'(0)=\lambda_1c_1+\lambda_2c_2\). Repeated: \(y(0)=c_1,\ y'(0)=\mu c_1+c_2\). Complex: \(y(0)=c_1,\ y'(0)=\alpha c_1+\beta c_2\).

A term of your trial already appears in \(y_H\): \(r=e^{kx}\) with \(k\) a characteristic root; \(r=\cos\omega x\) with \(\pm\omega i\) roots; \(r\) a polynomial when \(c=0\) (\(\lambda=0\) is a root).

Method 5 · Reduction of order (Recipe #3)

$$ y=u\,y_1,\qquad u''+u'\Big(p+\frac{2y_1'}{y_1}\Big)=0,\qquad v=u' $$
"Given that \(y_1=\ldots\) is a solution, find a second linearly independent solution / the general solution", or "use the method of reduction of order". Also any variable-coefficient ODE (e.g. \(x^2y''+\ldots\)) with a trial \(y=x^n\).
  1. If asked, find \(n\) by substituting \(y=x^n\): \(y'=nx^{n-1}\), \(y''=n(n-1)x^{n-2}\) → quadratic in \(n\).
  2. Divide by the \(y''\) coefficient to reach standard form and read off \(p(x)\).
  3. Compute \(p+2y_1'/y_1\) and set \(v=u'\): \(v'+v\,(p+2y_1'/y_1)=0\).
  4. Solve for \(v\) (separable: \(\ln|v|=\ldots\)); take the constant \(=0\) or keep \(A\).
  5. \(u=\int v\,dx\) → \(y_2=u\,y_1\) → \(y=Ay_1+By_2\).

Method 6 · Spring–mass–damper models

$$ mx''+\beta x'+kx=F(t),\qquad k=\frac{mg}{s},\qquad \Delta=\beta^2-4mk $$
Any question with a mass on a spring, "damping constant", "released", "overdamped/underdamped/critically damped", "steady state", "resonance".
  1. Units: SI (kg, m, N, s); take \(g=9.8\) m s\(^{-2}\) unless told otherwise.
  2. \(k\) from the equilibrium stretch: \(k=mg/s\), where \(s=\) equilibrium length \(-\) natural length.
  3. Set \(x\) = displacement below equilibrium. ICs: "stretched to length \(\ell\)" means \(x(0)=\ell-(\text{equilibrium length})\). "Released from rest" means \(x'(0)=0\). "Pushed up" means \(x'(0)\lt0\).
  4. Write \(mx''+\beta x'+kx=0\), divide by \(m\), solve, and classify by \(\Delta\).
  5. Critical damping constant: \(\beta_c=\sqrt{4mk}=2\sqrt{mk}\).

Method 7 · Amplitude–phase form

$$ c_1\cos\omega t+c_2\sin\omega t=R\cos(\omega t-\phi),\qquad R=\sqrt{c_1^2+c_2^2},\quad \tan\phi=\frac{c_2}{c_1} $$
"Find the amplitude", "maximum displacement" (undamped), "steady-state amplitude". Choose \(\phi\) in the quadrant of \((c_1,c_2)\).

Mini-example. \(\cos t+\sin t=\sqrt2\cos(t-\pi/4)\): amplitude \(\sqrt2\).

Example 1 — Distinct real roots, IVP: \(y''-y'-6y=0,\ y(0)=3,\ y'(0)=4\)

Q. Solve the initial value problem \(y''-y'-6y=0\), \(y(0)=3\), \(y'(0)=4\). [5 marks]

Characteristic equation. Trying \(y=e^{\lambda t}\) gives $$\lambda^2-\lambda-6=0\iff(\lambda-3)(\lambda+2)=0\iff\lambda=3\ \text{or}\ \lambda=-2.$$ Two distinct real roots, so the general solution is $$ y=c_1e^{3t}+c_2e^{-2t},\qquad c_1,c_2\in\mathbb R. $$

Apply the ICs. \(y'=3c_1e^{3t}-2c_2e^{-2t}\). $$ y(0)=c_1+c_2=3,\qquad y'(0)=3c_1-2c_2=4. $$ Doubling the first and adding: \(5c_1=10\Rightarrow c_1=2\), then \(c_2=1\).

$$\boxed{y=2e^{3t}+e^{-2t}}$$

Check: \(y(0)=2+1=3\) ✓, \(y'(0)=6-2=4\) ✓. It takes ten seconds and catches most sign slips.

Example 2 — Repeated root, IVP: \(y''+6y'+9y=0,\ y(0)=2,\ y'(0)=-1\)

Q. Solve \(y''+6y'+9y=0\) with \(y(0)=2\), \(y'(0)=-1\). [5 marks]

\(\lambda^2+6\lambda+9=(\lambda+3)^2=0\Rightarrow\lambda=-3\) (repeated, \(\Delta=36-36=0\)). So $$ y=(c_1+c_2t)e^{-3t}. $$ \(y'=c_2e^{-3t}-3(c_1+c_2t)e^{-3t}\) (product rule). Then $$ y(0)=c_1=2,\qquad y'(0)=c_2-3c_1=-1\Rightarrow c_2=5. $$

$$\boxed{y=(2+5t)e^{-3t}}$$

Writing \(y=c_1e^{-3t}+c_2e^{-3t}\) is wrong: that is really only one constant \((c_1+c_2)\), so no choice of constants can meet both ICs. The repeated root needs the \(t\).

Example 3 — Complex roots, IVP: \(y''-4y'+13y=0,\ y(0)=1,\ y'(0)=8\)

Q. Solve \(y''-4y'+13y=0\), \(y(0)=1\), \(y'(0)=8\). Describe the long-term behaviour. [6 marks]

\(\lambda^2-4\lambda+13=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\dfrac{4\pm\sqrt{16-52}}{2}=\dfrac{4\pm6i}{2}=2\pm3i\). So \(\alpha=2,\ \beta=3\) and $$ y=e^{2t}(A\cos3t+B\sin3t),\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R. $$ \(y(0)=A=1\). Using the shortcut \(y'(0)=\alpha A+\beta B\): \(2(1)+3B=8\Rightarrow B=2\). (Long way: \(y'=e^{2t}\big[(2A+3B)\cos3t+(2B-3A)\sin3t\big]\), so \(y'(0)=2A+3B\).)

$$\boxed{y=e^{2t}(\cos3t+2\sin3t)}$$

Behaviour: since \(\alpha=2\gt0\), the solution oscillates with angular frequency 3 inside a growing envelope \(\pm\sqrt5\,e^{2t}\). The oscillations grow without bound.

Example 4 — Superposition of forcings: \(y''-2y'-3y=6x+1+3e^{2x}\)

Q. Find the general solution of \(y''-2y'-3y=6x+1+3e^{2x}\). [8 marks]

Step 1 — \(y_H\). \(\lambda^2-2\lambda-3=(\lambda-3)(\lambda+1)=0\Rightarrow\lambda=3,-1\). \(y_H=Ae^{3x}+Be^{-x}\).

Step 2 — trial. The forcing is (degree-1 polynomial) + (exponential with \(k=2\)). Neither overlaps \(y_H\) (the roots are 3 and \(-1\), not 0 or 2), so $$ y_P=ax+b+Ce^{2x},\quad y_P'=a+2Ce^{2x},\quad y_P''=4Ce^{2x}. $$

Step 3 — substitute and match. $$ 4Ce^{2x}-2(a+2Ce^{2x})-3(ax+b+Ce^{2x})=-3ax+(-2a-3b)+(4-4-3)Ce^{2x}. $$ Match with \(6x+1+3e^{2x}\): $$ x:\ -3a=6\Rightarrow a=-2;\qquad 1:\ -2a-3b=1\Rightarrow 4-3b=1\Rightarrow b=1;\qquad e^{2x}:\ -3C=3\Rightarrow C=-1. $$

$$\boxed{y=Ae^{3x}+Be^{-x}-2x+1-e^{2x},\quad A,B\in\mathbb R}$$

You may split it: solve with \(r_1=6x+1\) and \(r_2=3e^{2x}\) separately and add the \(y_P\)'s. That's the superposition principle, and it keeps the algebra tidy.

Example 5 — Trig forcing: \(y''+2y'+5y=10\cos x\)

Q. Find the general solution of \(y''+2y'+5y=10\cos x\). [7 marks]

\(y_H\): \(\lambda^2+2\lambda+5=0\Rightarrow\lambda=-1\pm2i\), so \(y_H=e^{-x}(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)\).

Trial: the forcing has \(\cos x\), so use both \(\cos x\) and \(\sin x\): \(y_P=C\cos x+D\sin x\). No overlap (\(\pm i\) are not roots). $$ y_P'=-C\sin x+D\cos x,\qquad y_P''=-C\cos x-D\sin x. $$ $$ y_P''+2y_P'+5y_P=(-C+2D+5C)\cos x+(-D-2C+5D)\sin x=(4C+2D)\cos x+(4D-2C)\sin x. $$ Match with \(10\cos x+0\sin x\): \(4C+2D=10\) and \(4D-2C=0\Rightarrow C=2D\Rightarrow 10D=10\Rightarrow D=1,\ C=2\).

$$\boxed{y=e^{-x}(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)+2\cos x+\sin x}$$

Trying only \(y_P=C\cos x\) fails: the \(2y'\) term produces \(\sin x\), and you'd need \(-2C=0\) and \(4C=10\) at the same time. Always include both sin and cos.

Example 6 — The overlap rule: \(y''-y'-2y=6e^{2x}\), and the double-overlap version

Q. (a) Find a particular solution of \(y''-y'-2y=6e^{2x}\). (b) Find a particular solution of \(y''-4y'+4y=6e^{2x}\). [6 marks]

(a) \(\lambda^2-\lambda-2=(\lambda-2)(\lambda+1)\Rightarrow\lambda=2,-1\), \(y_H=Ae^{2x}+Be^{-x}\).

Naive trial \(Ce^{2x}\): substituting gives \((4-2-2)Ce^{2x}=0\ne6e^{2x}\). Impossible, because \(e^{2x}\) is already in \(y_H\). Overlap (simple root) → multiply by \(x\): $$ y_P=Cxe^{2x},\quad y_P'=C(1+2x)e^{2x},\quad y_P''=C(4+4x)e^{2x}. $$ $$ y_P''-y_P'-2y_P=C\big[(4+4x)-(1+2x)-2x\big]e^{2x}=3Ce^{2x}=6e^{2x}\Rightarrow C=2. $$ So \(y_P=2xe^{2x}\) and \(y=Ae^{2x}+Be^{-x}+2xe^{2x}\). (Shortcut check: \(C/P'(2)=6/(2\cdot2-1)=2\) ✓.)

(b) \(\lambda^2-4\lambda+4=(\lambda-2)^2\): \(\lambda=2\) is a double root and \(y_H=(A+Bx)e^{2x}\). Both \(e^{2x}\) and \(xe^{2x}\) are taken, so the trial becomes \(y_P=Cx^2e^{2x}\). $$ y_P'=C(2x+2x^2)e^{2x},\quad y_P''=C(2+8x+4x^2)e^{2x}, $$ $$ y_P''-4y_P'+4y_P=C\big[(2+8x+4x^2)-(8x+8x^2)+4x^2\big]e^{2x}=2Ce^{2x}=6e^{2x}\Rightarrow C=3. $$

$$\boxed{\text{(a) }y_P=2xe^{2x}\qquad\text{(b) }y_P=3x^2e^{2x}}$$

Notice how everything with \(x\) cancels: that's the sign your modification was right. If an \(x\)-term survives, you've made an algebra slip.

Example 7 — Reduction of order: \(x^2y''-3xy'+4y=0\), given \(y_1=x^2\)

Q. Verify that \(y_1=x^2\) solves \(x^2y''-3xy'+4y=0\) for \(x\gt0\), and use reduction of order to find the general solution. [8 marks]

Verify: \(x^2(2)-3x(2x)+4x^2=2x^2-6x^2+4x^2=0\) ✓.

Standard form: divide by \(x^2\): \(y''-\dfrac{3}{x}y'+\dfrac{4}{x^2}y=0\), so \(p(x)=-\dfrac3x\).

Reduction of order. Let \(y=u\,x^2\). Then \(u\) satisfies $$ u''+u'\Big(p+\frac{2y_1'}{y_1}\Big)=u''+u'\Big(-\frac3x+\frac{2(2x)}{x^2}\Big)=u''+\frac1x\,u'=0. $$ Let \(v=u'\): \(v'=-\dfrac{v}{x}\). \(v=0\) is an equilibrium solution (it gives nothing new). For \(v\ne0\): \(\dfrac{dv}{v}=-\dfrac{dx}{x}\Rightarrow\ln|v|=-\ln x+C\Rightarrow v=\dfrac{A}{x}\). Then \(u=\int v\,dx=A\ln x+B\).

$$\boxed{y=u\,x^2=Ax^2\ln x+Bx^2,\quad A,B\in\mathbb R,\ x\gt0}$$

Taking \(A=1,B=0\) gives the second solution \(y_2=x^2\ln x\). It isn't a constant multiple of \(x^2\), so the two are linearly independent. (Trying \(y=x^n\) here gives \(n^2-4n+4=(n-2)^2=0\), a double root. That's why the second solution isn't another power.)

Example 8 — Damped spring from physical data (classification + motion)

Q. A mass of 2 kg stretches a spring 0.49 m beyond its natural length when hanging at rest. The system is immersed in a fluid with damping constant \(16\) N s m\(^{-1}\). The mass is pulled down 0.1 m below equilibrium and released from rest. Take \(g=9.8\) m s\(^{-2}\). (a) Find \(k\). (b) Find \(x(t)\), the displacement below equilibrium. (c) Classify the damping. (d) What damping constant would make the system critically damped? [10 marks]

(a) At equilibrium \(ks=mg\Rightarrow k=\dfrac{2(9.8)}{0.49}=40\) N m\(^{-1}\).

(b) \(mx''+\beta x'+kx=0\Rightarrow 2x''+16x'+40x=0\Rightarrow x''+8x'+20x=0\). \(\lambda^2+8\lambda+20=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\dfrac{-8\pm\sqrt{64-80}}{2}=-4\pm2i\). $$ x=e^{-4t}(A\cos2t+B\sin2t). $$ ICs: \(x(0)=0.1\) (down is positive), \(x'(0)=0\) (released from rest). \(x(0)=A=0.1\); \(x'(0)=-4A+2B=0\Rightarrow B=0.2\). $$\boxed{x(t)=e^{-4t}\big(0.1\cos2t+0.2\sin2t\big)\ \text{m}}$$

(c) \(\Delta=\beta^2-4mk=256-320=-64\lt0\) → underdamped. The mass oscillates (angular frequency 2) with amplitude decaying like \(e^{-4t}\).

(d) Critical: \(\beta^2=4mk=320\Rightarrow\beta=\sqrt{320}=8\sqrt5\approx17.9\) N s m\(^{-1}\).

Don't forget to divide by \(m=2\) before reading off the characteristic equation, or use \(2\lambda^2+16\lambda+40=0\) directly. Both give the same roots. What goes wrong is using \(\lambda^2+16\lambda+40\).

Example 9 — Forced + damped: transient and steady state, \(x''+2x'+2x=10\cos2t\)

Q. A damped oscillator satisfies \(x''+2x'+2x=10\cos2t\). (a) Find the general solution. (b) Identify the transient and steady-state parts. (c) Find the amplitude of the steady-state motion. [9 marks]

(a) \(\lambda^2+2\lambda+2=0\Rightarrow\lambda=-1\pm i\): \(x_H=e^{-t}(A\cos t+B\sin t)\). Trial \(x_P=C\cos2t+D\sin2t\) (no overlap: \(\pm2i\) are not roots). $$ x_P''+2x_P'+2x_P=(-4C+4D+2C)\cos2t+(-4D-4C+2D)\sin2t=(-2C+4D)\cos2t+(-4C-2D)\sin2t. $$ Match: \(-2C+4D=10\), \(-4C-2D=0\Rightarrow D=-2C\Rightarrow-2C-8C=10\Rightarrow C=-1,\ D=2\). $$\boxed{x=e^{-t}(A\cos t+B\sin t)-\cos2t+2\sin2t}$$

(b) Transient: \(e^{-t}(A\cos t+B\sin t)\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\). Steady state: \(-\cos2t+2\sin2t\), which oscillates at the driving frequency 2.

(c) Amplitude \(=\sqrt{(-1)^2+2^2}=\sqrt5\).

Example 10 — Undamped resonance: \(x''+9x=12\sin3t,\ x(0)=0,\ x'(0)=0\)

Q. An undamped spring starting at rest at equilibrium is driven by \(x''+9x=12\sin3t\). Solve for \(x(t)\) and describe the motion. [9 marks]

\(\lambda^2+9=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm3i\): \(x_H=A\cos3t+B\sin3t\), natural frequency \(\omega=3\). The forcing frequency is also 3, so the usual trial \(C\cos3t+D\sin3t\) overlaps \(x_H\). Multiply by \(t\): $$ x_P=t(C\cos3t+D\sin3t). $$ $$ x_P''=(6D-9Ct)\cos3t+(-6C-9Dt)\sin3t\ \Rightarrow\ x_P''+9x_P=6D\cos3t-6C\sin3t. $$ Match \(12\sin3t\): \(6D=0,\ -6C=12\Rightarrow C=-2,\ D=0\). So \(x_P=-2t\cos3t\). General: \(x=A\cos3t+B\sin3t-2t\cos3t\). \(x(0)=A=0\). \(x'=3B\cos3t-2\cos3t+6t\sin3t\) (with \(A=0\)), so \(x'(0)=3B-2=0\Rightarrow B=\tfrac23\).

$$\boxed{x(t)=\tfrac23\sin3t-2t\cos3t}$$

Motion: the \(-2t\cos3t\) term oscillates with amplitude \(2t\), which grows without bound. This is resonance: the driving frequency equals the natural frequency and there's no damping to limit it.

  2. Write \(y_H\) in the correct case form, with "\(A,B\in\mathbb R\)".

📄 Real exam question — 2020 S1 Q7 [10 marks]

Find the general solution \(y(x)\) to the ODE $$ 4y''-4y'+5y=e^{3x}. $$

Full worked solution — 2020 S1 Q7 (follows the official solution)

Solve for \(y_H\). Characteristic equation: $$ 4\lambda^2-4\lambda+5=0\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{16-4(4)(5)}}{2(4)}=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{-64}}{8}=\frac{4\pm8i}{8}=\frac12\pm i. $$ So \(\alpha=\tfrac12,\ \beta=1\) and $$ y_H=e^{x/2}\big(A\sin x+B\cos x\big),\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R. $$

Find \(y_P\). Guess \(y_P=ae^{3x}\) (3 is not a root, so there's no overlap). \(y_P'=3ae^{3x}\), \(y_P''=9ae^{3x}\). Substitute: $$ 36ae^{3x}-12ae^{3x}+5ae^{3x}=e^{3x}\ \Rightarrow\ 29a=1\ \Rightarrow\ a=\tfrac1{29}. $$

$$\boxed{y=y_H+y_P=e^{x/2}\big(A\sin x+B\cos x\big)+\frac{e^{3x}}{29},\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R}$$

The official solution is laid out exactly as "Solve for \(y_H\)" → "Find \(y_P\)" → "\(y=y_H+y_P\)" and ends with "\(A,B\in\mathbb R\)". Copy that skeleton. The common slip here is forgetting the leading 4: dividing by \(2a=8\), not 2, gives \(\tfrac12\pm i\), not \(2\pm4i\).

Type B — Find \(n\) for a power solution, then reduction of order 2020 S2 Q3 · 10 marks

Looks like: a variable-coefficient ODE (\(x^2y''\), \(xy'\), \(y\)), "\(y=x^n\) is a solution for some integer \(n\)", then "use reduction of order to find the general solution". Tutorial twin: T03 Q1 (\(xy''-3y'-12y/x=0\), \(y_1=x^6\), answer \(y_2=x^{-2}\)).

📄 Real exam question — 2020 S2 Q3 [10 marks]

For some positive integer \(n\), the function \(y=x^n\) is a solution of the ODE $$ 2x^2y''-3xy'-3y=0. $$ Find the value of \(n\). Use the obtained solution and the method of reduction of order to find the general solution of the ODE, for \(x\gt0\).

Full worked solution — 2020 S2 Q3 (with the marking scheme's point allocation)

Find \(n\) [2 + 1]. \(y=x^n\Rightarrow y'=nx^{n-1},\ y''=n(n-1)x^{n-2}\). Substituting: $$ \big(2n(n-1)-3n-3\big)x^n=0\ \Rightarrow\ 2n^2-5n-3=0\ \Rightarrow\ (2n+1)(n-3)=0. $$ \(n\) must be a positive integer, so \(n=3\) and \(y_1=x^3\).

Standard form [1]. Divide by \(2x^2\): $$ y''-\frac{3}{2x}y'-\frac{3}{2x^2}y=0,\qquad p(x)=-\frac{3}{2x}. $$

Reduction of order [1 + 1]. With \(y=u\,x^3\), \(u\) satisfies \(u''+u'\big(p+\tfrac{2y_1'}{y_1}\big)=0\): $$ u''+u'\Big(-\frac{3}{2x}+\frac{2(3x^2)}{x^3}\Big)=u''+u'\Big(-\frac3{2x}+\frac6x\Big)=u''+\frac{9}{2x}u'=0. $$

Solve for \(v=u'\) [1 + 1]. \(v'+\dfrac{9}{2x}v=0\). \(v=0\) is an equilibrium solution. If \(v\ne0\): $$ \frac{v'}{v}=-\frac{9}{2x}\Rightarrow\ln|v|=-\tfrac92\ln|x|+C\Rightarrow v=Ax^{-9/2},\ A\in\mathbb R\quad(x\gt0). $$

Integrate [1]. \(u=\int v\,dx=-\tfrac27Ax^{-7/2}+B=\tilde A x^{-7/2}+B\).

General solution [1].

$$\boxed{y=u\,x^3=\tilde A x^{-1/2}+Bx^{3},\qquad \tilde A,B\in\mathbb R,\ x\gt0}$$

Marking scheme: 2 for substituting \(x^n\) correctly, 1 for \(n=3\), 1 for the standard form (you must divide by \(2x^2\) to get \(p\)), 1 for quoting the ROO equation, 1 for substituting \(p\) and \(y_1\), 1 for noting \(v=0\) is an equilibrium solution, 1 for \(v\), 1 for \(u\), 1 for the general solution. The other root \(n=-\tfrac12\) is a useful check: it's exactly the second solution you derive.

The most expensive slip is using \(p=-3x\) (the un-divided coefficient). The whole \(v\) equation is then wrong and you lose every mark after the standard-form mark.

Type C — Homogeneous IVP poster / tutorial level · ~5–6 marks

📄 Poster B example: Solve the IVP \(y''+2y'+2y=0,\ y(0)=1,\ y'(0)=-1\).

Full worked solution — Poster B IVP

\(\lambda^2+2\lambda+2=0\Rightarrow\lambda=-1\pm i\), so \(\alpha=-1,\ \beta=1\) and \(y=e^{-t}(C_1\cos t+C_2\sin t)\). \(y(0)=C_1=1\). \(y'(0)=\alpha C_1+\beta C_2=-1+C_2=-1\Rightarrow C_2=0\).

Type D — Spring–mass model from words tutorial level (T03 Q3) · ~10 marks

Looks like: natural length, equilibrium length, a mass, "stretched to … and released", a damping constant, "is the system over/under/critically damped?"

📄 Tutorial 3 Q3. A mass of 2 kg is attached to a spring hanging from a fixed support. The natural length of the spring is 0.5 m. The hanging mass causes the spring to have an equilibrium length of 0.7 m. (a) If the spring is stretched to a length of 1 m and then released with initial velocity 0 m s\(^{-1}\), find the position of the mass at any time \(t\). (b) Suppose that the spring and mass system is immersed in a fluid with damping constant 100 N s m\(^{-1}\). Find the position of the mass at any time \(t\) if it starts from the equilibrium position and is given a push to start it with an initial downward velocity of 0.6 m s\(^{-1}\). Is the system overdamped, underdamped or critically-damped?

Full worked solution — T03 Q3

\(k\): stretch at equilibrium \(s=0.7-0.5=0.2\) m, so \(2g=0.2k\Rightarrow k=\dfrac{2(9.8)}{0.2}=98\) N m\(^{-1}\).

(a) \(x''+\tfrac km x=0\Rightarrow x''+49x=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm7i\), \(x=c_1\cos7t+c_2\sin7t\). \(x\) is measured down from equilibrium, so \(x(0)=1-0.7=0.3\) m and \(x'(0)=0\). That gives \(c_1=0.3,\ 7c_2=0\): $$\boxed{x(t)=0.3\cos7t}$$

(b) \(2x''+100x'+98x=0\Rightarrow x''+50x'+49x=0\Rightarrow(\lambda+1)(\lambda+49)=0\), \(\lambda=-1,-49\). \(x=c_1e^{-t}+c_2e^{-49t}\). \(x(0)=c_1+c_2=0\), \(x'(0)=-c_1-49c_2=0.6\Rightarrow-48c_2=0.6\Rightarrow c_2=-\tfrac1{80},\ c_1=\tfrac1{80}\). $$\boxed{x(t)=\tfrac1{80}\big(e^{-t}-e^{-49t}\big)}$$ Two distinct real roots (\(\Delta=100^2-4(2)(98)=9216\gt0\)) → overdamped.

\(x(0)=1\) is wrong: 1 m is the spring's length. The displacement from equilibrium is \(1-0.7=0.3\).

Type E — Qualitative "show that" / structure questions tutorial level (T03 Q6, Q9)

Looks like: "If \(a,b,c\gt0\), show \(\lim_{t\to\infty}y(t)=0\)" (T03 Q6), or "what goes wrong with the polynomial trial when \(c=0\)?" (T03 Q9).

Model answers — T03 Q6 and Q9

Q6. Characteristic equation \(ar^2+br+c=0\) with \(a,b,c\gt0\). Case \(\Delta\gt0\): the roots have product \(c/a\gt0\) (same sign) and sum \(-b/a\lt0\), so both are negative and \(y=c_1e^{r_1t}+c_2e^{r_2t}\to0\). Case \(\Delta=0\): \(r=-b/2a\lt0\) and \(te^{rt}\to0\), so \(y\to0\). Case \(\Delta\lt0\): \(\alpha=-b/2a\lt0\), and \(|e^{\alpha t}(c_1\cos\beta t+c_2\sin\beta t)|\le(|c_1|+|c_2|)e^{\alpha t}\to0\). So \(y\to0\) in every case.

Q9. If \(c=0\): \(a\lambda^2+b\lambda=0\Rightarrow\lambda=0,-b/a\), so \(y_h=c_1+c_2e^{-bx/a}\). A degree-\(n\) polynomial trial fails because its constant term is a homogeneous solution: \(ay''+by'\) of a degree-\(n\) polynomial has degree only \(n-1\). So the trial must be a polynomial of degree \(n+1\) (equivalently \(x\times\) the full degree-\(n\) polynomial), which is the overlap rule with \(\lambda=0\).

§6 · Traps & marks lost

Applying the ICs to \(y_H\) alone. For a forced IVP you must apply \(y(0),y'(0)\) to the full \(y=y_H+y_P\). Fitting the constants to \(y_H\) first and then adding \(y_P\) breaks the initial conditions.
Standard form before reduction of order. The ROO formula uses \(p(x)\) from \(y''+p\,y'+q\,y=0\). In 2020 S2 Q3 the marking scheme gives a separate mark for dividing by \(2x^2\). Using the raw coefficient \(-3x\) wrecks everything after it.
Missing the overlap. Always compare your trial with \(y_H\) before differentiating. The symptom of a missed overlap is an impossible equation such as \(0=6\). If you see that, don't stop: multiply the trial by \(x\) and redo it.
Half a trig trial. A forcing of \(10\cos x\) still needs \(A\cos x+B\sin x\). The \(y'\) term turns cos into sin. (The exception is \(y''+cy=\cos\omega x\) with no \(y'\) term, where \(B\) simply comes out 0. Including it costs nothing.)
Incomplete polynomial trial. \(r=x^2\) needs \(ax^2+bx+c\), not \(ax^2\) (T03 Q4 gives \(y_P=-\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac12x-\tfrac34\), where every coefficient is nonzero). And \(r=xe^{x}\) needs \((ax+b)e^{x}\), not \(axe^{x}\) (T03 Q5c: \(y_P=(\tfrac19x+\tfrac2{27})e^{x}\)).
Repeated root written as two exponentials. \(c_1e^{4x}+c_2e^{4x}\) is one constant in disguise. Use \((c_1+c_2x)e^{4x}\).
Complex answer left in \(e^{(\alpha\pm\beta i)x}\) form, or \(\alpha,\beta\) mixed up. For \(\lambda=\tfrac12\pm i\) (2020 S1 Q7) the answer is \(e^{x/2}(A\sin x+B\cos x)\): \(\alpha=\tfrac12\) goes in the exponential and \(\beta=1\) inside the trig functions. Also watch the leading coefficient: \(\alpha=-b/(2a)\), not \(-b/2\).
Sign slips in the characteristic equation. Even the official T03 solutions contain one: for \(y''-5y'+4y=0\) they print "\(\lambda^2-6\lambda+4=0\)" and then correctly give \(\lambda=1,4\) (the correct equation is \(\lambda^2-5\lambda+4=0\)). Substitute your roots back into the equation you wrote.
Spring set-up errors. (i) \(x(0)\) is displacement from equilibrium, not the spring length. (ii) \(k\) comes from the equilibrium stretch \(s\), not from the "stretched to" length. (iii) Divide by \(m\) (or keep \(m\lambda^2\)). (iv) Up is negative when down is positive.
Forgetting the equilibrium / zero case when separating. In ROO, dividing by \(v\) assumes \(v\ne0\). The 2020 S2 marking scheme awarded a mark for noting "\(v=0\) is an equilibrium solution". Say it in one line.
Resonance sign. For \(y''+\omega^2y=F\sin\omega t\), \(y_P=-\dfrac{F}{2\omega}t\cos\omega t\) (minus sign). For \(F\cos\omega t\), \(y_P=+\dfrac{F}{2\omega}t\sin\omega t\). If unsure, derive it: substitute \(t(A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t)\).

§7 · Self-check

1. General solution of \(y''+16y=0\)?

\(\lambda^2+16=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm4i\) (\(\alpha=0,\beta=4\)): \(y=A\cos4x+B\sin4x\).

2. General solution of \(3y''+2y'=0\)?

\(3\lambda^2+2\lambda=\lambda(3\lambda+2)=0\Rightarrow\lambda=0,-\tfrac23\): \(y=A+Be^{-2x/3}\). (\(e^{0x}=1\), the constant solution.)

3. Roots \(\lambda=-2\pm5i\). Write the general solution.

\(y=e^{-2x}(A\cos5x+B\sin5x)\).

4. A general solution is \(y=(A+Bx)e^{-x}\). What was the (monic) ODE?

Double root \(-1\): \((\lambda+1)^2=\lambda^2+2\lambda+1\Rightarrow y''+2y'+y=0\).

5. What trial for \(y''-3y'+2y=5e^{x}\)?

Roots \(1,2\). \(e^{x}\) overlaps (simple root) → \(y_P=Axe^{x}\).

6. What trial for \(y''+4y=\sin2x\)? For \(y''+4y=\sin x\)?

Roots \(\pm2i\). \(\sin2x\) overlaps → \(x(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)\) (resonance). \(\sin x\) doesn't → \(A\cos x+B\sin x\).

7. What trial for \(y''-y=e^{-x}\), and what is \(y_P\)?

Roots \(\pm1\): overlap → \(Axe^{-x}\). Shortcut \(C/P'(-1)=1/(-2)\): \(y_P=-\tfrac12xe^{-x}\).

8. Particular solution of \(y''+y=3\)?

Constant trial \(A\): \(A=3\), so \(y_P=3\) (no overlap: \(\lambda=\pm i\)).

9. \(2x''+12x'+18x=0\): over, under or critical?

\(\Delta=144-4(2)(18)=0\) → critically damped.

10. A 0.5 kg mass stretches a spring 9.8 cm. Find \(k\) and \(\omega\) (\(g=9.8\)).

\(k=\dfrac{0.5(9.8)}{0.098}=50\) N/m; \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{100}=10\) rad/s.

11. In reduction of order for \(y''+py'+qy=0\) with \(y=uy_1\), which term vanishes and why?

The \(u\) term: its coefficient is \(y_1''+py_1'+qy_1=0\) because \(y_1\) is a solution. That leaves a first-order equation in \(v=u'\).

12. Why does the transient die out in a damped forced oscillator?

With \(m,\beta,k\gt0\) every characteristic root has negative real part (T03 Q6), so \(x_H\to0\). Only \(x_P\), the steady state at the driving frequency, remains.

13. You substitute \(y_P=Ce^{2x}\) and get \(0=6e^{2x}\). What happened and what do you do?

\(e^{2x}\) is in \(y_H\) (\(2\) is a root). Multiply by \(x\): \(y_P=Cxe^{2x}\), or by \(x^2\) if 2 is a double root.

8.5 · Reduction of order

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — deriving the repeated-root solution: \(y''-4y'+4y=0\), \(y_1=e^{2x}\)

Already in standard form, \(p=-4\). With \(y=u\,e^{2x}\): $$ u''+u'\Big(p+\frac{2y_1'}{y_1}\Big)=u''+u'\big(-4+4\big)=u''=0. $$ So \(u=A+Bx\), and \(y=(A+Bx)e^{2x}\). That's the repeated-root formula, derived rather than memorised.

Worked B — 2020 S2 style: \(x^2y''+2xy'-6y=0\), \(y=x^n\) with \(n\) a positive integer

Find \(n\). \(\big(n(n-1)+2n-6\big)x^n=0\Rightarrow n^2+n-6=(n+3)(n-2)=0\). \(n\) is a positive integer, so \(n=2\): \(y_1=x^2\).

Standard form: \(y''+\dfrac2xy'-\dfrac6{x^2}y=0\), \(p=\dfrac2x\).

ROO: \(u''+u'\Big(\dfrac2x+\dfrac{2(2x)}{x^2}\Big)=u''+\dfrac6xu'=0\). With \(v=u'\): \(v=0\) is an equilibrium solution; otherwise \(\ln|v|=-6\ln x+C\Rightarrow v=Ax^{-6}\). \(u=-\tfrac{A}{5}x^{-5}+B\).

$$\boxed{y=u\,x^2=\tilde Ax^{-3}+Bx^2,\quad x\gt0}$$

Check with the other root: \(n=-3\) gives \(x^{-3}\) ✓. In the exam, still show the ROO steps. The question asks for the method.

Worked A — undamped SHM from physical data

(b) \(x=0\iff2e^{-t}=e^{-4t}\iff e^{3t}=\tfrac12\iff t=-\tfrac13\ln2\lt0\). So there's no zero for \(t\gt0\) (overdamped). (c) \(x'=-2e^{-t}+4e^{-4t}=0\iff e^{3t}=2\iff t=\tfrac13\ln2\). \(x_{\max}=2\cdot2^{-1/3}-2^{-4/3}=2^{-1/3}\big(2-\tfrac12\big)=\tfrac32\cdot2^{-1/3}\) m (\(x'(0)=2\gt0\) so it's a maximum).

STRETCH · 4 marks Q5. For \(x''+\beta x'+9x=0\) (\(\beta\ge0\)), find the ranges of \(\beta\) giving each damping type. For \(\beta=2\), write the general solution and the time between successive zeros.

Solution

\(\Delta=\beta^2-36\): under \(0\le\beta\lt6\) (\(\beta=0\) is undamped SHM), critical \(\beta=6\), over \(\beta\gt6\). \(\beta=2\): \(\lambda=-1\pm\sqrt{-32}/2=-1\pm2\sqrt2\,i\). \(x=e^{-t}\big(A\cos2\sqrt2t+B\sin2\sqrt2t\big)\). Zeros are half a quasi-period apart: \(\pi/(2\sqrt2)\) s.

8.7 · Forced oscillations, steady state & resonance

📖 Learn from these

Worked A — steady-state amplitude: \(x''+4x'+5x=8\cos t\)

\(\lambda=-2\pm i\): \(x_H=e^{-2t}(A\cos t+B\sin t)\) (transient). Trial \(C\cos t+D\sin t\): \(\cos:\ -C+4D+5C=4C+4D=8\); \(\sin:\ -D-4C+5D=4D-4C=0\Rightarrow C=D=1\). $$\boxed{x=e^{-2t}(A\cos t+B\sin t)+\cos t+\sin t}$$ Steady state \(\cos t+\sin t=\sqrt2\cos(t-\pi/4)\): amplitude \(\sqrt2\), same frequency as the driving force.

Worked B — resonance IVP: \(x''+4x=4\sin2t,\ x(0)=0,\ x'(0)=0\)

\(x_H=A\cos2t+B\sin2t\). Resonance: \(x_P=t(C\cos2t+D\sin2t)\) → \(4D\cos2t-4C\sin2t=4\sin2t\Rightarrow C=-1,\ D=0\): \(x_P=-t\cos2t\). \(x=A\cos2t+B\sin2t-t\cos2t\). \(x(0)=A=0\). \(x'(0)=2B-1=0\Rightarrow B=\tfrac12\). $$\boxed{x=\tfrac12\sin2t-t\cos2t}$$ Amplitude grows like \(t\): resonance.

STANDARD · 5 marks Q1. Solve \(x''+x=3\cos2t\), \(x(0)=0\), \(x'(0)=0\). Is there resonance?

Solution

\(x_H=A\cos t+B\sin t\). Driving frequency \(2\ne1\), so there's no resonance. Trial \(C\cos2t\): \(-4C+C=-3C=3\Rightarrow C=-1\). \(x=A\cos t+B\sin t-\cos2t\). \(x(0)=A-1=0\Rightarrow A=1\); \(x'(0)=B=0\). \(x=\cos t-\cos2t\) (bounded).

STANDARD · 5 marks Q2. For \(x''+2x'+x=25\sin2t\), find the general solution and the steady-state amplitude.

Solution

\(x_H=(A+Bt)e^{-t}\). Trial \(C\cos2t+D\sin2t\): \(\cos:\ -4C+4D+C=-3C+4D=0\); \(\sin:\ -4D-4C+D=-3D-4C=25\). \(C=\tfrac43D\Rightarrow-3D-\tfrac{16}3D=25\Rightarrow D=-3,\ C=-4\). \(x=(A+Bt)e^{-t}-4\cos2t-3\sin2t\). Amplitude \(\sqrt{16+9}=5\).

STANDARD · 4 marks Q3. For \(x''+16x=8\cos\omega t\), which \(\omega\) gives resonance? For that \(\omega\), find \(x_P\).

Solution

Natural frequency \(\sqrt{16}=4\), so \(\omega=4\). \(x_P=\tfrac{8}{2\cdot4}t\sin4t=t\sin4t\).

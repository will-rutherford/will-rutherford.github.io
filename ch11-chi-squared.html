1 · Concepts in depth

The one idea behind everything

A χ² test compares the counts you observed (\(O\)) against the counts a model says you should expect (\(E\)). If the model is right, \(O\) and \(E\) are close and the discrepancies \((O-E)\) are just noise; if the model is wrong, some cells drift far from expectation. The single test statistic $$T=\sum_{\text{cells}}\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}$$ measures the total squared, relative discrepancy. Small \(T\) ⇒ good agreement; large \(T\) ⇒ evidence against the model. Everything else in the chapter is just two different ways of deciding what "expected" means.

  • Goodness-of-fit (GoF): "expected" comes from claimed proportions — a theory, a genetics ratio, a hypothesised model. Question wording: "are the data consistent with…", "does this accord with the theory", "is each category equally likely".
  • Test of independence: "expected" comes from assuming the two categorical variables are unrelated, so each cell's expected count is row total × column total ÷ n. Question wording: "is X associated with Y", "test for independence", "is there an association between…".

🧭 Intuition — why the statistic is \((O-E)^2/E\) and not just \(O-E\)

Take the formula apart piece by piece — each part fixes a real problem:

  • \(O-E\) — the miss. How far this cell landed from where the model said it should.
  • Square it — kill the cancellation. Raw misses always sum to zero across the table (overshoots exactly balance undershoots, because both O and E sum to \(n\)), so \(\sum(O-E)=0\) tells you nothing. Squaring makes every miss count, and makes big misses count disproportionately.
  • Divide by \(E\) — make it relative. Missing by 10 is shocking if you expected 15, and trivial if you expected 1500. Dividing by \(E\) converts each miss into "surprise per unit of expectation", so cells of different sizes compete fairly.

Summing over all cells gives one number: the total surprise in the table. That's all \(T\) is.

χ² tells you whether an association exists — it says nothing about how strong it is (a huge \(n\) can make a tiny association significant). For direction and substance, compare O vs E cell by cell and say which way reality deviates — that comparison is what upgrades a conclusion to full marks.

The multinomial distribution — the foundation (notes §11.1)

Throw \(n\) items into \(k\) boxes, box \(i\) chosen with probability \(p_i\) (with \(\sum p_i =1\)). The resulting counts \((X_1,\dots,X_k)\) have a multinomial distribution, written \((X_1,\dots,X_k)\sim\mathsf{Mnom}(n,p_1,\dots,p_k)\): $$\mathbb{P}(X_1=x_1,\dots,X_k=x_k)=\frac{n!}{x_1!\,x_2!\cdots x_k!}\,p_1^{x_1}p_2^{x_2}\cdots p_k^{x_k}, \qquad \sum_i x_i=n.$$ This is the binomial generalised from 2 boxes to \(k\) boxes. A contingency table of counts is just a multinomial sample whose boxes are the cells. Every χ² test in this course is a statement about a multinomial.

The mean count in box \(i\) is \(\mathbb{E}[X_i]=n p_i\). That is exactly the "expected count" \(E_i\) you build the test statistic from.

Why the statistic is χ² — and where the degrees of freedom come from

Theorem 11.1 (known parameters). If \((X_1,\dots,X_k)\sim\mathsf{Mnom}(n,p_1,\dots,p_k)\), then for large \(n\) $$\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(X_i-np_i)^2}{np_i}\ \approx\ \chi^2_{k-1}.$$ Intuition: each \((X_i-np_i)/\sqrt{np_i}\) is approximately a standard normal (CLT), and a sum of squared standard normals is χ² — that is the definition of the χ² distribution (new to you? see the Ground Floor for the full build-up from Z). You start with \(k\) boxes but the counts must sum to \(n\) — one linear constraint — so only \(k-1\) of them are free. Hence \(k-1\) degrees of freedom.

Theorem 11.2 (estimated parameters). If the model's probabilities depend on \(r\) unknown parameters that you must estimate from the same data, you "spend" \(r\) more degrees of freedom: $$\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(X_i-n\hat p_i)^2}{n\hat p_i}\ \approx\ \chi^2_{k-1-r}.$$ Every parameter estimated from the data buys the model one extra chance to fit, so the null distribution shrinks by one df per estimated parameter.

Independence testing IS a goodness-of-fit test (notes §11.3)

Take an \(r\times c\) contingency table with total \(n\). Let row variable be \(U\), column variable \(V\). "Independent" means the joint probability factorises: $$H_0:\quad p_{ij}=p_i\,q_j\quad\text{for all }i,j,$$ where \(p_i=\mathbb{P}(U=i)\) and \(q_j=\mathbb{P}(V=j)\) are the (unknown) row and column marginals. We don't know these marginals, so we estimate them from the data: $$\hat p_i=\frac{\text{row }i\text{ total}}{n},\qquad \hat q_j=\frac{\text{col }j\text{ total}}{n}.$$ The expected count in cell \((i,j)\) is therefore $$\boxed{\,E_{ij}=n\,\hat p_i\,\hat q_j=\frac{(\text{row }i\text{ total})\times(\text{col }j\text{ total})}{n}\,}\tag{11.1}$$ and the test statistic is the usual \(T=\sum_{i,j}(X_{ij}-E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}\).

Degrees of freedom. There are \(rc\) cells (so \(rc-1\) free after the total constraint), and we estimated \((r-1)\) free row proportions plus \((c-1)\) free column proportions. So $$\text{df}=(rc-1)-(r-1)-(c-1)=(r-1)(c-1).$$ Memorise the shortcut: df \(=(r-1)(c-1)\) for an \(r\times c\) independence test.

Quick way to see \((r-1)(c-1)\): once the row and column totals of the table are fixed, you can only freely choose the counts in an \((r-1)\times(c-1)\) corner — every remaining cell is then forced by the margins. df literally counts the free cells. (Try it on a 2×2: fix the margins, choose ONE cell, and watch the other three fill themselves in — df = 1.)

Two facts that decide marks

Validity rule (Remark 11.1)

The χ² approximation only holds for large \(n\). The course's rule of thumb is $$np_i\ge 5\ \text{for all }i,\qquad\text{i.e. every expected count } E\ge 5.$$ Always compute the \(E\)'s first and confirm the smallest one is \(\ge 5\). If a cell falls below 5 the approximation is doubtful (in practice you'd pool categories) — state this.

The test is always right-tailed

Only large \(T\) is evidence against \(H_0\) — a small \(T\) means "\(O\) close to \(E\)", which supports the model. So the P-value is always the upper tail \(\mathbb{P}(\chi^2_{\text{df}}\ge t)\), and you reject when \(t\ge q\), the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile. There is no such thing as a two-sided χ² test here.

2 · Key formulas & when to use them

Multinomial pmf $$\mathbb{P}(X_1=x_1,\dots,X_k=x_k)=\frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}\,p_1^{x_1}\cdots p_k^{x_k}$$
You're asked for the probability of a specific breakdown of \(n\) items across \(k\) categories (e.g. "of 7 recruits, 2 in class 1, 4 in class 2, 1 in class 3"). Often the lead-in to a χ² question or a Ch 3 joint-distribution part.
Chi-squared test statistic (both flavours) $$T=\sum_{\text{cells}}\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}=\sum_{\text{cells}}\frac{(\text{observed}-\text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}}$$
Always — this is the single statistic for every χ² test. It is on the provided formula sheet, so you only need to know how to build the \(E\)'s and read the df.
GoF, known/claimed proportions — expected counts and df $$E_i=n\,\pi_i,\qquad \text{df}=k-1$$ where \(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_k\) are the hypothesised proportions and \(k\) is the number of categories.
"Are the data consistent with proportions \(1:2:1\) / with the claimed model / with each outcome equally likely?" No parameters estimated from the data.
GoF, parameters estimated from the data $$E_i=n\,\hat\pi_i,\qquad \text{df}=k-1-r$$ where \(r\) = number of parameters estimated (e.g. fitting a Poisson mean, or a Binomial \(p\)).
"Test whether the data follow a Poisson / Binomial / etc. distribution" where the distribution's parameter is estimated first. Subtract one df per estimated parameter.
Test of independence (contingency table) $$E_{ij}=\frac{(\text{row }i\text{ total})\times(\text{col }j\text{ total})}{n},\qquad \text{df}=(r-1)(c-1)$$
The flagship exam move: "Is X associated with Y?", "Test for independence", "Is there an association between the two variables?" on an \(r\times c\) table of counts. This is the final part of the joint-table question in 2023/2024/2025.
Decision rule & P-value
  • Critical-value form: reject \(H_0\) at level \(\alpha\) if \(t\ge q\), where \(q\) is the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of \(\chi^2_{\text{df}}\) (read from the provided χ² table).
  • P-value form: \(P=\mathbb{P}(\chi^2_{\text{df}}\ge t)\) (upper tail). Reject if \(P<\alpha\). By hand you bracket \(P\) between two table columns (see §5).
Every χ² test ends here. If the question says "report the P-value", bracket it from the table; if it says "at the 5% level", compare \(t\) with the \(0.95\)-quantile \(q\).

★ FLAGSHIP — 2025 Q2 [16 marks], part (d) [5 marks]: test of independence, 2×3 table

Transcribed in full from Semester 2 Examinations 2025, Question 2.

A study of \(n=120\) people investigates the relationship between screen use before bedtime and sleep quality. Let \(X\) denote whether a person uses screens within 30 minutes of bedtime (\(0=\)No, \(1=\)Yes). Let \(Y\) denote the person's sleep quality (\(0=\)poor, \(1=\)fair, \(2=\)good). The following table shows the joint frequency counts.

Y=0 (poor)Y=1 (fair)Y=2 (good)Row total
X=0 (No)258740
X=1 (Yes)15204580
Col total402852120

Table 1: Joint frequency table of \(X\) and \(Y\). (Row and column totals are added here as a working aid; the exam paper prints only the inner \(2\times3\) block of counts.)

Define the joint probability mass function (pmf) of \(X\) and \(Y\) as \(f(x,y)=\mathbb{P}(X=x,Y=y)=n_{xy}/120\), where \(n_{xy}\) is the number of individuals with \(X=x\) and \(Y=y\).

(a) Determine the marginal pmf of \(X\) and of \(Y\). [4 marks]
(b) Determine the expectation of \(X\) and of \(Y\). [2 marks]
(c) Determine the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). [5 marks]
(d) Perform an appropriate statistical test to determine whether screen use is associated with sleep quality. Report the test statistic, the \(P\)-value, a statistical conclusion, and a conclusion in context. [5 marks]

Parts (a)–(c) are the Ch 3 marginal / expectation / correlation build-up (worked on the Ch 3 page). Part (d) — the words "associated with" on a table of counts ⇒ χ² test of independence — is solved below.

Step 1 — Hypotheses (words AND symbols).
\(H_0\): screen use and sleep quality are independent (no association), i.e. \(p_{ij}=p_i q_j\) for all \(i,j\).
\(H_1\): screen use and sleep quality are not independent (they are associated), i.e. \(p_{ij}\ne p_i q_j\) for some \(i,j\).

Step 2 — Expected counts \(E_{ij}=\dfrac{\text{row}\times\text{col}}{n}\):

E11 = 40·40/120 = 13.33 E12 = 40·28/120 = 9.33 E13 = 40·52/120 = 17.33 E21 = 80·40/120 = 26.67 E22 = 80·28/120 = 18.67 E23 = 80·52/120 = 34.67
Every \(E\ge 5\) (smallest is 9.33), so the χ² approximation is valid — say this for the mark.

Step 3 — Test statistic \(T=\sum (O-E)^2/E\), cell by cell:

(25−13.33)²/13.33 = 10.208 (8−9.33)²/9.33 = 0.190 (7−17.33)²/17.33 = 6.160 (15−26.67)²/26.67 = 5.104 (20−18.67)²/18.67 = 0.095 (45−34.67)²/34.67 = 3.080 T = 10.208+0.190+6.160+5.104+0.095+3.080 = 24.84

Step 4 — Distribution & df. Under \(H_0\), \(T\approx\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}=\chi^2_{(2-1)(3-1)}=\chi^2_2\).

Step 5 — P-value (bracket from the table). For df \(=2\), the largest tabulated quantile is the \(0.995\)-quantile \(=10.6\). Since \(t=24.84>10.6\), $$P=\mathbb{P}(\chi^2_2\ge 24.84)\lt 0.005.$$ (Exact value \(P\approx 4.0\times10^{-6}\); "\(P<0.005\)" is the correct exam answer from the table.)

Step 6 — Statistical conclusion. \(P<0.005<0.05\), so reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level.

Step 7 — Conclusion in context. There is very strong evidence that screen use before bed is associated with sleep quality. Reading the cells: non-screen users were concentrated in "poor" (25 of 40 observed vs 13.3 expected), while screen users were concentrated in "good" (45 of 80 vs 34.7 expected) — the two variables are clearly not independent in this sample.

Note the direction is the opposite of the "common sense" story — always describe the association that the data actually show, not the one you assumed.

Multinomial probability (the Ch 3 crossover) — notes Ex 11.1

Study. On enlistment, army recruits are sorted by a standardised aptitude test into one of three IQ classes: Class 1 (below average), Class 2 (average) and Class 3 (above average). Across the whole population of recruits the proportions falling in the three classes are \(p_1=0.266,\ p_2=0.468,\ p_3=0.266\). Let \((X_1,X_2,X_3)\) be the numbers of a fresh intake of \(n=7\) recruits that fall in each class, so \((X_1,X_2,X_3)\sim\mathsf{Mnom}(7;0.266,0.468,0.266)\).

(a) Find the probability that of the 7 new recruits, exactly 2 fall in Class 1, 4 in Class 2 and 1 in Class 3, i.e. \(\mathbb{P}(X_1=2,X_2=4,X_3=1)\). [3 marks]
P = 7!/(2!·4!·1!) · (0.266)² · (0.468)⁴ · (0.266)¹ = 105 · 0.070756 · 0.047976 · 0.266 = 0.0948

So about a 9.5% chance of that exact split. This is the multinomial pmf — the same object that underlies the whole chapter, and a natural link to joint distributions in Ch 3.

4 · Exam question types

Type A — Test of independence (the guaranteed one)

Looks like: the last part of a multi-part question that first built a joint / contingency table of counts. Wording: "perform an appropriate test to determine whether X is associated with Y", "is there an association between…", "test for independence".

Marks: 5–6. Seen: 2025 Q2(d) [5], 2023 Q5(e) [5], 2024 Q5(e) [6].

Recipe:
  1. State \(H_0\) (independent / not associated) and \(H_1\) (associated) — words and symbols.
  2. Row totals, column totals, grand total \(n\).
  3. \(E_{ij}=\text{row}\times\text{col}/n\) for every cell; confirm all \(E\ge 5\).
  4. \(T=\sum(O-E)^2/E\) (show each cell's contribution).
  5. df \(=(r-1)(c-1)\); state \(T\approx\chi^2_{\text{df}}\) under \(H_0\).
  6. Bracket \(P=\mathbb{P}(\chi^2_{\text{df}}\ge t)\) from the table (name both bounding quantiles).
  7. Compare with \(\alpha\); statistical conclusion; then one sentence in the story's language.

Type B — Goodness-of-fit to claimed proportions / a model

Looks like: "Are the data consistent with the proportions \(a:b:c\)?", "does this accord with the theory?", "are the categories equally likely?", "test whether the outcomes match the claimed model". Not yet on a 2023–2025 paper, but it is core notes material (Ex 11.3, exercises 1–2) and squarely examinable — treat it as live.

Marks: ~5.

Recipe:
  1. \(H_0\): \(p_i=\pi_i\) (the claimed proportions); \(H_1\): \(H_0\) false.
  2. \(E_i=n\pi_i\) for each category; confirm all \(E\ge 5\).
  3. \(T=\sum(O-E)^2/E\).
  4. df \(=k-1\) (subtract an extra \(r\) if you estimated \(r\) parameters).
  5. Bracket \(P\); conclude statistically and in context.

Type C — "What are the expected counts?" (stand-alone part)

Looks like: "If there were no association, what would the expected counts be?" or "What are the expected counts if all areas are equally attractive?" — often part (a), before the test.

Marks: 2–3. Seen: notes Ex 11.4.1(a), Ex 11.4.2(a).

Recipe: independence ⇒ \(E_{ij}=\text{row}\times\text{col}/n\); equal-preference GoF ⇒ \(E_i=n/k\); claimed-ratio GoF ⇒ \(E_i=n\pi_i\). Present them in a table and note whether all \(\ge 5\).

Type D — Multinomial probability (lead-in / Ch 3 crossover)

Looks like: "What is the probability that of \(n\) items, \(x_1\) are …, \(x_2\) are …?" Plug into the multinomial pmf.

Marks: 2–3. Seen: notes Ex 11.1; conceptually inside every joint-table question (Ch 3).

"are the categories / areas equally likely / equally preferred" GoF with \(\pi_i=1/k\), so \(E_i=n/k\); df \(=k-1\).
"test whether the data follow a Poisson / Binomial distribution" (parameter not given) GoF with estimated parameter: \(E_i=n\hat\pi_i\), df \(=k-1-r\) (\(r\) = params estimated).
"what are the expected counts (if independent / equally likely)?" Just compute \(E\): row×col/n (independence) or \(n/k\) / \(n\pi_i\) (GoF). Present in a table; check all \(\ge 5\).
"report the P-value" (χ² test, table provided) Bracket \(t\) between two adjacent quantiles of \(\chi^2_{\text{df}}\); state e.g. "\(0.025
"at the 5% significance level" / "using \(\alpha=0.05\)" Compare \(t\) with the \(0.950\)-quantile \(q\); reject \(H_0\) iff \(t\ge q\).
"state a conclusion in context" / "in the context of the study" One plain sentence naming the two variables and whether they're associated, matching the DATA (e.g. "screen users tended to report better sleep — the variables are associated"). Not just "reject \(H_0\)".
"state the hypotheses" for a χ² test \(H_0\): independent / \(p_i=\pi_i\); \(H_1\): not independent / \(H_0\) false. Give words and symbols; \(H_1\) is never a specific alternative.
"is the test valid / are assumptions met?" Check every expected count \(E\ge 5\) (Remark 11.1) and that observations are independent counts from one multinomial sample. Flag any \(E<5\) or small \(n\).
"probability that of \(n\), \(x_1\) are…, \(x_2\) are…" Multinomial pmf: \(\dfrac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}p_1^{x_1}\cdots p_k^{x_k}\).
Question ends a joint-frequency-table problem after marginals / correlation parts The final part is almost certainly this test of independence — link back to the joint pmf built in Ch 3.

6 · Traps & marks lost

Wrong degrees of freedom. Independence: df \(=(r-1)(c-1)\), NOT \(rc-1\) and NOT \(k-1\). GoF: df \(=k-1\) (minus estimated params). A 2×3 independence test is df\(=2\); a 5-category GoF is df\(=4\). The wrong df sends you to the wrong table row and blows the P-value.
Expected counts computed the wrong way. For independence use row×col/n; for claimed-ratio GoF use \(n\pi_i\); for "equally likely" use \(n/k\). Mixing these up is the single most common error — read whether the "expected" comes from the margins (independence) or a stated model (GoF).
Dividing by \(O\) instead of \(E\). The statistic is \(\sum(O-E)^2/\mathbf{E}\) — you divide each squared discrepancy by the expected count, never the observed. Easy slip under time pressure.
Forgetting the validity check. "State whether the test is valid" is a free mark: confirm every \(E\ge 5\). In 2024 Q5(e) two cells are only ~6.2–6.8 with \(n=50\) — you should note the approximation is borderline. Checking \(O\ge 5\) instead of \(E\ge 5\) is wrong.
Treating it as two-sided. The χ² test is always upper-tailed: \(P=\mathbb{P}(\chi^2_{\text{df}}\ge t)\). Do not halve or double anything; a small \(t\) means good agreement (large P), not a "significant" left tail.
P-value bracket too loose. When \(t\) lands between two quantile columns, name both bounds, e.g. "\(5.99<6.66<7.38\Rightarrow 0.025
Rounding the \(E\)'s too early. Keep the expected counts (which are often non-integers like 13.33, 34.67) to full precision inside \((O-E)^2/E\); rounding to whole numbers first can shift \(T\) enough to change the P-value bracket. Then report \(T\) and \(P\) to ≥3 sig figs.
Naming a directional \(H_1\). For χ² the alternative is only "not independent" / "\(H_0\) false" — never "screen use worsens sleep" or a one-directional claim. The test detects any departure, not a specific direction.
Stopping at "reject \(H_0\)". The mark scheme wants a conclusion in context: name the variables and the association in the study's own language, consistent with what the data actually show.

1. A 4×3 contingency table. What degrees of freedom does the independence test use?

df \(=(r-1)(c-1)=(4-1)(3-1)=6\).

df \(=k-1=6-1=5\). (Only subtract more if you estimated parameters from the data.)

5. Why divide by \(E\) and not \(O\), and why is only the upper tail used?

Dividing by \(E\) standardises each discrepancy (each \((X_i-np_i)/\sqrt{np_i}\) is approximately standard normal, so its square is approximately \(\chi^2_1\)). Only large discrepancies — large \(T\) — are evidence against \(H_0\); small \(T\) means \(O\approx E\), which supports \(H_0\). Hence the P-value is the upper tail \(\mathbb{P}(\chi^2_{\text{df}}\ge t)\).

6. An expected count comes out as 3.1. What should you say?

The rule of thumb \(E\ge 5\) (Remark 11.1) is violated, so the χ² approximation is unreliable for this table. In practice you would pool that category with a neighbour; in the exam, state that the validity condition fails and interpret results with caution.

7. Genetics claims phenotypes in ratio 9:3:3:1. Out of n=160, what are the expected counts, and what df?

Proportions \(=\tfrac{9}{16},\tfrac{3}{16},\tfrac{3}{16},\tfrac{1}{16}\). Expected \(=160\times\) each \(=90,\,30,\,30,\,10\) (all \(\ge 5\) ✓). df \(=k-1=4-1=3\).

8. State \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) for testing whether diet type is associated with disease status (2×2 table).

\(H_0\): diet type and disease status are independent (\(p_{ij}=p_iq_j\) for all \(i,j\)); \(H_1\): they are associated (\(p_{ij}\ne p_iq_j\) for some \(i,j\)). df \(=(2-1)(2-1)=1\).

Exam-day pipeline (memorise the order): recognise flavour (associated → independence; consistent with model → GoF) → build \(E\) → check all \(E\ge 5\) → \(T=\sum(O-E)^2/E\) → df → bracket upper-tail \(P\) from the table → compare with \(\alpha\) → conclusion in context. The formula and the χ² table are handed to you — marks are for recognition, correct \(E\)/df, and a contextual conclusion.

Worked D1 — three outcomes

Setup. A weighted spinner lands on Red, Green or Blue with probabilities \(0.5, 0.3, 0.2\) respectively. It is spun \(n=10\) times independently.

(a) Find the probability of getting exactly 4 Reds, 3 Greens and 3 Blues, i.e. \(\mathbb{P}(4\text{ Red},3\text{ Green},3\text{ Blue})\). [2 marks]
P = 10!/(4!·3!·3!) · (0.5)⁴ · (0.3)³ · (0.2)³ = 4200 · 0.0625 · 0.027 · 0.008 = 0.0567

So about a \(5.7\%\) chance of that exact breakdown.

Compute the coefficient \(10!/(4!\,3!\,3!)=4200\) first, then multiply by each probability power. Keep full precision and give ≥3 sig figs.
Worked D2 — four outcomes, and the link to expected counts

Setup. Website visitors independently pick one of four subscription plans with probabilities \(0.40, 0.25, 0.25, 0.10\) respectively. Consider the next \(n=10\) visitors.

(a) Find the probability that the four plans are chosen 3, 2, 4 and 1 times respectively, i.e. \(\mathbb{P}(3,2,4,1)\). [3 marks]
P = 10!/(3!·2!·4!·1!) · (0.4)³ · (0.25)² · (0.25)⁴ · (0.1)¹ = 12600 · 0.064 · 0.0625 · 0.00390625 · 0.1 = 0.0197

About \(2.0\%\). Link: the mean count for plan \(i\) is \(\mathbb{E}[X_i]=np_i\) — here \((4, 2.5, 2.5, 1)\). Those are exactly the "expected counts" a GoF test would compare against, so the multinomial pmf and the χ² machinery describe the same object.

Worked E1 — bracketing a significant statistic (df 3)

Setup. A χ² test of independence on a \(2\times4\) contingency table of counts yields the test statistic \(T=8.10\). (The table is not reproduced — this drill isolates the final bracketing-and-decision step, which is where P-value marks are typically won or lost.)

(a) Bracket the \(P\)-value using the provided χ² table and give the decision at the 5% significance level. [2 marks]

df \(=(2-1)(4-1)=3\). From the df-3 row: the \(0.950\)-quantile is \(7.81\) and the \(0.975\)-quantile is \(9.35\). Since \(7.81\lt 8.10\lt 9.35\), $$0.025\lt P\lt 0.05.$$ (Exact \(P\approx 0.044\).) As \(P\lt 0.05\), reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level.

Name both bounding quantiles (\(7.81\) and \(9.35\)) — that is what earns the tight bracket \(0.025\lt P\lt 0.05\) rather than a loose "\(P\lt 0.05\)".
Worked E2 — a non-significant statistic + a full contextual conclusion (df 5)

Setup. A goodness-of-fit test comparing observed counts over \(k=6\) categories against a claimed model yields \(T=4.20\), on df \(=k-1=5\). (Counts omitted — this drill isolates the bracketing and contextual-conclusion step.)

(a) Bracket the \(P\)-value from the provided χ² table, give the 5%-level decision, and write a one-sentence conclusion in context. [3 marks]

From the df-5 row: the \(0.250\)-quantile is \(2.67\) and the \(0.750\)-quantile is \(6.63\). Since \(2.67\lt 4.20\lt 6.63\), $$0.25\lt P\lt 0.75.$$ (Exact \(P\approx 0.52\).) \(P\) is large, so do not reject \(H_0\).

Contextual conclusion (template). "There is no evidence that the observed category counts depart from the claimed model; the data are consistent with it." When you do reject, upgrade this to name the direction — which categories were over- or under-represented — because χ² alone never tells you strength or direction; only the O-vs-E comparison does.

E-Q2 [STANDARD · 3 marks]. A χ² test on a 3×2 table (df 2) gives \(T=5.50\). Bracket \(P\) and state the decision at the 5% level. Watch the boundary.

Show solution

df 2: \(0.900\)-quantile \(4.61\), \(0.950\)-quantile \(5.99\). Since \(4.61\lt 5.50\lt 5.99\), \(0.05\lt P\lt 0.10\) (exact \(\approx 0.064\)). Because \(P\gt 0.05\), do not reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level — the result is suggestive but not significant.

\(T=5.50\) is just below the \(0.950\)-quantile \(5.99\), so it fails at 5%. Reading the wrong column (or rounding \(T\) up) is exactly how a "reject" is claimed by mistake here.

Using the arena. Do it in two passes. Pass 1 — recognition: read each prompt and, before any arithmetic, say out loud "independence (df \((r-1)(c-1)\))" or "GoF (df \(k-1\))" and write the hypotheses — that split is where most marks are won or lost. Pass 2 — execution: build \(E\), check every \(E\ge 5\), sum \((O-E)^2/E\), bracket the upper-tail P from the §2 table naming both quantiles, then finish with a sentence in the story's own words that names the direction of the association. Re-attempt any question you needed the solution for a day later — fluency on this pipeline is worth a guaranteed 5–6 marks.

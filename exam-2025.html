Question 1 — Continuous distribution [10 marks]

Let \(X\) have pdf \(f(x)=c\,x^{a}\) for \(0\le x\le 5\), where \(c>0,\ a>0\), and \(\mathbb P\!\left(X\lt \tfrac54\right)=\tfrac1{32}\).
5 marks(a) Determine the values of \(a\) and \(c\).
Worked solution
A pdf must integrate to 1; a probability statement gives a second equation. Two unknowns \((a,c)\) → two equations. The clean trick is to divide the two integrals so \(c\) cancels and \(a\) drops out alone.
Equation 1 (normalisation): $$\int_0^5 c\,x^{a}\,dx = \frac{c\,5^{a+1}}{a+1}=1.$$ Equation 2 (given probability): $$\mathbb P\!\left(X\lt\tfrac54\right)=\int_0^{5/4} c\,x^a\,dx=\frac{c\,(5/4)^{a+1}}{a+1}=\frac1{32}.$$ Divide Eq 2 by Eq 1 — the \(c/(a+1)\) cancels: $$\left(\frac{5/4}{5}\right)^{a+1}=\left(\frac14\right)^{a+1}=\frac1{32}\;\Rightarrow\;4^{a+1}=32\;\Rightarrow\;2^{2(a+1)}=2^5.$$ So \(2(a+1)=5\Rightarrow a+1=\tfrac52\Rightarrow \boxed{a=\tfrac32=1.5}.\)
Back-substitute into Eq 1: \(c=\dfrac{a+1}{5^{a+1}}=\dfrac{2.5}{5^{2.5}}=\dfrac{\sqrt5}{50}.\)
\(a = 1.50,\qquad c = \dfrac{\sqrt5}{50}=0.0447\) (3 s.f.).
Because \(c/(a+1)=1/5^{a+1}\), the cdf collapses to the tidy form \(F(x)=\left(\dfrac{x}{5}\right)^{2.5}\) on \([0,5]\). Keep this — it makes parts (b) and (c) one-liners.
Don't try to solve the two equations numerically on the FX82 blindly. Dividing to eliminate \(c\) turns it into a clean index law \(4^{a+1}=32\); students who don't divide burn time and often mis-handle the fractional power.
2 marks(b) Determine \(\mathbb P(X\gt 4)\).
Worked solution
Right-tail probability of a continuous RV → use the cdf: \(\mathbb P(X\gt4)=1-F(4)\).
Using \(F(x)=(x/5)^{2.5}\): $$\mathbb P(X\gt4)=1-F(4)=1-\left(\tfrac45\right)^{2.5}=1-(0.8)^{2.5}=1-0.57243.$$
\(\mathbb P(X\gt4)=0.4276\) (4 s.f.) \(\approx 0.428.\)
\((0.8)^{2.5}\) on the FX82: enter 0.8 ^ 2.5. Do not round \(2.5\) to \(2\) or \(3\) — the fractional exponent is the whole point.
3 marks(c) Determine the median and the mode of \(X\).
Worked solution
Median \(m\): solve \(F(m)=0.5\). Mode: maximise \(f(x)\) on the support — check monotonicity / endpoints, since \(f\) may have no interior turning point.
Median: $$\left(\frac m5\right)^{2.5}=0.5\;\Rightarrow\;\frac m5=0.5^{1/2.5}=0.5^{0.4}=0.75807\;\Rightarrow\;m=5(0.75807).$$
Median \(m = 3.79\) (3 s.f.).
Mode: \(f(x)=c\,x^{1.5}\) with \(c>0\). Its derivative \(f'(x)=1.5\,c\,x^{0.5}\gt0\) for all \(x\in(0,5]\), so \(f\) is strictly increasing — no interior maximum. The maximum is at the right endpoint.
Mode \(=5\).
Don't set \(f'(x)=0\) and "solve" — that gives \(x=0\), which is the minimum. When a density is monotone, the mode sits at an endpoint. State the increasing-derivative argument to earn the mark.

Question 2 — Joint pmf & test of association [16 marks]

\(n=120\) people. \(X\): uses screens within 30 min of bedtime (0=No, 1=Yes). \(Y\): sleep quality (0=poor, 1=fair, 2=good). Joint frequency counts \(n_{xy}\), with \(f(x,y)=n_{xy}/120\):
Y=0 (poor)Y=1 (fair)Y=2 (good)Row total
X=0 (No)258740
X=1 (Yes)15204580
Col total402852120
4 marks(a) Determine the marginal pmf of \(X\) and of \(Y\).
Worked solution
Marginal pmf = sum the joint over the other variable = row totals / \(n\) (for \(X\)) and column totals / \(n\) (for \(Y\)).
$$f_X(0)=\tfrac{40}{120}=\tfrac13,\qquad f_X(1)=\tfrac{80}{120}=\tfrac23.$$ $$f_Y(0)=\tfrac{40}{120}=\tfrac13,\quad f_Y(1)=\tfrac{28}{120}=\tfrac{7}{30},\quad f_Y(2)=\tfrac{52}{120}=\tfrac{13}{30}.$$
\(f_X=(0.333,\,0.667)\); \(\;f_Y=(0.333,\,0.233,\,0.433)\).
Sanity check: each marginal must sum to 1. \(\tfrac13+\tfrac{7}{30}+\tfrac{13}{30}=\tfrac{10+7+13}{30}=1\). ✓
2 marks(b) Determine \(\mathbb E X\) and \(\mathbb E Y\).
Worked solution
\(\mathbb E X=\sum x\,f_X(x)\) using the marginals from (a).
$$\mathbb E X = 0\cdot\tfrac13+1\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac23=0.667.$$ $$\mathbb E Y = 0\cdot\tfrac{40}{120}+1\cdot\tfrac{28}{120}+2\cdot\tfrac{52}{120}=\tfrac{28+104}{120}=\tfrac{132}{120}=1.10.$$
\(\mathbb E X = 0.667,\qquad \mathbb E Y = 1.10.\)
5 marks(c) Determine the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\).
Worked solution
Correlation \(\rho=\dfrac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}X\,\operatorname{Var}Y}}\). Build \(\mathbb E[XY]\) from the joint table, then \(\operatorname{Cov}=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E X\,\mathbb E Y\), and each variance via \(\mathbb E[\cdot^2]-(\mathbb E\cdot)^2\).
\(\mathbb E[XY]\): only \(X=1\) rows contribute (the \(X=0\) row gives \(x=0\)): $$\mathbb E[XY]=1\!\cdot\!1\!\cdot\!\tfrac{20}{120}+1\!\cdot\!2\!\cdot\!\tfrac{45}{120}=\tfrac{20+90}{120}=\tfrac{110}{120}=0.91667.$$ Covariance: $$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0.91667-(0.66667)(1.10)=0.91667-0.73333=0.18333.$$ Variances (\(X\) is Bernoulli\((\tfrac23)\)): $$\operatorname{Var}X=\tfrac23-\left(\tfrac23\right)^2=\tfrac29=0.22222.$$ $$\mathbb E[Y^2]=0+1\cdot\tfrac{28}{120}+4\cdot\tfrac{52}{120}=\tfrac{236}{120}=1.96667,\quad \operatorname{Var}Y=1.96667-1.10^2=0.75667.$$ Correlation: $$\rho=\frac{0.18333}{\sqrt{0.22222\times0.75667}}=\frac{0.18333}{0.41006}.$$
\(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0.183,\qquad \rho(X,Y)=0.447\) (3 s.f.).
There is a moderate positive correlation (\(\rho\approx0.45\)) between screen use and sleep-quality score: people who use screens before bed tend to have higher \(Y\) values (more of them fall in the "good" category) in this sample.
Correlation \(\ne\) causation, and \(Y\) is only an ordinal score coded 0/1/2 — the numeric \(\rho\) depends on that coding. That is why part (d) uses a \(\chi^2\) test of association, which does not assume the codes are equally spaced.
5 marks(d) Perform an appropriate test of whether screen use is associated with sleep quality. Report the test statistic, \(P\)-value, statistical conclusion, and a conclusion in context.
Worked solution
"Is categorical variable A associated with categorical variable B?" on a contingency table → \(\chi^2\) test of independence (formula sheet #77).
Hypotheses: \(H_0\): screen use and sleep quality are independent; \(H_1\): they are associated (not independent).
Expected counts \(=\dfrac{(\text{row total})(\text{col total})}{120}\):
Y=0Y=1Y=2
X=013.339.3317.33
X=126.6718.6734.67
Test statistic \(\chi^2=\sum\dfrac{(O-E)^2}{E}\):
(25−13.33)²/13.33 = 10.208 ( 8− 9.33)²/ 9.33 = 0.190 ( 7−17.33)²/17.33 = 6.160 (15−26.67)²/26.67 = 5.104 (20−18.67)²/18.67 = 0.095 (45−34.67)²/34.67 = 3.080 χ² = 24.84
Degrees of freedom: \((\text{rows}-1)(\text{cols}-1)=(2-1)(3-1)=2.\)
\(P\)-value from the \(\chi^2_2\) table (p.13): the largest tabulated quantile at df\(=2\) is \(10.6\) (the 0.995 quantile). Since \(24.84\gt10.6\), we read \(P\lt0.005\). (Exact value \(P=4.0\times10^{-6}\).)
\(\chi^2_{\text{obs}}=24.8\) on \(2\) df, \(P\lt0.005\) (exact \(4.0\times10^{-6}\)). Reject \(H_0\).
There is very strong evidence (\(P\lt0.005\)) that screen use within 30 minutes of bedtime is associated with sleep quality: the observed counts differ markedly from what independence predicts — screen users are far more concentrated in the "good" (and away from "poor") sleep category than independence would give.
Check the expected-count rule: all \(E\ge5\) here, so the \(\chi^2\) approximation is valid — state this. And use observed vs expected, never observed vs observed. Don't apply a continuity correction (not taught / not on the formula sheet for this test).

Question 3 — Two-sample diet trial [19 marks]

Gardner et al. (2018): 609 adults randomised to Low-Carb (LC, \(n=304\)) or Low-Fat (LF, \(n=305\)). Weight (kg) at baseline and 12 months; summary of the weight change:
Diet\(n\)Baseline mean (SD)12-mo mean (SD)Weight change mean (SD)
Low-Carb30496.30 (15.70)90.31 (16.72)−5.99 (5.69)
Low-Fat30597.50 (14.70)92.21 (15.59)−5.29 (5.70)
4 marks(a) Calculate a 95% CI for the LC population mean weight change (kg), to 2 dp, and interpret.
Worked solution
One sample, mean of a quantitative variable, \(\sigma\) unknown → one-sample \(t\) interval \(\bar X\pm q\,\dfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\) (formula sheet #62), \(q=t_{n-1,\,0.975}\).
Use only the LC weight-change column: \(\bar X=-5.99,\ S=5.69,\ n=304.\)
\(q=t_{303,\,0.975}\). df\(=303\) is off the table; the nearest tabulated rows are df\(=200\ (q=1.97)\) and \(\infty\ (q=1.96)\) — take \(q\approx1.97\). $$\text{SE}=\frac{5.69}{\sqrt{304}}=\frac{5.69}{17.436}=0.32635,\qquad \text{margin}=1.97\times0.32635=0.6429.$$ $$-5.99\pm0.64=(-6.63,\ -5.35).$$
95% CI \(=(-6.63,\ -5.35)\) kg.
We are 95% confident the true mean 12-month weight change on the low-carb diet lies between \(-6.63\) and \(-5.35\) kg. The whole interval is negative, so low-carb participants lost weight on average — a genuine (not chance) reduction.
With \(q=1.96\) you get the same interval to 2 dp \((-6.63,-5.35)\), so either \(t\)-row is safe here. Large \(n\) makes \(t\) and \(z\) essentially identical.
4 marks(b) The aim is whether one diet produced a different change than the other. State the hypotheses in mathematical form; explain all notation.
Worked solution
"Different" (either direction) → two-sided test comparing two population means.
Let \(\mu_{LC}\) = population mean 12-month weight change on the low-carb diet, and \(\mu_{LF}\) = population mean 12-month weight change on the low-fat diet (both in kg). $$H_0:\ \mu_{LC}=\mu_{LF}\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\ \mu_{LC}\ne\mu_{LF}.$$
Equivalently \(H_0:\mu_{LC}-\mu_{LF}=0\) vs \(H_1:\mu_{LC}-\mu_{LF}\ne0\).
These are population parameters (\(\mu\)), never the sample means \(\bar X\). Hypotheses are always about the population. "Different" ⇒ \(\ne\) (two-sided); do not write a one-sided \(\lt\) unless the question says "less" / "greater".
7 marks(c) Perform an appropriate hypothesis test for the mean difference in weight change across the two diets. Report the \(t\)-statistic to 2 s.f. and conclude in context.
Worked solution
Two independent groups, quantitative outcome, compare means. The two SDs are almost identical (5.69 vs 5.70) → use the pooled two-sample \(t\)-test (formula sheet #74, equal variances).
Pooled variance (#64): $$S_p^2=\frac{(m-1)S_X^2+(n-1)S_Y^2}{m+n-2}=\frac{303(5.69^2)+304(5.70^2)}{607}=32.433,\quad S_p=5.6950.$$ Test statistic under \(H_0:\mu_{LC}=\mu_{LF}\): $$T=\frac{\bar X-\bar Y}{S_p\sqrt{\tfrac1m+\tfrac1n}}=\frac{-5.99-(-5.29)}{5.6950\sqrt{\tfrac1{304}+\tfrac1{305}}}=\frac{-0.70}{5.6950(0.081044)}=\frac{-0.70}{0.46156}.$$ $$T=-1.5166\approx-1.5\quad\text{on }m+n-2=607\text{ df}.$$ \(P\)-value: two-sided with huge df, so use the \(t_\infty\) (Normal) row: \(1.28\ (0.90)\lt1.52\lt1.64\ (0.95)\), so each tail is between 0.05 and 0.10 ⇒ two-sided \(0.10\lt P\lt0.20\). (Exact \(P=0.130\).) Since \(P\gt0.05\), do not reject \(H_0\).
\(T=-1.5\) (2 s.f.), df \(=607\), \(P\approx0.13\;(\gt0.05)\). Fail to reject \(H_0\).
There is no significant difference in mean 12-month weight change between the low-carb and low-fat diets (\(t=-1.5,\ P\approx0.13\)). Although low-carb lost \(0.70\) kg more on average, that gap is well within sampling variation — the trial does not show one diet as superior for weight loss.
Welch (unequal-variance) gives \(T=-1.52\), df \(\approx607\), \(P=0.130\) — identical conclusion. With SDs this close, pooled vs Welch makes no practical difference; state your choice and why.
The outcome is the weight change column — don't test the baseline or 12-month weights. And the two groups are independent samples (different people), so this is a two-sample test, not a paired test.
4 marks(d) State the assumptions for the test in (c) and comment on their validity using Figure 1 / Table 1. If two-sample, state pooled vs Welch and justify.
Worked solution
Figure 1 is a 2×3 grid of histograms (rows = LC, LF; columns = baseline, 12-month, 12-month change). The "12-month change" histograms are roughly symmetric, single-peaked and bell-shaped, centred just below 0, with no severe skew or extreme outliers.
Assumptions:
  • Independence — within and between groups. Valid: participants were randomised and are different individuals in each diet arm.
  • Normality of the weight-change in each population (or large \(n\) via CLT). Valid: the change histograms in Figure 1 are approximately symmetric/bell-shaped, and with \(n\approx300\) per group the CLT makes \(\bar X\) approximately normal regardless.
  • Equal variances (needed for the pooled version). Valid: sample SDs are \(5.69\) vs \(5.70\) — essentially equal — so pooling is justified.
Assumptions (independence, approx. normality, equal variance) all appear satisfied → the pooled two-sample \(t\)-test is appropriate.
Rule of thumb: if the larger SD is less than about twice the smaller, treat variances as equal (pooled). Here \(5.70/5.69\approx1.00\) — pooled is clearly fine.

4 marks(c) Is there evidence of a significant interaction? State the hypotheses (with notation), the null distribution of the \(F\)-statistic, its observed value, and the \(P\)-value.

Two-way ANOVA interaction test → \(F=MS_{\text{inter}}/MS_{res}\), compared to \(F_{(a-1)(b-1),\,df_{res}}\).

Observed \(F=1.23\).
\(P\)-value: from the \(F\)-table at \(\gamma=0.95\), \(F_{2,\,54}\approx3.2\) (between \(F_{2,40}=3.23\) and \(F_{2,60}=3.15\)). Since \(1.23\ll3.2\), \(P\gg0.05\) (exact \(P=0.300\)).
\(F=1.23\sim F_{2,54}\) under \(H_0\); \(P\approx0.30\ (\gt0.05)\). Do not reject \(H_0\).
There is no significant interaction between mouth-rinse and flossing frequency (\(F=1.23,\ P\approx0.30\)). Despite the non-parallel look of Figure 2, the effect of flossing does not depend significantly on which rinse is used — so the two factors can be interpreted through their main effects.
The interaction \(F\) uses df \((2,54)\) — the numerator df is the interaction df (2), not a main-effect df. Reading the wrong df pair off the \(F\)-table is the classic lost mark here.

3 marks(e) Multiple comparisons across mouth-rinse. From the pairwise output, which treatments differ at the 5% level? Interpret in context.

Pairwise \(t\)-tests with pooled SD, Bonferroni-adjusted \(P\)-values:
Chlorhexidine vs Lemongrass: \(P=1.000\); Prophylaxis vs Lemongrass: \(P=0.036\); Prophylaxis vs Chlorhexidine: \(P=0.016\).
Post-hoc pairwise comparison: compare each adjusted \(P\) to \(\alpha=0.05\). \(P\lt0.05\) ⇒ that pair differs significantly.
  • Lemongrass vs Chlorhexidine: \(P=1.000\gt0.05\) → not significantly different.
  • Prophylaxis vs Lemongrass: \(P=0.036\lt0.05\) → significantly different.
  • Prophylaxis vs Chlorhexidine: \(P=0.016\lt0.05\) → significantly different.
Prophylaxis differs significantly from both Lemongrass and Chlorhexidine; Lemongrass and Chlorhexidine do not differ.
The Prophylaxis (control) group has a significantly different mean Plaque Index from both active rinses, while Lemongrass and Chlorhexidine give statistically indistinguishable plaque levels. In context: the two active mouth-rinses lower plaque to a similar degree, and both differ from the control — but there is no detectable advantage of Chlorhexidine over Lemongrass.

Question 5 — Simple linear regression (Old Faithful) [18 marks]

Old Faithful geyser: predict waiting time (min) from eruption duration (min). R output:
Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 33.4744 1.1549 28.98 <2e-16 *** Duration 10.7296 0.3148 34.09 <2e-16 *** Residual standard error: 5.914 on 270 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.8115, Adjusted R-squared: 0.8108 F-statistic: 1162 on 1 and 270 DF, p-value: < 2.2e-16
2 marks(a) Write the fitted regression equation (coefficients to 2 dp) and define all notation.
Worked solution
Read \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) straight from the "Estimate" column.
$$\widehat{\text{Waiting}}=33.47+10.73\times\text{Duration}.$$ where \(\widehat{\text{Waiting}}\) is the predicted mean waiting time (minutes) to the next eruption, and Duration is the eruption duration (minutes). \(\hat\beta_0=33.47\) min is the intercept and \(\hat\beta_1=10.73\) min/min is the slope.

Interpret the slope in words for full marks: each extra minute of eruption duration is associated with about \(10.73\) extra minutes of waiting time.
2 marks(b) A waiting time of 76 min was observed with duration 4.8 min. Find the residual.
Worked solution
Residual \(=y-\hat y\) (observed − fitted). Predict at \(x=4.8\), then subtract from 76.
$$\hat y=33.4744+10.7296(4.8)=33.4744+51.5021=84.9765.$$ $$e=y-\hat y=76-84.9765=-8.9765.$$

The observed waiting time was about 8.98 minutes shorter than the model predicts for a 4.8-minute eruption (the model over-predicts this point).

5 marks(c) Construct a 95% CI for the slope; interpret and comment on significance of the linear relationship.
Worked solution
CI for a regression slope: \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{n-2,\,0.975}\times\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\). Then "significant linear relationship" ⇔ the CI excludes 0.
\(n-2=270\) df; \(t_{270,0.975}\approx1.97\) (df\(=200\) row, \(q=1.97\)); \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=0.3148\). $$10.7296\pm1.97(0.3148)=10.7296\pm0.6202=(10.11,\ 11.35).$$
95% CI for slope \(=(10.11,\ 11.35)\) min per min.
We are 95% confident that each additional minute of eruption duration is associated with between \(10.11\) and \(11.35\) more minutes of waiting time. The interval lies entirely above 0, so there is strong statistical evidence of a significant positive linear relationship (consistent with the tiny \(P\lt2\times10^{-16}\) for Duration).
Two equivalent ways to argue significance: (i) CI excludes 0, or (ii) the \(t\)-value \(=34.09\) with \(P\lt2\times10^{-16}\). Quote one and you're done.
5 marks(d) State the model assumptions and, using Figure 5, briefly say whether each appears valid.

4 marks(e) For a fixed \(x_0\), without formulas: how does the width of (a) the 95% confidence interval for the mean at \(x_0\), and (b) the 95% prediction interval for a new \(Y\) at \(x_0\), behave as \(n\to\infty\)?
Worked solution
Conceptual: a CI for the mean estimates a fixed line (only estimation error), while a PI must also swallow one new observation's random scatter \(\sigma\), which never disappears.
(a) Confidence interval for the mean response: its width comes only from uncertainty in the estimated line. As \(n\) grows, the coefficients are pinned down ever more precisely, so the width shrinks toward 0. (b) Prediction interval for a new \(Y\): its width combines the same estimation error plus the irreducible scatter \(\sigma\) of an individual point about the line. Estimation error vanishes, but \(\sigma\) does not, so the width shrinks toward a positive constant (roughly \(\pm1.96\,\sigma\)), never to 0.
(a) CI width \(\to0\). (b) PI width \(\to\) a positive constant (\(\approx\pm1.96\sigma\)), not 0.
The classic error is saying both go to 0. A prediction interval is fundamentally wider than a confidence interval and stays wide because you can never predict a single random outcome exactly, no matter how much data you have.

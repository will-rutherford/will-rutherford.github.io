Question 1 — Joint pmf of two indicators [12 marks]

Two storage tanks. \(A,B\in\{0,1\}\): leak detected (1) or not (0). Joint pmf, with \(0\le p\le1\):
A \\ BB = 0B = 1
A = 0\(\tfrac14 p^2\)\(p\)
A = 1\(p\)\(p^2\)
3 marks(a) Find the value of \(p\).
Worked solution
A valid pmf must sum to 1. Add all four cells, set \(=1\), solve the quadratic, and keep the root in \([0,1]\).
$$\tfrac14 p^2 + p + p + p^2 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \tfrac54 p^2 + 2p - 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 5p^2 + 8p - 4 = 0.$$ Quadratic formula: \(p=\dfrac{-8\pm\sqrt{64+80}}{10}=\dfrac{-8\pm12}{10}\Rightarrow p=0.4\ \text{or}\ p=-2.\) Since \(0\le p\le1\), reject \(p=-2\).
\(p = 0.4\).
Verify: \(\tfrac14(0.16)+0.4+0.4+0.16=0.04+0.4+0.4+0.16=1.00\). ✓ Always plug the root back in.
Two roots appear — you must discard the one outside \([0,1]\) and say why (a probability can't be negative). Dropping the invalid-root justification loses a mark.
2 marks(b) What is the marginal pmf of \(A\)?
Worked solution
Marginal of \(A\) = sum each row over \(B\).
$$\mathbb P(A=0)=\tfrac14 p^2+p=0.04+0.4=0.44,\qquad \mathbb P(A=1)=p+p^2=0.4+0.16=0.56.$$
\(f_A(0)=0.44,\quad f_A(1)=0.56.\) (So \(A\sim\text{Ber}(0.56)\).)
Recognising \(A\sim\text{Ber}(0.56)\) makes part (c) instant: mean \(=0.56\), variance \(=0.56\times0.44\).
3 marks(c) Determine the expected value and variance of \(A\).
Worked solution
\(A\) is Bernoulli: \(\mathbb E A = \mathbb P(A=1)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}A = \mathbb P(A=1)\,\mathbb P(A=0)\).
$$\mathbb E A = 0(0.44)+1(0.56)=0.56.$$ $$\operatorname{Var}A = \mathbb E[A^2]-(\mathbb E A)^2 = 0.56 - 0.56^2 = 0.56(1-0.56)=0.56\times0.44=0.2464.$$
\(\mathbb E A = 0.560,\qquad \operatorname{Var}A = 0.246\) (3 s.f.).
For a 0/1 variable \(\mathbb E[A^2]=\mathbb E A\) (since \(0^2=0,1^2=1\)) — don't compute \(0.56^2\) for \(\mathbb E[A^2]\).
4 marks(d) Determine the covariance and correlation between \(A\) and \(B\).
Worked solution
\(\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=\mathbb E[AB]-\mathbb E A\,\mathbb E B\); \(\rho=\operatorname{Cov}/(\sigma_A\sigma_B)\). Get the \(B\) marginal first (identical structure here).
Marginal of \(B\) (sum columns): \(\mathbb P(B=0)=\tfrac14p^2+p=0.44,\ \mathbb P(B=1)=p+p^2=0.56.\) So \(\mathbb E B=0.56,\ \operatorname{Var}B=0.2464\) (same as \(A\)).
\(\mathbb E[AB]\): only the \((A{=}1,B{=}1)\) cell has \(ab\ne0\): \(\mathbb E[AB]=1\cdot1\cdot p^2=0.16.\)
Covariance: $$\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=0.16-(0.56)(0.56)=0.16-0.3136=-0.1536.$$ Correlation (here \(\sigma_A\sigma_B=\operatorname{Var}A=0.2464\)): $$\rho=\frac{-0.1536}{\sqrt{0.2464\times0.2464}}=\frac{-0.1536}{0.2464}=-0.6234.$$
\(\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=-0.154,\qquad \rho(A,B)=-0.623\) (3 s.f.).
Leakage in the two tanks is moderately negatively correlated (\(\rho\approx-0.62\)): a detected leak in one tank tends to coincide with no detected leak in the other. The negative covariance also confirms \(A\) and \(B\) are not independent.
Because \(\operatorname{Var}A=\operatorname{Var}B\), the denominator is just \(0.2464\), not its square root of a product of two different numbers — but write \(\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}A\,\operatorname{Var}B}\) explicitly so the method is visible.

Question 2 — Estimator: unbiasedness & variance [12 marks]

\(X_1,\dots,X_n\) iid with cdf \(F(x;\theta)=x^{1/\theta}\) for \(x\in(0,1)\), \(\theta>0\). Estimator \(\displaystyle\hat\theta=-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log X_i.\)
Given identities (for \(a>-1\)): \(\displaystyle\int_0^1(\log x)\,x^a\,dx=-\frac1{(a+1)^2}\), \(\displaystyle\int_0^1(\log x)^2 x^a\,dx=\frac2{(a+1)^3}.\)
2 marks(a) Find the pdf of \(X\).
Worked solution
pdf = derivative of the cdf: \(f(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}F(x;\theta)\).
$$f(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\,x^{1/\theta}=\frac1\theta\,x^{\,1/\theta-1},\qquad 0\lt x\lt1.$$
\(f(x)=\dfrac1\theta\,x^{1/\theta-1}\) on \((0,1)\), and \(0\) otherwise.
This is a Beta\((1/\theta,1)\) / power-function density. Note the exponent is \(a=1/\theta-1\); you'll feed exactly this \(a\) into the given integral identities in (b)–(c).
4 marks(b) Show that \(\hat\theta\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\theta\).
Worked solution
Unbiased ⇔ \(\mathbb E[\hat\theta]=\theta\). Use linearity: \(\mathbb E[\hat\theta]=-\mathbb E[\log X]\), then evaluate \(\mathbb E[\log X]\) with the first identity at \(a=1/\theta-1\).
$$\mathbb E[\log X]=\int_0^1 (\log x)\,\frac1\theta x^{1/\theta-1}\,dx=\frac1\theta\int_0^1(\log x)\,x^{(1/\theta-1)}\,dx.$$ With \(a=\tfrac1\theta-1\), so \(a+1=\tfrac1\theta\): $$=\frac1\theta\left(-\frac1{(1/\theta)^2}\right)=\frac1\theta\left(-\theta^2\right)=-\theta.$$ Therefore, by iid + linearity, $$\mathbb E[\hat\theta]=-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E[\log X_i]=-\mathbb E[\log X]=-(-\theta)=\theta.$$
\(\mathbb E[\hat\theta]=\theta\) ⇒ \(\hat\theta\) is unbiased. ∎
Keep track of the two minus signs: \(\mathbb E[\log X]=-\theta\) is negative (as it must be, since \(\log X\lt0\) on \((0,1)\)), and the leading \(-\tfrac1n\) flips it back to \(+\theta\).
4 marks(c) Determine \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\).
Worked solution
iid ⇒ \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)=\dfrac1{n^2}\sum\operatorname{Var}(\log X_i)=\dfrac1n\operatorname{Var}(\log X)\). Get \(\operatorname{Var}(\log X)=\mathbb E[(\log X)^2]-(\mathbb E[\log X])^2\) using the second identity.
$$\mathbb E[(\log X)^2]=\frac1\theta\int_0^1(\log x)^2 x^{1/\theta-1}\,dx=\frac1\theta\cdot\frac2{(1/\theta)^3}=\frac1\theta\cdot2\theta^3=2\theta^2.$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(\log X)=2\theta^2-(-\theta)^2=2\theta^2-\theta^2=\theta^2.$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)=\frac1n\operatorname{Var}(\log X)=\frac{\theta^2}{n}.$$
\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)=\dfrac{\theta^2}{n}.\)
Pulling the constant out of the variance gives \(1/n^2\) times a sum of \(n\) equal terms \(=1/n\) times one term — not \(1/n^2\). And \((\mathbb E[\log X])^2=(-\theta)^2=\theta^2\) is positive.
2 marks(d) What is the (approximate) distribution of \(\hat\theta\) for large \(n\)?
Worked solution
\(\hat\theta\) is a sample mean of iid terms \((-\log X_i)\) with finite mean and variance → Central Limit Theorem.
By the CLT, for large \(n\), $$\hat\theta \;\overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\; \mathsf N\!\left(\theta,\ \frac{\theta^2}{n}\right).$$
\(\hat\theta\approx\mathsf N\big(\theta,\ \theta^2/n\big)\) for large \(n\).
The mean and variance come straight from (b) and (c). The CLT applies because \(\hat\theta=\frac1n\sum(-\log X_i)\) is an average of iid random variables.

Question 3 — Paired difference: blood pressure [20 marks]

Schimanski et al. (2014): \(n=44\) subjects, systolic BP (mmHg) at forearm and upper arm (same person → paired).
ForearmUpper armForearm − Upper arm
Mean \(\bar x\)134.30132.082.22
SD \(s\)22.1925.569.64
4 marks(a) State the hypotheses to answer the researchers' question, in mathematical form; explain all notation.
Worked solution
Same subjects measured twice → work with the differences \(D=\)Forearm\(-\)Upper arm. "Can forearm be used as an alternative?" → is there any mean difference → two-sided.
Let \(\mu_D\) = population mean of the paired difference \(D=\text{Forearm}-\text{Upper arm}\) systolic BP (mmHg). $$H_0:\ \mu_D=0\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\ \mu_D\ne0.$$
\(H_0:\mu_D=0\) vs \(H_1:\mu_D\ne0\), \(\mu_D=\) mean difference (forearm − upper arm).
This is a paired (one-sample-on-differences) setup — use \(\mu_D\) and the difference SD \(9.64\). Do not treat forearm and upper arm as two independent samples with SDs 22.19 and 25.56 (they're measured on the same 44 people, so they're correlated).
5 marks(b) Perform the test for the mean difference. Report the \(t\)-statistic to 2 s.f., conclude, and interpret in context.
Worked solution
Paired \(t\)-test = one-sample \(t\) on the differences: \(T=\dfrac{\bar d-0}{s_D/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}\) (formula sheet #72).
$$T=\frac{2.22-0}{9.64/\sqrt{44}}=\frac{2.22}{9.64/6.6332}=\frac{2.22}{1.4532}=1.5276\approx1.5,\quad \text{df}=43.$$ \(P\)-value: two-sided, df\(=43\) (use df\(=40\) row): \(1.30\ (0.90)\lt1.53\lt1.68\ (0.95)\) ⇒ each tail between 0.05 and 0.10 ⇒ \(0.10\lt P\lt0.20\). (Exact \(P=0.134\).) Since \(P\gt0.05\), do not reject \(H_0\).
\(T=1.5\) (2 s.f.), df \(=43\), \(P\approx0.13\ (\gt0.05)\). Fail to reject \(H_0\).
There is no significant difference between forearm and upper-arm systolic BP (\(t=1.5,\ P\approx0.13\)). The observed \(2.22\) mmHg gap is within sampling variation, so the data support the forearm being a reasonable alternative measurement site when the upper arm is unavailable.
\(\sqrt{44}=6.63\), not \(44\). Dividing by \(n\) instead of \(\sqrt n\) is the most common paired-test slip and inflates \(T\) sixfold.
4 marks(c) A worker claims forearm readings are on average 3 mmHg larger than upper-arm. Test this claim.
Worked solution
Same paired test, but the null value shifts to the claimed 3 mmHg: \(H_0:\mu_D=3\) vs \(H_1:\mu_D\ne3\).
$$T=\frac{\bar d-3}{s_D/\sqrt n}=\frac{2.22-3}{1.4532}=\frac{-0.78}{1.4532}=-0.5367\approx-0.54,\quad \text{df}=43.$$ \(P\)-value: \(|T|=0.54\) is tiny (\(\lt1.30\), the 0.90 quantile), so each tail \(\gt0.10\) ⇒ \(P\gt0.20\). (Exact \(P=0.594\).) Fail to reject \(H_0\).
\(T=-0.54\), df \(=43\), \(P\approx0.59\ (\gt0.05)\). Fail to reject \(H_0\).
The data are entirely consistent with the worker's claim of a 3 mmHg average difference (\(t=-0.54,\ P\approx0.59\)); there is no evidence to contradict it.
Only the number you subtract changes (3 instead of 0); the SE \(s_D/\sqrt n=1.4532\) is unchanged. Don't re-derive the SE. "Fail to reject" here means consistent with — not proof that — \(\mu_D=3\).
4 marks(d) Construct a 95% CI for the standard deviation \(\sigma\) of the difference. State assumptions.
Worked solution
CI for a variance/SD of normal data → the \(\chi^2\) interval (formula sheet #63): \(\left(\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{q_2},\ \dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{q_1}\right)\), \(q_1=\chi^2_{n-1,0.025},\ q_2=\chi^2_{n-1,0.975}\). Then square-root the endpoints for \(\sigma\).
Assumption: the differences \(D\) are normally distributed (this CI is not robust to non-normality).
\(n-1=43\), \(S^2=9.64^2=92.930\), \((n-1)S^2=43\times92.930=3995.97\).
df\(=43\) is off the \(\chi^2\) table, so interpolate between df\(=40\) and df\(=50\):
q1 = χ²(43, .025): 40→24.4, 50→32.4 ⇒ 24.4 + 0.3(8.0) = 26.8 q2 = χ²(43, .975): 40→59.3, 50→71.4 ⇒ 59.3 + 0.3(12.1) = 62.9
(Exact values are \(q_1=26.79,\ q_2=62.99\) — the interpolation is spot on.) $$\sigma^2\in\left(\frac{3995.97}{62.9},\ \frac{3995.97}{26.8}\right)=(63.5,\ 149.1).$$ $$\sigma\in(\sqrt{63.5},\ \sqrt{149.1})=(7.97,\ 12.2).$$
95% CI for \(\sigma\): \((7.97,\ 12.2)\) mmHg.
We are 95% confident the true SD of the forearm−upper-arm BP difference lies between about \(7.97\) and \(12.2\) mmHg — substantial person-to-person variability in the discrepancy between the two sites.
The bigger \(\chi^2\) quantile \(q_2\) goes under the smaller endpoint. Swapping \(q_1,q_2\) flips the interval. And take the square root only at the very end to convert the variance CI to a SD CI.
3 marks(e) A future study wants the 95% margin of error for the mean difference \(\le1\) mmHg. Smallest number of subjects (use \(s=9.64\))?
Worked solution
Margin of error \(E=q\,\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\). Set \(E\le1\), solve for \(n\), round up. For a sample-size formula use the Normal quantile \(q=1.96\) (\(n\) unknown).
$$1.96\cdot\frac{9.64}{\sqrt n}\le1 \;\Rightarrow\; \sqrt n\ge1.96\times9.64=18.894 \;\Rightarrow\; n\ge18.894^2=357.0.$$
\(n=357\) subjects.
The future study would need at least 357 paired subjects to pin the mean forearm−upper-arm difference down to within \(\pm1\) mmHg with 95% confidence.
Always round sample sizes up (ceiling), never to nearest — 356.998 becomes 357, because 356 would give a margin slightly above 1. Use the SD of the difference (9.64), the "relevant" SD for a paired mean.

4 marks(b) State the ANOVA assumptions and discuss validity from the plots and table.
Worked solution
Figure 2: side-by-side boxplots of pollutant concentration for the four land covers. Forrest has the widest box/whiskers; Developed is the narrowest with one low outlier. Medians are broadly similar; boxes are roughly symmetric.
Model \(Y_{ik}=\mu_i+\varepsilon_{ik}\), \(\varepsilon_{ik}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathsf N(0,\sigma^2)\). Assumptions:
  • Independence — samples collected randomly within each area ⇒ reasonable.
  • Normality within groups — boxplots look roughly symmetric (one mild outlier in Developed) ⇒ broadly acceptable, and \(n=25\) per group helps via the CLT.
  • Equal variances (homoscedasticity) — doubtful: SDs range \(1.19\) to \(3.17\); the largest is \(3.17/1.19\approx2.7\times\) the smallest, and Forrest's box is visibly wider. This assumption is questionable.
Independence and approximate normality look fine, but the equal-variance assumption is doubtful (SD ratio \(\approx2.7\), Forrest much more variable).
Quantify equal-variance with the SD (or variance) ratio. A largest:smallest SD ratio under ~2 is usually fine; \(2.7\) here is a genuine red flag worth stating.
2 marks(c) State the null and alternative hypotheses mathematically. Define notation.
Worked solution Let \(\mu_F,\mu_W,\mu_D,\mu_G\) be the population mean pollutant concentrations (ppb) for Forrest, Wetland, Developed, Grassland. $$H_0:\ \mu_F=\mu_W=\mu_D=\mu_G\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\ \text{at least one }\mu_i\text{ differs}.$$
\(H_0\): all four means equal; \(H_1\): not all equal.
\(H_1\) is "at least one mean differs", not "all means are different". ANOVA only detects that some difference exists, not which pairs.
5 marks(d) Assuming assumptions hold, test at \(\alpha=0.05\) whether mean pollution differs across land covers. Conclude statistically and in context.
Worked solution
ANOVA \(F\)-test: compare \(F=3.16\) to \(F_{3,96}\) at the 0.95 quantile.
Under \(H_0\), \(F\sim F_{3,96}\). Observed \(F=3.16\).
Critical value from the \(F\)-table (\(\gamma=0.95\)): \(F_{3,60}=2.76\) and \(F_{3,120}=2.68\), so \(F_{3,96}\approx2.70\). Since \(3.16\gt2.70\), reject \(H_0\).
\(P\)-value: \(3.16\) exceeds the 0.95 quantile \((\approx2.70)\), so \(P\lt0.05\) (exact \(P=0.0280\)).
\(F=3.16\sim F_{3,96}\); \(F\gt F_{3,96,0.95}\approx2.70\), \(P=0.0280\ (\lt0.05)\). Reject \(H_0\).
There is significant evidence (\(F=3.16,\ P=0.028\)) that mean pollutant concentration differs across the four land-cover types. At least one land cover has a different average pollution level from the others — pollution is not uniform across the landscape.
Because the equal-variance assumption looked shaky in (b), flag that \(P=0.028\) is only moderately below 0.05, so the conclusion is not rock-solid. ANOVA doesn't say which covers differ — that needs post-hoc tests (see part (e)).
2 marks(e) A friend says: skip ANOVA, just do all pairwise \(t\)-tests. Why is this reasoning flawed and what is correct?
Worked solution
This is the multiple-comparisons problem: each \(t\)-test has its own Type I error, and running many inflates the overall (family-wise) false-positive rate.
With 4 groups there are \(\binom42=6\) pairwise tests. If each uses \(\alpha=0.05\), the chance of at least one false positive is far above 5% (roughly \(1-0.95^6\approx26\%\)). So the friend would likely "find" a spurious difference.
Flaw: many \(t\)-tests inflate the family-wise Type I error rate. Correct approach: run one ANOVA \(F\)-test first (controls overall \(\alpha\)); only if significant, follow with post-hoc pairwise tests using a correction (e.g. Bonferroni/Tukey).
Don't answer "because ANOVA is more powerful" — the key issue is error-rate inflation from doing many tests, which the omnibus ANOVA + corrected post-hoc procedure controls.
2 marks(f) Calculate \(R^2\) and interpret it for the ANOVA model.
Worked solution
\(R^2=\dfrac{\text{SST}-\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}=\dfrac{\text{SSF}}{\text{SST}}\) (formula sheet #82) = fraction of total variation explained by the factor.
$$R^2=\frac{45.96}{510.78}=0.0900.$$
\(R^2=0.0900\) (3 s.f.), i.e. about \(9.0\%\).
Only about 9% of the total variation in pollutant concentration is explained by land-cover type; the remaining ~91% is within-group (residual) variability. So although land cover has a statistically significant effect, it explains only a small part of the variation in pollution.
A result can be statistically significant (part d) yet have a small \(R^2\) — significance and effect size are different things. Mentioning both shows real understanding.

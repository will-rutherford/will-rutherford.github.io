Question 1 — Continuous random variable 15 marks

$X$ has pdf $f(x)=k\,x^3$ for $1\le x\le 2$ (and 0 elsewhere), where $k$ is a positive constant.

(a) Determine the value of $k$. 2

Density integrates to 1 → $\int_1^2 k x^3\,dx=1$.
$$\int_1^2 k x^3\,dx=k\left[\tfrac{x^4}{4}\right]_1^2=k\cdot\tfrac{16-1}{4}=\tfrac{15k}{4}=1\;\Rightarrow\;k=\tfrac{4}{15}.$$
$k=\dfrac{4}{15}\approx 0.267$.

(b) Determine $\mathbb E(X)$. 3

$$\mathbb E X=\int_1^2 x\,k x^3\,dx=k\int_1^2 x^4\,dx=k\left[\tfrac{x^5}{5}\right]_1^2=\tfrac{4}{15}\cdot\tfrac{32-1}{5}=\tfrac{4}{15}\cdot\tfrac{31}{5}=\tfrac{124}{75}.$$
$\mathbb E X=\dfrac{124}{75}\approx 1.65$.

(c) Determine $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. 4

$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E X^2-(\mathbb E X)^2$; get $\mathbb E X^2=\int x^2 f\,dx$.
$$\mathbb E X^2=k\int_1^2 x^5\,dx=k\left[\tfrac{x^6}{6}\right]_1^2=\tfrac{4}{15}\cdot\tfrac{64-1}{6}=\tfrac{4}{15}\cdot\tfrac{63}{6}=\tfrac{4}{15}\cdot\tfrac{21}{2}=2.8.$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=2.8-\left(\tfrac{124}{75}\right)^2=2.8-2.7335=0.0665.$$
$\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.0665$ (s.d. $\approx 0.258$).
Keep $\mathbb E X$ to full precision ($124/75=1.65333\ldots$) when squaring. Rounding it to 1.65 early gives $2.7225$ and a noticeably wrong variance.

(d) Determine the cumulative distribution function of $X$. 3

$F(x)=\int_1^x f(t)\,dt$ on the support; 0 below, 1 above.
For $1\le x\le 2$: $$F(x)=\int_1^x k t^3\,dt=k\left[\tfrac{t^4}{4}\right]_1^x=\tfrac{4}{15}\cdot\tfrac{x^4-1}{4}=\frac{x^4-1}{15}.$$ $$F(x)=\begin{cases}0,& x\lt 1\\[2pt]\dfrac{x^4-1}{15},& 1\le x\le 2\\[2pt] 1,& x\gt 2\end{cases}$$ Check: $F(2)=\tfrac{16-1}{15}=1$. ✓

(e) Determine the median of $X$. 3

Median $m$ solves $F(m)=0.5$.
$$\frac{m^4-1}{15}=0.5\;\Rightarrow\;m^4-1=7.5\;\Rightarrow\;m^4=8.5\;\Rightarrow\;m=8.5^{1/4}=1.71.$$
median $=8.5^{1/4}\approx 1.71$.
On the FX82: $8.5^{1/4}=\sqrt{\sqrt{8.5}}$ — two square-root presses. Confirm $1\lt 1.71\lt 2$, so it sits inside the support.

Question 2 — Properties of an estimator 10 marks

$X_1,\dots,X_n$ independent with $X_i\sim\mathsf N(\theta y_i,\;y_i^2)$, where $y_1,\dots,y_n$ are known non-zero constants. Estimator $$\hat\theta=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{X_i}{y_i}.$$

(a) Find $\mathbb E(\hat\theta)$ and state whether $\hat\theta$ is unbiased for $\theta$. 4

"Unbiased?" → compute $\mathbb E(\hat\theta)$ using linearity (#51); unbiased iff it equals $\theta$.
Since $\mathbb E(X_i)=\theta y_i$, $$\mathbb E(\hat\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\mathbb E(X_i)}{y_i}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\theta y_i}{y_i}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \theta=\frac1n(n\theta)=\theta.$$
$\mathbb E(\hat\theta)=\theta$, so $\hat\theta$ is an unbiased estimator of $\theta$.

(b) Determine $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)$. 4

Independence ⇒ variances add (#52); pull out constants with $\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Here $\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=y_i^2$.
$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\frac{X_i}{y_i}\Big)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X_i)}{y_i^2}=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{y_i^2}{y_i^2}=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n 1=\frac{n}{n^2}=\frac1n.$$
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)=\dfrac1n$.
The whole point of dividing by $y_i$ is that it exactly cancels the $y_i^2$ variance, giving each term variance 1 — a clean, constant-free answer.

(c) What is the distribution of $\hat\theta$? 2

A linear combination of independent normal random variables is itself normal (#54). Combining with (a) and (b): $$\hat\theta\sim\mathsf N\!\left(\theta,\ \tfrac1n\right).$$
$\hat\theta\sim\mathsf N(\theta,\,1/n)$ — exactly normal (no CLT/approximation needed, since the $X_i$ are already normal).

Question 3 — Data ethics (multiple choice) 4 marks

(a) Discarding data from wells where all cells died "for unknown reasons" is best described as: 2

A) Trimming   B) Cooking   C) Fudging   D) Stacking
Match the action to the classic categories of scientific data manipulation. Selectively discarding results that don't fit (keeping only the "good" wells) is cooking.
B) Cooking. Cooking = retaining only the observations that suit the desired outcome and quietly dropping the inconvenient ones. Here entire wells with poor (all-dead) results are thrown out, biasing what remains.
Distinguish the terms: Trimming = smoothing/shaving individual measurements to look tidier; Cooking = selectively discarding whole results; Fudging/forging = inventing data outright.

(b) Which is a significant ethical concern about the caffeine/cognitive-failures survey? 2

The study collects caffeine, age, gender, education, and alcohol use per person. Options A–D; the key issue is data sensitivity.
C) Personal information is being collected, so steps must be taken to ensure participants' privacy and anonymity.
The survey gathers identifying and sensitive personal data (health-related behaviours, demographics). The genuine ethical obligation is confidentiality / privacy / anonymity — the hallmark concern of human-data research. The other options describe implausible harms or sampling quibbles, not ethics-of-design issues.

(a) State $H_0$ and $H_1$ for the researchers' question; explain the notation. 4

Two independent groups, testing for a difference in means → two-sample $t$. "Differences in protein content" → two-sided.
Let $\mu_S$ and $\mu_G$ be the true mean protein contents (g/100 g) of sheep and goat pâté. $$H_0:\;\mu_S=\mu_G\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\;\mu_S\ne\mu_G.$$
Equal sample s.d.'s (both 0.17) point to the equal-variance (pooled) two-sample $t$-procedure, used in (b) and (c).

(b) 95% CI for the mean difference (3 sig figs); state statistical and contextual conclusions. 7

Equal variances → pooled variance $S_p^2$ (#63), then CI $\bar X-\bar Y\pm q\,S_p\sqrt{\tfrac1m+\tfrac1n}$ with $q=t_{0.975,\,m+n-2}$ (#64).
Pooled variance ($m=n=6$, both $s=0.17$): $$S_p^2=\frac{(m-1)S_X^2+(n-1)S_Y^2}{m+n-2}=\frac{5(0.17^2)+5(0.17^2)}{10}=0.17^2=0.0289,\quad S_p=0.17.$$ $df=m+n-2=10$; from tables $t_{10,\,0.975}=2.23$. With $\bar X-\bar Y=20.7-19.2=1.5$: $$1.5\pm 2.23\,(0.17)\sqrt{\tfrac16+\tfrac16}=1.5\pm 2.23(0.0981)=1.5\pm 0.219=(1.28,\ 1.72).$$
We are 95% confident the mean protein content of sheep pâté exceeds goat pâté by between 1.28 and 1.72 g per 100 g. As the interval lies entirely above 0, there is a statistically significant difference: sheep pâté has more protein on average.
With both $s$ equal, $S_p=0.17$ falls straight out — no arithmetic needed. Report the CI to 3 sig figs as asked.

(c) Test the producer's claim that sheep pâté has at least 2 g more protein than goat (5% level). 7

Claim is a difference $\ge 2$ (not $=0$), so this is a one-sided test against a non-zero value. $H_0$ takes the boundary $\mu_S-\mu_G=2$; the test statistic subtracts 2.
$$H_0:\;\mu_S-\mu_G=2\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\;\mu_S-\mu_G\lt 2$$ (seeking evidence against the "at least 2 g" claim). Test statistic (pooled, $S_p=0.17$): $$T=\frac{(\bar X-\bar Y)-2}{S_p\sqrt{\tfrac1m+\tfrac1n}}=\frac{1.5-2}{0.17\sqrt{1/3}}=\frac{-0.5}{0.0981}=-5.09\quad\text{on }10\text{ df}.$$ $p$-value (one-sided), from tables ($t_{10}$): the $0.9995$ quantile is $4.59$; since $|{-5.09}|\gt 4.59$, the one-tail area is $p\lt 0.0005$ (exact $p=0.000234$). Reject $H_0$.
There is strong evidence ($p\lt 0.001$) that sheep pâté does not have at least 2 g more protein per 100 g than goat pâté — the true mean difference is significantly less than 2 g. The producer's claim is not supported (the data put the gap around 1.5 g, per part (b)).
The "2 g" goes into the numerator: $T=\frac{(\bar X-\bar Y)-2}{\text{s.e.}}$, not $\frac{\bar X-\bar Y}{\text{s.e.}}$. Testing against 0 here would answer the wrong question.

(d) A farmer claims it takes more than five years to grow 3 cm in diameter. Is there evidence against this? 4

Slope $\beta_1$ = years of age per cm of diameter. Growing 3 cm corresponds to $3\beta_1$ years. Claim: $3\beta_1\gt 5$, i.e. $\beta_1\gt 5/3$. Test the slope against $5/3$.
$$H_0:\;\beta_1=\tfrac{5}{3}\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\;\beta_1\lt \tfrac53\ \ (\text{evidence against the "}\gt 5\text{ yr" claim}).$$ $$T=\frac{\hat\beta_1-5/3}{\text{s.e.}(\hat\beta_1)}=\frac{0.6372-1.6667}{0.0187}=\frac{-1.0295}{0.0187}=-55.1\quad\text{on }106\text{ df}.$$ This is astronomically far into the tail: from tables even the $0.9995$ quantile of $t_{106}$ is only ≈3.4, so $p\lt 0.0005$ (exact $p\approx 4\times10^{-80}$). Reject $H_0$.
There is overwhelming evidence against the farmer's claim. The model estimates only $3\times0.6372\approx 1.9$ years to grow 3 cm — far less than five. Growth is much faster than the farmer believes.
Translate the claim into the slope: "5 years for 3 cm" means $\beta_1$ compared with $5/3\approx1.667$, not with 0 or 5. Testing $\beta_1=0$ would just confirm the slope is non-zero — the wrong question.

(e) For ±5-year accuracy, is the model feasible in practice? 2

The residual standard error is 5.454 years — already larger than the required ±5 years, and a 95% prediction interval spans roughly $\pm 2\times5.45\approx\pm11$ years (cf. the width $(0.66,22.4)$ in (c)).
No — the model is not accurate enough. Typical prediction errors (≈5.5 years, with prediction intervals of ~±11 years) exceed the ±5-year tolerance, so it cannot reliably age individual trees to within 5 years despite the high $R^2$.
High $R^2$ (0.916) is not the same as high predictive accuracy. Judge feasibility by the residual/prediction error against the stated tolerance, not by $R^2$.

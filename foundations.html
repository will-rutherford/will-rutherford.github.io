Why this page exists. Every CI and every test in this course ends the same way: "…where \(q\) is the \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile of \(t_{n-1}\)" (or \(\chi^2_{n-1}\), or \(F\)). If you don't know what those distributions are — where they come from, why the subscript is \(n-1\), what the quantile notation means — the recipes are just spells. This page is the ground floor underneath Chapters 6, 7, 8 and 11. Read it once, slowly, and the rest of the site stops being magic.

1 · Statistics are random variables too

The one idea everything rests on

A statistic is any number you compute from data — the sample mean \(\bar x\), the sample variance \(s^2\), a sample proportion \(\widehat p\). Here is the shift in thinking the whole course demands:

Your data \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are a random sample. If you re-ran the study tomorrow you would get different numbers, so any statistic you compute would come out different too. That means a statistic like \(\bar X\) is itself a random variable — it has a distribution, a mean, and a spread — exactly like a single data point does.

Lower-case \(\bar x = 75.98\) is the one number you actually got (an estimate). Upper-case \(\bar X = \tfrac1n\sum X_i\) is the random recipe that would give a different number each repeat (an estimator). The whole game is: study the distribution of \(\bar X\) so you can say how far your single \(\bar x\) might be from the truth.

The sampling distribution

Imagine drawing sample after sample after sample, computing \(\bar x\) each time, and making a histogram of all those \(\bar x\) values. That histogram is the sampling distribution of the mean. It is not the distribution of the raw data — it is the distribution of the statistic.

Two things are true of it (both proved in Ch 3), for data with population mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu \qquad\text{and}\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.\]

The mean of the sampling distribution sits right on the truth \(\mu\) (that's why \(\bar X\) is unbiased). The variance is the population variance shrunk by a factor of \(n\) — average more numbers and the average wobbles less. That shrinkage is the single most important fact in inferential statistics.

Standard error vs standard deviation — the classic confusion

Take the square root of \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\):

\[\underbrace{\sigma}_{\text{SD of the DATA}}\qquad\text{vs}\qquad \underbrace{\operatorname{s.e.}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}}_{\text{SD of the STATISTic }\bar X}.\]

  • Standard deviation \(\sigma\) (estimated by \(s\)) measures how spread out the individual observations are. It does not shrink as you collect more data — a taller pile of people still has the same spread of heights.
  • Standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) (estimated by \(s/\sqrt n\)) measures how spread out the sample mean is across hypothetical repeats. It does shrink — like \(1/\sqrt n\).
Forgetting to divide by \(\sqrt n\) — using \(s\) where you needed \(s/\sqrt n\) — is the single most common mistake in the whole course. Every CI and test statistic for a mean uses the standard error, never the raw SD.
The \(\sqrt n\) also explains why halving a CI's width costs four times the sample size: width \(\propto 1/\sqrt n\), so to divide width by 2 you multiply \(n\) by \(4\).

2 · \(Z\): the \(\sigma\)-known ideal world

Standardising the mean

Suppose the data are Normal: \(X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). Then a linear combination of Normals is Normal, so the sample mean is exactly Normal:

\[\bar X \sim N\!\left(\mu,\;\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right).\]

Now do the move you already know from Ch 1–2 — subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation — except the "standard deviation" here is the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt n\):

\[Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1).\]

This is a pivot: a quantity built from the data and the unknown \(\mu\) whose distribution — standard Normal — contains no unknown parameters at all. That is what makes it useful: we know its quantiles exactly (\(1.96\), etc.) no matter what \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) happen to be.

You may use \(Z\) for a mean only when \(\sigma\) is genuinely known (handed to you in the problem). That is rare in practice — you almost never know the population SD but not its mean.

Why \(Z\) is not enough

In real problems you don't know \(\sigma\). The only honest thing to do is estimate it from the same sample, using \(s\). So you'd like to write

\[\frac{\bar X-\mu}{s/\sqrt n}.\]

But now there is a problem: \(s\) is itself a random quantity that changes sample to sample. You've replaced a fixed denominator with a jittery one. This extra randomness means the ratio is no longer \(N(0,1)\) — and pretending it is would make your intervals too narrow. Fixing this is exactly what Student's \(t\) does.

3 · \(t\): what happens when you estimate \(\sigma\)

The definition that actually matters

Replace the known \(\sigma\) with the sample \(s\), and the standardised mean follows a new distribution — Student's \(t\) with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom:

\[t=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{s/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}.\]

Read this carefully, because it is the single most misunderstood object on the course:

\(t_{n-1}\) is NOT the distribution of your data. It is the distribution of the statistic \((\bar X-\mu)/(s/\sqrt n)\). Your data can be heights, weights, blood pressures — anything Normal. The standardised sample mean computed from them is what follows \(t\).

Why heavier tails than \(Z\)?

Compared with \(Z\), the \(t\)-distribution is bell-shaped and symmetric about 0, but with heavier tails — more probability far from the centre. The reason is exactly the extra randomness we introduced: sometimes \(s\) comes out too small purely by chance, which inflates the ratio \((\bar X-\mu)/(s/\sqrt n)\) and produces occasional large values. To stay honest about that risk, \(t\) spreads more mass into the tails, which makes its critical values larger than \(1.96\). Larger critical value → wider interval → the price you pay for not knowing \(\sigma\).

Degrees of freedom: why \(n-1\)?

The \(s\) in the denominator is computed from the deviations \(X_i-\bar X\). Here is the one-line reason there are only \(n-1\) "free" pieces of information in them:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar X)=0\quad\text{always.}\]

The deviations are forced to sum to zero, so once you know any \(n-1\) of them, the last one is determined — it carries no new information. You spent one degree of freedom estimating the centre \(\bar X\); \(n-1\) remain to estimate spread. That "\(n-1\)" is the same one you divide by in \(s^2=\tfrac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\), and it is the subscript on \(t_{n-1}\).

Critical values: \(t\) vs \(Z\), side by side

The \(0.975\) quantile (the number you use for a 95% two-sided interval) shrinks toward \(1.96\) as \(df\) grows:

\(df=n-1\)\(t_{0.975,\,df}\) (95% critical value)comment

As \(df\to\infty\), \(s\) becomes an essentially perfect estimate of \(\sigma\), the extra randomness vanishes, and \(t_{df}\to N(0,1)\). That is why the very last row of the \(t\)-table (labelled \(\infty\)) is the standard-Normal row.

Why this course never needs a separate \(z\)-table for means. The formula sheet's mean CI (item 62) always uses the \(t_{n-1}\) quantile. For large \(n\) that quantile is already \(\approx 1.96\) — e.g. \(t_{0.975,303}=1.97\) — so reading the \(t\)-table's \(\infty\) row gives you the Normal value anyway. One table covers both worlds.

4 · Quantile-notation clinic

What "\(1-\alpha/2\) quantile" actually means

For a 95% interval, \(\alpha=0.05\) (the total probability left outside the interval). It is split evenly into two tails of \(\alpha/2 = 0.025\) each. The quantile you want cuts off the upper tail, so it is the value with area \(1-\alpha/2\) below it:

\[1-\frac{\alpha}{2}=1-\frac{0.05}{2}=1-0.025=0.975.\]

The misreading that wrecks marks. \(1-\alpha/2\) means \(1-(\alpha/2)=0.975\). It does NOT mean \((1-\alpha)/2=0.95/2=0.475\). The division is only under the \(\alpha\); the "1 minus" wraps the whole thing. Say it as "one, minus alpha-over-two".

Two labelling conventions for the same number

Tables and textbooks disagree on whether the subscript names the area below or the tail area above. They point to the identical value:

  • Area-below (quantile) convention: \(t_{0.975,\,14}=2.14\). "The value with 0.975 of the area to its left."
  • Tail-area convention: \(t_{0.025,\,14}=2.14\). "The value cutting off an upper tail of 0.025." (Some books write \(t_{\alpha/2}\) for this.)

Both describe the point at \(+2.14\) on the \(t_{14}\) axis. The formula sheet uses the quantile (area-below) convention — "the \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile" — so \(0.975\) is what you look up. If a source writes \(t_{0.025,14}\) it means the same positive number by the tail-area convention.

Sanity check: a 95% critical value must be a bit bigger than 1.96 (for finite \(df\)) and positive. If your table lookup gives you 0.475 or a negative number, you've read the wrong column.

The picture (95% interval, symmetric \(t\) or \(Z\))

Total area \(=1\). Middle \(95\%\) is the interval; each tail is \(2.5\%\). The cut-points are \(\pm q\) where \(q\) is the \(0.975\) quantile.

5 · \(\chi^2\): the distribution of a variance

Definition — a sum of squared Normals

Take \(k\) independent standard Normals \(Z_1,\dots,Z_k\), square each, and add them up. The result follows a chi-square distribution with \(k\) degrees of freedom:

\[\chi^2_k = Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\dots+Z_k^2.\]

That's the whole definition. You never need its pdf on this course — only its shape and the one key fact below.

Its shape — and why it's lopsided

  • Strictly positive. It is a sum of squares, so it can never be negative — the distribution lives entirely on \([0,\infty)\).
  • Right-skewed (a long tail to the right), especially for small \(k\). It is not symmetric, unlike \(Z\) and \(t\).
  • Mean \(=k\). Each \(Z_i^2\) has mean 1, and there are \(k\) of them.
  • As \(k\) grows it becomes more symmetric and bell-like, but for the \(df\) you'll meet it stays visibly skewed.

Because it's asymmetric, its lower and upper quantiles are not mirror images — you must look up two different numbers, unlike the single \(\pm q\) of \(t\).

The one key fact you must know cold

For an iid Normal sample, the (scaled) sample variance follows a chi-square:

\[\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}.\]

This is the pivot for variance: it links the thing you can compute (\(s^2\)) to the thing you want (\(\sigma^2\)) through a distribution with no other unknowns. The \(df\) is \(n-1\) for the very same reason as in \(t\) — \(s^2\) uses the deviations \(X_i-\bar X\), which sum to zero.

This result requires Normal data and — unlike the mean CI — is not rescued by the CLT for large \(n\). Whenever you build a variance/SD interval you must state the normality assumption.

Deriving the variance CI — slowly, and why the big quantile gives the small bound

Start from the pivot and trap it between its lower and upper quantiles. For a \(95\%\) interval with \(df=n-1\), let \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,\,n-1}\) (small) and \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,\,n-1}\) (big):

\[\mathbb P\!\left(q_1 \lt \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2} \lt q_2\right)=0.95.\]

Now solve the inequality for \(\sigma^2\). This is where people go wrong, so take it one step at a time. Take reciprocals of all three parts — and remember taking reciprocals flips the direction of each inequality:

\[\frac{1}{q_1} \gt \frac{\sigma^2}{(n-1)s^2} \gt \frac{1}{q_2}.\]

Multiply through by \((n-1)s^2\) (positive, so directions stay) and rewrite left-to-right:

\[\frac{(n-1)s^2}{q_2} \lt \sigma^2 \lt \frac{(n-1)s^2}{q_1}.\]

There it is: the big quantile \(q_2\) landed in the denominator of the lower bound, and the small quantile \(q_1\) in the denominator of the upper bound. Dividing by a bigger number gives a smaller result — that reciprocal flip is why the quantiles appear to be "crossed". They are not crossed; the algebra put them there.

So the 95% CI for the variance is

\[\left(\frac{(n-1)s^2}{q_2},\;\frac{(n-1)s^2}{q_1}\right),\qquad q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,\,n-1},\;\; q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,\,n-1}.\]

For a CI on the standard deviation \(\sigma\), take the square root of both endpoints — and only at the very end.

The 2024 Q3(d) ambush. That question asked for a CI for the standard deviation of paired BP differences. Two ways to lose marks: (1) putting \(q_1\) under the lower bound (interval comes out backwards), and (2) forgetting the final square root (you'd report a variance interval when a SD was asked). See Ch 6 WE3 for the full worked numbers.

6 · \(F\): comparing two variances

Definition — a ratio of two chi-squares

Take two independent chi-square variables, divide each by its own degrees of freedom, and take the ratio:

\[F=\frac{\chi^2_{d_1}/d_1}{\chi^2_{d_2}/d_2}\sim F(d_1,d_2).\]

It has two degrees-of-freedom parameters — numerator \(d_1\) and denominator \(d_2\), in that order (they are not interchangeable). Like \(\chi^2\) it is positive and right-skewed.

Why it exists

Each \(\chi^2/df\) is essentially an estimate of a variance. Their ratio therefore compares two variances. If two groups really have the same variance, the ratio sits near 1; a ratio far above 1 is evidence they differ.

This is the engine of ANOVA: the \(F\)-statistic is

\[F=\frac{\text{between-group variance}}{\text{within-group variance}}.\]

If the group means are truly equal, between-group variation is just noise of the same size as within-group variation, so \(F\approx 1\). If the means differ, the numerator inflates and \(F\) grows large — that's what a significant ANOVA detects. The full machinery (the ANOVA table, reading the \(F\)-row, \(p\)-values) lives in Ch 8 — ANOVA. For foundations, the ratio-of-chi-squares idea is all you need.

7 · The universal CI machine

Every confidence interval on this course is the same three moves: (1) form a pivot — a statistic whose distribution has no unknowns; (2) trap it between quantiles of that reference distribution; (3) rearrange for the parameter. Only the pivot and its reference distribution change:

ParameterPivotReference distributionQuantiles needed
Mean \(\mu\) (\(\sigma\) unknown)\(\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{s/\sqrt n}\)\(t_{n-1}\)one \(\pm q\)  (\(q=t_{0.975}\))
Mean \(\mu\) (\(\sigma\) known, rare)\(\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\)\(N(0,1)\)one \(\pm q\)  (\(q=1.96\))
Variance \(\sigma^2\) / SD \(\sigma\)\(\dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2}\)\(\chi^2_{n-1}\)two: \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025}\), \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975}\)
Proportion \(p\)\(\dfrac{\widehat p-p}{\sqrt{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)/n}}\)\(N(0,1)\) (via CLT)one \(\pm q\)  (\(q=1.96\))
Ratio of variances / ANOVA\(\dfrac{s_1^2/\sigma_1^2}{s_2^2/\sigma_2^2}\)\(F(d_1,d_2)\)\(F\)-quantile(s) (see Ch 8)
The symmetry payoff. \(t\) and \(Z\) are symmetric, so one number \(q\) does both ends via \(\pm q\). \(\chi^2\) and \(F\) are skewed, so you need two separate quantiles. That single fact — symmetric ⟹ one quantile, skewed ⟹ two — tells you at a glance how many table lookups a problem needs.

8 · Table-reading drills

Attempt each before revealing. Every number is verified with scipy.stats. Practise reading both conventions.

D1. For a 95% CI with \(n=15\), what quantile of \(t\) do you look up, and what is it?

\(df=n-1=14\); \(\alpha=0.05\); you want the \(1-\alpha/2 = 0.975\) quantile. \(t_{0.975,\,14}=2.14\). (In tail-area notation the same number is \(t_{0.025,14}\).)

D2. A source writes \(t_{0.025,\,14}\). Is this the same as your answer to D1, and is it positive or negative?

Same magnitude: it is the upper-tail-area label for the point at \(+2.14\), so it is positive, \(=2.14\). The lower cut-point \(-2.14\) would be written \(t_{0.975}\) in tail-area terms (or the \(0.025\) quantile). Symmetric distribution ⟹ mirror images.

D3. \(n=5\), 95% CI for a mean. What is \(t_{0.975,\,4}\), and why is it so much bigger than 1.96?

\(df=4\), so \(t_{0.975,4}=2.78\). It's large because with only 4 degrees of freedom \(s\) is a poor, jittery estimate of \(\sigma\); the heavy \(t\)-tails widen the interval to stay honest. Contrast \(t_{0.975,30}=2.04\) and \(t_{0.975,\infty}=1.96\).

D4. \(n=44\), 95% variance CI. Which two \(\chi^2\) quantiles do you need, and what are they?

\(df=43\). You need both tails: \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,\,43}=26.79\) and \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,\,43}=62.99\). The big one \(q_2=62.99\) goes under the lower bound; the small one \(q_1=26.79\) under the upper bound. (If your table stops at \(df=40\): \(24.43\) and \(59.34\).)

D5. \(n=27\) juice bottles, 95% CI for the variance. State the two quantiles and the reason there are two, not one.

\(df=26\): \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,\,26}=13.84\), \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,\,26}=41.92\). Two are needed because \(\chi^2\) is skewed — its lower and upper quantiles are not mirror images, so no single \(\pm q\) works (that shortcut only exists for the symmetric \(t\) and \(Z\)).

D6. What is the \(0.95\) quantile of \(\chi^2_{10}\), and roughly where is the distribution's centre?

\(\chi^2_{0.95,\,10}=18.31\). The centre (mean) is at \(k=10\), and because the distribution is right-skewed the \(0.95\) quantile sits well to the right of the mean. (For a two-sided 95% variance CI at \(df=10\) you'd instead use \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,10}=3.25\) and \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,10}=20.48\).)

D7. Confidence 90% instead of 95%. What is \(\alpha\), what is \(1-\alpha/2\), and what \(Z\) quantile results?

\(\alpha=0.10\); \(1-\alpha/2 = 1-0.05 = 0.95\); \(z_{0.95}=1.645\). Note it is smaller than the 95%-CI value \(1.96\) — less confidence ⟹ narrower interval ⟹ smaller quantile. And it is emphatically not \((1-\alpha)/2 = 0.45\).

9 · What you do not need

This page deliberately stops short of things STAT1301 never asks. To keep your revision ruthless, here is what you can safely ignore:

  • The pdf formulas of \(t\), \(\chi^2\) or \(F\). You never write them down, integrate them, or evaluate them by hand — you read quantiles off the provided tables (or a computer).
  • Derivations of these distributions — e.g. proving that \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\), or that \(t\) is a Normal over a scaled root-chi-square. Interesting, not examinable here.
  • Moment-generating functions (MGFs) of \(t\), \(\chi^2\), \(F\). Not required (and \(t\)'s doesn't even exist in the usual sense).
  • Gamma / beta function machinery behind the densities.
What you do need: the definition in words (t = standardised mean with \(\sigma\) estimated; \(\chi^2\) = sum of squared \(Z\)s; \(F\) = ratio of scaled \(\chi^2\)s), the shape (symmetric vs skewed), the key pivots, and the ability to read a quantile in either convention. That's the whole ground floor.

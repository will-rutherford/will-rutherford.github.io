§1 · Concepts in depth

The one-sentence idea

ANOVA answers "do these groups have the same mean, or not?" for more than two groups at once. The response is a quantitative variable; the group labels come from one or more categorical explanatory variables called factors (each factor tested at several levels). A two-sample \(t\)-test handles exactly two groups (notes §7.5); ANOVA generalises it to \(d\) groups — and to two crossed factors — while keeping the machinery of a single hypothesis test. (Notes §8.1.)

Response = numeric (yield, plaque index, healing time). Factor = category (fertilizer, mouth-rinse, land cover). If the "predictor" is itself numeric and you fit a line, that's regression (Ch 9), not ANOVA.
New to the underlying machinery — sampling distributions, standard error, and where the \(Z\), \(t\), \(\chi^2\) and \(F\) shapes come from? That background is taught in one place: see 📐 Foundations. ANOVA leans on the \(F\) distribution, so it is worth skimming that section first.

🧭 The core idea — signal vs noise (read this first)

Forget formulas for a moment. Picture the numbers from three groups plotted on a line. There are two kinds of spread in that picture:

  • BETWEEN groups — how far apart the three group averages sit from each other. This is the "signal": if the groups really are different, their averages drift apart.
  • WITHIN groups — how much the individual points scatter around their own group's average. This is the "noise": ordinary person-to-person, plot-to-plot randomness that has nothing to do with which group you are in.

ANOVA is one question in disguise: is the between-group spread big compared to the within-group noise? If the group averages are further apart than random scatter alone could plausibly produce, the groups genuinely differ. If the gap between averages is no bigger than the wobble you already see inside each group, it could easily be a fluke. That comparison — signal divided by noise — is exactly what the \(F\)-statistic computes.

The name paradox, resolved. "Why is it called Analysis of Variance when I want to compare means?" Because the tool for judging whether means are far apart is to measure two variances and compare them: the variance of the group means (between) against the variance within groups. Big between-variance relative to within-variance ⇒ the means are not all equal. You analyse variances to draw a conclusion about means.

Vocabulary (the words the exam uses without defining)

  • Factor — a categorical explanatory variable (e.g. fertilizer type). Level — one specific category of that factor (e.g. brand A). A one-way study has one factor; a two-way study crosses two.
  • Replicate — one observation within a level/cell. \(n_i\) = number of replicates in level \(i\).
  • Balanced design — every group/cell has the same number of replicates. Balance is what makes the sums of squares add up exactly (§1.6), which is why table-completion questions always say "balanced".
  • Residual — one point minus its own group mean, \(e_{ik}=Y_{ik}-\overline Y_i\). Residuals are the within-group scatter, measured point by point.

Why not just run lots of \(t\)-tests?

With 4 groups there are \(\binom{4}{2}=6\) pairwise \(t\)-tests. Each carries a Type-I error risk of \(\alpha=0.05\); run six and the chance of at least one false positive balloons well above 5%. ANOVA delivers a single test of "all means equal" at one controlled \(\alpha\). Only after ANOVA rejects do you do controlled pairwise follow-ups (multiple comparisons, §1.7). This exact "why is my friend wrong?" argument was worth 2 marks in 2024 Q4(e).

One-way (single-factor) model — Definition 8.1

One factor with \(d\) levels \(i=1,\dots,d\); within level \(i\) there are \(n_i\) replicates \(k=1,\dots,n_i\); total \(n=n_1+\cdots+n_d\).

$$Y_{ik}=\mu_i+\varepsilon_{ik},\qquad \varepsilon_{ik}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$$

Each level has its own mean \(\mu_i\) but a common variance \(\sigma^2\) — equal variance is baked into the model. R uses the equivalent "factor-effects" form \(Y_{ik}=\mu_1+\alpha_i+\varepsilon_{ik}\), where \(\mu_1\) is the reference (level-1) mean and \(\alpha_i=\mu_i-\mu_1\) is the incremental effect of level \(i\). (Notes §8.2.1, eq 8.1–8.3.)

🧭 The equation in plain words. \(Y_{ik}=\mu_i+\varepsilon_{ik}\) reads: "any single measurement = the true average of the group it belongs to, plus a random personal wobble." The wobble \(\varepsilon\) has mean 0 (it pushes up as often as down) and the same size \(\sigma\) in every group. The factor-effects version \(Y=\mu_1+\alpha_i+\varepsilon\) just re-writes each group mean as "the baseline group's mean, plus/minus a shift \(\alpha_i\)". So \(\alpha_i=0\) for every level is the same statement as "all the group means are equal" — which is why the hypotheses can be written either as \(\mu_1=\dots=\mu_d\) or as "all \(\alpha_i=0\)".

Estimation — where the numbers come from

Group means: \(\widehat{\mu_i}=\overline Y_i=\frac1{n_i}\sum_k Y_{ik}\) — just the sample mean within each level.

The variance \(\sigma^2\) is pooled across all groups (that is the whole point of assuming equal variance). Using residuals \(e_{ik}=Y_{ik}-\overline Y_i\), and losing \(d\) degrees of freedom to the \(d\) estimated group means:

$$\widehat{\sigma^2}=\frac{\sum_i\sum_k (Y_{ik}-\overline Y_i)^2}{n-d}=\frac{\text{SSE}}{n-d}=\text{MSE}.$$

MSE (mean squared residual error) is the "within-group noise" yardstick. Its square root, \(\sqrt{\text{MSE}}\), is the pooled standard deviation — a single estimate of \(\sigma\) that the exam repeatedly asks for. (Notes §8.2.2, eq 8.4.)

Partitioning variability & the \(F\)-test

Total variability splits cleanly into "between groups" + "within groups":

$$\underbrace{\text{SST}}_{\sum\sum(Y_{ik}-\overline Y)^2}=\underbrace{\text{SSF}}_{\text{between (factor)}}+\underbrace{\text{SSE}}_{\text{within (error)}}$$

where \(\text{SSF}=\sum_i n_i(\overline Y_i-\overline Y)^2\) measures how far the group means spread from the grand mean. Convert each SS to a mean square by dividing by its DF, then form the ratio:

$$F=\frac{\text{MSF}}{\text{MSE}}=\frac{\text{SSF}/(d-1)}{\text{SSE}/(n-d)}\ \overset{H_0}{\sim}\ F(d-1,\ n-d).$$

Intuition: \(F\) compares between-group spread to within-group noise. If \(H_0:\mu_1=\cdots=\mu_d\) is true, both estimate the same \(\sigma^2\) and \(F\approx1\). Big between-group differences push \(F\) up — so it is a right-one-sided test: reject for large \(F\), P-value \(=\mathbb P(F>f)\). (Notes §8.2.3, Theorem 8.1.)

🧭 What each quantity means in words. SS (sum of squares) = raw total amount of a kind of spread, in squared units — it grows just by adding more data, so it is not yet comparable. MS (mean square) = SS ÷ its df = spread per degree of freedom, i.e. an average variance that no longer depends on sample size. MSF is the between-group variance (signal); MSE is the within-group variance (noise). \(F\) = MSF / MSE is literally "signal-to-noise as a single number": about 1 when nothing is going on, much bigger than 1 when the group means genuinely differ.
🧭 Why the \(F\) needs two degrees of freedom. \(F\) is a ratio of two independent variance estimates, and the shape of that ratio's distribution depends on how much data went into each estimate separately — so it carries one df for the numerator (between: \(d-1\)) and one for the denominator (within: \(n-d\)). A ratio of two things needs to know the reliability of the top and of the bottom. Full derivation of the \(F\) family as a ratio of variances is in 📐 Foundations.
🧭 Why the df partition the way they do. Degrees of freedom = independent pieces of information left over. You start with \(n-1\) total (one lost to estimating the grand mean). Pinning down \(d\) group means costs \(d-1\) of them to "between" (the last group mean is fixed once you know the grand mean and the others), leaving \(n-d\) for "within". So \((d-1)+(n-d)=n-1\) is not a coincidence — the information splits the same way the spread does.
A significant \(F\) does NOT mean all groups differ. Rejecting \(H_0\) says only "the means are not all equal" — i.e. at least one group differs from at least one other. It could be that just one group stands apart while the rest are indistinguishable. To find out which pairs differ you must run the multiple-comparison follow-up (§1.7) — never claim "every group is different" from the \(F\)-test alone.

Two-way (two-factor) ANOVA — Definition 8.2

Factor 1 has \(d_1\) levels, Factor 2 has \(d_2\) levels; \(n_{ij}\) replicates in cell \((i,j)\); total \(n=\sum_i\sum_j n_{ij}\).

$$Y_{ijk}=\mu+\alpha_i+\beta_j+\gamma_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ijk},\qquad \varepsilon_{ijk}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$$
  • \(\mu\) — grand mean; \(\alpha_i\) — main effect of Factor 1; \(\beta_j\) — main effect of Factor 2;
  • \(\gamma_{ij}\) — interaction effect (the joint effect beyond the two separate main effects).

Total SS decomposes into four pieces: \(\text{SST}=\text{SSF1}+\text{SSF2}+\text{SSF12}+\text{SSE}\), giving three separate \(F\)-tests (Factor 1, Factor 2, Interaction), each = its MS ÷ MSE. (Notes §8.3, Table 8.4.) In R: lm(y ~ A*B) is shorthand for A + B + A:B.

Interaction — the concept the exam loves

🧭 What "interaction" means, physically. Two factors interact when the effect of one depends on the level of the other. Everyday example: caffeine and sleep. Coffee wakes you up (main effect of caffeine); a good night's sleep also helps (main effect of sleep). But coffee's boost is huge when you're exhausted and tiny when you're already well-rested — so the effect of coffee changes depending on how much you slept. That "the benefit of one thing depends on the other" is an interaction. No interaction would mean coffee adds the same fixed lift no matter how you slept — the two effects simply stack. (Recipe example: salt improves any dish a bit, sugar improves dessert — but salt in dessert ruins it: the effect of salt flips sign depending on the dish. That sign-flip is a strong interaction.)

Interaction present = the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other factor. On an interaction plot (mean response vs one factor, a separate line per level of the other):

  • Parallel lines ⇒ NO interaction — the gap between lines is constant, so each factor acts independently.
  • Non-parallel / crossing lines ⇒ possible interaction — the effect of one factor changes across levels of the other.

"Comment on whether there could be an interaction using the plot" is a recurring 2-mark part (2025 Q4(a)). You are only asked to read the picture — say parallel/not-parallel and what that implies. The formal decision comes from the interaction \(F\)-test. (Notes §8.3.4, Fig 8.5.)

If the interaction is significant, do not interpret the main effects in isolation — the main-effect story is misleading because each factor's effect changes across the other. Report the interaction and, if needed, analyse one factor within a fixed level of the other (R's subset=). (Notes p 175–176.)

Randomization & blocking (notes §8.4)

Designed experiments use randomization to protect against nuisance factors — variables that aren't of interest but influence the response (e.g. plot soil quality, plant location). Randomization is also how the independence assumption is secured.

  • Completely randomized design (CRD): every treatment-to-unit allocation is equally likely. Simple, but by chance a nuisance factor can end up unbalanced across treatments.
  • Randomized block design (RBD): group units into blocks that are internally homogeneous with respect to the nuisance factor (e.g. each row = a block), then randomize treatments within each block so every block gets each treatment equally often. This removes block-to-block variability from the error term ⇒ smaller MSE ⇒ more powerful test. A block is analysed as a second factor in a two-way ANOVA (see notes Exercise 4: fertilizer × block).
Balanced vs unbalanced. Equal cell counts = balanced. When balanced, the Type I/II/III sums of squares all agree, so the additivity SST = ΣSS holds exactly — which is why the exam's table-completion questions specify a balanced design. Unbalanced data needs car::Anova (out of scope). (Notes p 177.)

Multiple comparisons — which groups differ? (notes §8.5)

A significant ANOVA says "not all means are equal" but not which pairs differ. Follow up with pairwise tests, but control the inflated Type-I error. The course uses the Bonferroni procedure: for \(m\) comparisons, test each at \(\alpha/m\) — equivalently, R multiplies each pairwise P-value by \(m\) (capped at 1) and you compare to the ordinary \(\alpha\).

🧭 Why divide \(\alpha\) by \(m\)? Each test you run has its own \(\alpha\) chance of a false alarm. Run \(m\) tests and the false alarms accumulate — roughly \(m\times\alpha\) chance that at least one of them fires by luck (the same error-inflation that rules out doing many \(t\)-tests). Bonferroni's fix is deliberately blunt: make each individual test \(m\) times stricter (threshold \(\alpha/m\)) so that the \(m\) risks, added back up, land at your intended overall \(\alpha\). It is the price of asking several questions at once and still wanting the whole family to be trustworthy at 5%.

R's pairwise.t.test(..., p.adj="bonferroni") prints a matrix of adjusted P-values. Reading rule: an entry \(<\alpha\) ⇒ those two groups differ significantly; \(\ge\alpha\) (often shown as 1.000) ⇒ no significant difference. Interpreting this output was examined in 2025 Q4(e) and 2023 Q5(d).

Assumptions & how to check them from plots

The ANOVA \(F\)-test requires the errors \(\varepsilon\) to be independent, normally distributed, with constant variance \(\sigma^2\) (homoscedasticity) across groups.

AssumptionHow to assess
Normality (within groups)Normal Q–Q plot of residuals roughly on the straight line; group histograms/boxplots roughly symmetric.
Equal varianceResiduals-vs-fitted shows an even band (no funnel); group SDs / boxplot spreads similar. Rule of thumb: largest group SD ≲ 2× smallest.
IndependenceNot visible in a plot — argue it from the design (proper randomization, no repeated measures on the same unit).
\(R^2=\dfrac{\text{SSF}}{\text{SST}}=1-\dfrac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}\) is the proportion of total variation in the response explained by the factor(s) — a routine follow-up part (2024 Q4(f), 2022-style). It is not an assumption; it's an effect-size summary.

§2 · Key formulas & when to use them

Everything here is on the provided formula sheet (STAT1301_formula.pdf) — marks come from recognising which line to use and executing it, not recall.

Degrees of freedom (the skeleton of every ANOVA table)

One-way (\(d\) levels, \(n\) total): Factor \(=d-1\); Error \(=n-d\); Total \(=n-1\).
Two-way (\(d_1\times d_2\), \(n\) total): F1 \(=d_1-1\); F2 \(=d_2-1\); Interaction \(=(d_1-1)(d_2-1)\); Error \(=n-d_1d_2\); Total \(=n-1\).
Any "complete the ANOVA table" / "find the X cells" question. Fill DF first — every other cell depends on it.

Mean squares, F, and SS additivity

$$\text{MS}=\frac{\text{SS}}{\text{DF}}\qquad F=\frac{\text{MS}_{\text{effect}}}{\text{MSE}}\qquad \text{SST}=\sum(\text{effect SS})+\text{SSE}$$ Rearrange as needed: \(\text{SS}=\text{MS}\times\text{DF}\); \(\text{MS}_{\text{effect}}=F\times\text{MSE}\); \(\text{SSE}=\text{SST}-\sum(\text{effect SS})\).
Filling any missing SS/MS/F cell. These three identities + the DF skeleton solve every table-completion question.

The table-completion algorithm (drill this — guaranteed marks)

  1. DF column first from the design (counts of levels & total \(n\)). Check it sums to \(n-1\).
  2. Fill SS via additivity: the one missing SS = Total − (all other SS). (Balanced ⇒ exact.)
  3. MS = SS/DF for each source (never for Total).
  4. \(F\) = MS\(_{\text{effect}}\)/MSE for each effect row.
  5. Back-solve if given an MS or \(F\): \(\text{SS}=\text{MS}\cdot\text{DF}\), or \(\text{MS}_{\text{eff}}=F\cdot\text{MSE}\).
  6. P-value = \(\mathbb P(F>f)\) from the \(F\)-table with (numerator DF, error DF).

Test statistic distribution under \(H_0\)

One-way: \(F=\dfrac{\text{MSF}}{\text{MSE}}\sim F(d-1,\ n-d)\).
Two-way effect: \(F=\dfrac{\text{MS}_{\text{effect}}}{\text{MSE}}\sim F(\text{DF}_{\text{effect}},\ n-d_1d_2)\).
"State the distribution of the \(F\)-statistic under \(H_0\)" — quote the family AND both DF. Right-one-sided: reject if \(f>F_{\alpha}(\text{df}_1,\text{df}_2)\) or \(\mathbb P(F>f)<\alpha\).

Effect size & pooled spread

$$R^2=\frac{\text{SSF}}{\text{SST}}=1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}\qquad s_{\text{pooled}}=\sqrt{\text{MSE}}$$
"What is the \(R^2\)?" / "pooled standard deviation?" — both fall straight out of a completed table.

Bonferroni multiple comparisons

For \(m\) pairwise comparisons: test each at \(\alpha_{\text{adj}}=\alpha/m\); equivalently reject a pair if its adjusted P-value \(<\alpha\). With \(d\) groups, \(m=\binom d2\).
After a significant ANOVA, "which treatments differ?" Read the adjusted P-value matrix: entry \(<\alpha\) ⇒ significant difference.

§4 · Exam question types (mined from past papers)

Type A — "Complete the ANOVA table / find the X cells" ⭐ every year

Looks like: a table with DF/SS/MS/F cells replaced by X or blanks, plus the study design and 1–2 given numbers. Mark range 3–4 marks.

Recipe:
  1. DF column from design (levels & \(n\)); check sums to \(n-1\).
  2. Fill the one missing SS via additivity (SST = ΣSS).
  3. MS = SS/DF; \(F\) = MS\(_{\text{eff}}\)/MSE.
  4. Back-solve any cell from a given MS or \(F\).

Seen in: 2025 Q4(b) two-way; 2024 Q4(a) one-way; 2023 Q5(a) one-way; notes Ex 1(c), 2(a), 3(c), 4(b), 5(b).

Type B — "Comment on whether there could be an interaction using the plot"

Looks like: one/two interaction plots. Mark range 2 marks.

Recipe: state whether the lines are (roughly) parallel or not. Parallel ⇒ little/no evidence of interaction (each factor acts independently). Non-parallel/crossing ⇒ possible interaction (effect of one factor depends on the other). Say what it means in context.

Seen in: 2025 Q4(a); drawing the plot in notes Ex 5(a).

Type C — "State the hypotheses & the distribution of \(F\) under \(H_0\)"

Looks like: write \(H_0/H_1\) in symbols and give the \(F\) distribution. Mark range 2–4 marks.

Templates:
  • One-way: \(H_0:\mu_1=\cdots=\mu_d\) vs \(H_1:\) not all equal; \(F\sim F(d-1,n-d)\).
  • Two-way main effect: \(H_0:\alpha_i=0\ \forall i\) (or \(\beta_j=0\)); \(F\sim F(d_1-1,\ n-d_1d_2)\).
  • Interaction: \(H_0:\gamma_{ij}=0\ \forall i,j\) vs \(H_1:\) some \(\gamma_{ij}\neq0\); \(F\sim F((d_1-1)(d_2-1),\ n-d_1d_2)\).
Always define \(\mu_i\) etc. in words ("mean response for level \(i\)").

Seen in: 2025 Q4(c); 2024 Q4(c); 2023 Q5(b); notes Ex 1(b), 3(b).

Type D — "State the assumptions & assess their validity from the plots"

Looks like: residuals-vs-fitted + Normal Q–Q (and sometimes group boxplots/SD table). Mark range 4 marks.

Recipe: name all three — normality, equal variance, independence. Q–Q on the line ⇒ normal; even band in residuals-vs-fitted (and comparable group SDs) ⇒ equal variance; randomization ⇒ independence. Give a one-line verdict per assumption referring to the specific plot.

Seen in: 2025 Q4(d); 2024 Q4(b); 2023 Q5(c); notes Ex 3(e), 5(e).

Type E — "Which treatments differ?" (multiple comparisons output)

Looks like: a Bonferroni pairwise P-value matrix. Mark range 2–3 marks.

Recipe: scan each cell; entry \(<\alpha\) ⇒ that pair differs significantly; \(\ge\alpha\) (e.g. 1.000) ⇒ no significant difference. Translate into context and, if a claim is given ("as effective as"), state whether the data support it.

Seen in: 2025 Q4(e); 2023 Q5(d); notes Ex 6.

Type F — Conceptual / follow-up parts

  • "Why not multiple \(t\)-tests?" → inflated Type-I error; ANOVA gives one controlled test, follow with Bonferroni. (2024 Q4(e), 2 marks.)
  • "\(R^2\) and interpret" → \(R^2=\text{SSF}/\text{SST}\); proportion of variation explained by the factor. (2024 Q4(f).)
  • "Pooled standard deviation" → \(\sqrt{\text{MSE}}\). (Notes Ex 2(b), 3(f).)
  • "How would you enter the data for R?" → stacked / long format: one column for the numeric response, one column for the factor level; not one column per group. (Notes Ex 1(a).)
  • "What does the block P-value tell you?" → whether blocking mattered (a significant block effect ⇒ the nuisance factor was worth removing). (Notes Ex 4(c).)

§6 · Traps & marks lost

Interaction DF is a product, not a sum. It is \((d_1-1)(d_2-1)\), not \((d_1-1)+(d_2-1)\). For a \(3\times2\): \(2\times1=2\). Getting this wrong cascades into every MS and \(F\).
Error DF in two-way is \(n-d_1d_2\) (total minus number of cells), not \(n-d_1-d_2\). For 2025 Q4: \(60-6=54\), not \(60-5\).
Never compute MS for the Total row. There is no "MS Total". Only effect rows and the error row get an MS.
All two-way \(F\)-tests share MSE as the denominator. Don't divide the interaction MS by the Factor-1 MS — divide every effect MS by MSE.
Interaction significant ⇒ don't interpret main effects alone. Report the interaction; if you must, examine one factor within a fixed level of the other. A common lost mark is confidently stating a main-effect direction when the interaction is significant.
ANOVA is right-one-sided. The P-value is \(\mathbb P(F>f)\) only — never double it, never use a left tail. \(F\) is always \(\ge0\).
Reading Bonferroni output backwards. A small adjusted P-value means the pair differs. An entry of 1.000 means "no difference," not "identical/perfect."
Independence is not a plot. You cannot "see" it in a Q–Q or residual plot — justify it from the randomized design. Stating "the Q–Q plot shows independence" loses the mark.
Sig figs. The rubric demands ≥3 significant figures. Carry MSE unrounded (e.g. \(0.078815\)) through to \(F\); round only the final reported value. Rounding MSE early can turn \(F=1.231\) into a visibly wrong number.
Wrong data format. "How to enter for R" wants long format (response column + factor column). A column-per-group table is the wrong format for ANOVA (notes §8.2.4).
Equal-variance assumption is part of the model. Don't forget it — one-way and two-way ANOVA both assume a single common \(\sigma^2\). Check it with residual spread / group SDs.

§7 · Self-check

Q1. A one-way ANOVA has 5 groups of 8 observations each. Give all the DF.

\(d=5,\ n=40\). Factor \(=d-1=4\); Error \(=n-d=35\); Total \(=n-1=39\). (Check \(4+35=39\).)

Q2. A balanced \(4\times3\) two-way design has 5 replicates per cell. Give the interaction DF and the error DF.

\(d_1=4,\ d_2=3,\ n=4\times3\times5=60\). Interaction \(=(4-1)(3-1)=6\); Error \(=n-d_1d_2=60-12=48\). Total \(=59\) (check \(3+2+6+48=59\)).

Q3. One-way table: SST = 90, SSF = 30, \(d=3\), \(n=30\). Find \(F\) and \(R^2\).

SSE \(=90-30=60\). MSF \(=30/2=15\); MSE \(=60/27=2.222\); \(F=15/2.222=6.75\). \(R^2=30/90=0.333\) — the factor explains 33% of the variation.

Q4. Under \(H_0\), what is the distribution of the interaction \(F\) in a balanced \(3\times2\) design with 10 per cell?

\(F\sim F\big((3-1)(2-1),\ 60-6\big)=F(2,54)\). (This is exactly 2025 Q4's interaction test.)

Q5. An interaction plot shows two clearly crossing lines. What do you conclude, and what must you avoid?

Crossing (non-parallel) lines ⇒ evidence of a possible interaction: the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. You must not interpret either main effect as a single overall number — its meaning changes across the other factor's levels.

Q6. A Bonferroni matrix shows P-values A–B = 1.000, A–C = 0.012, B–C = 0.048 (\(\alpha=0.05\)). Which groups differ?

A–C (0.012) and B–C (0.048) are both \(<0.05\) ⇒ C differs significantly from both A and B. A–B (1.000) ⇒ A and B do not differ. So C stands apart while A and B are indistinguishable.

Q7. Why can't you just run all pairwise \(t\)-tests instead of ANOVA?

Each test risks a Type-I error at \(\alpha\); running many inflates the overall false-positive rate far above \(\alpha\). ANOVA gives one omnibus test at controlled \(\alpha\); only after it rejects do you run adjusted (Bonferroni) pairwise comparisons.

Q8. Given SSE = 4.256 on 54 DF, what is the pooled standard deviation?

MSE \(=4.256/54=0.078815\); pooled SD \(=\sqrt{0.078815}=0.281\). (This is the shared denominator's root from 2025 Q4.)

A one-way ANOVA on \(d=3\) groups, \(n=30\), reports \(\text{MSE}=4.00\) and \(F=5.50\) but the SS column is smudged. Reconstruct SSF, SSE, SST, and state the test conclusion at 5%.

Topic 3 — Reading interaction plots

These plots aren't drawn here — instead the pattern is described in words so you practise the exam skill of turning "what the lines do" into "what it means". The lines plot mean response (y) against the levels of one factor, with a separate line for each level of the other factor.

📖 Learn from these

WE3.1 — Parallel lines (car fuel economy) → no interaction

Plot described. Mean fuel economy (km/L) on the y-axis against tyre pressure (Low / High) on the x-axis; two lines, one for Eco driving mode and one for Sport. Both lines slope gently upward from Low to High pressure, and the Eco line sits about 4 km/L above the Sport line at both pressures — the vertical gap between the lines is essentially constant.

Interpretation. The lines are (near) parallel ⇒ little/no evidence of an interaction. The benefit of higher tyre pressure is about the same whether you drive in Eco or Sport, and the Eco-vs-Sport gap is about the same at either pressure. The two factors appear to act independently (their effects add). A formal interaction \(F\)-test would confirm this.

WE3.2 — Crossing lines (crop yield) → interaction present

Plot described. Mean crop yield (t/ha) against irrigation (Drip / Flood); one line for variety Alpha, one for variety Omega. Under Drip, Alpha is well above Omega; under Flood, the lines cross over so Omega ends up above Alpha.

Interpretation. The lines cross (strongly non-parallel) ⇒ clear visual evidence of an interaction: which variety yields more depends on the irrigation method — Alpha wins under Drip, Omega wins under Flood. You must not summarise "the best variety" as a single answer; the variety effect reverses across irrigation. Confirm with the interaction \(F\)-test, and if significant, analyse each variety within a fixed irrigation type.

WARM-UP · YT3.1 — What does "parallel" mean? [1 mark]

On an interaction plot the two lines are almost perfectly parallel. In one sentence, what does that indicate?

Solution. Parallel lines indicate no (or negligible) interaction — the effect of one factor is the same across all levels of the other, so the factors act independently.

STANDARD · YT3.2 — One flat, one steep line [2 marks]

Mean recovery time (days) against physiotherapy (None / Daily) for two age groups: the Under-40 line is almost flat, while the Over-60 line drops steeply from None to Daily. The lines do not cross but clearly fan apart. Comment on interaction.

Solution. The lines are not parallel (one flat, one steep) ⇒ possible interaction: physiotherapy barely changes recovery time for the under-40s but shortens it substantially for the over-60s, so the effect of physiotherapy depends on age group. (Non-parallel without crossing still signals interaction.) A formal interaction \(F\)-test is needed to confirm.

EXAM-LEVEL · YT3.3 — Reconcile plot with the F-test [3 marks]

An analyst says "the interaction lines look a bit non-parallel, so there must be an interaction." The interaction \(F\)-test returns \(F(2,24)=1.24\), P \(=0.31\). Explain who is right and how to word the conclusion.

Solution. The plot only shows sample means, which will rarely be exactly parallel even when no true interaction exists — mild non-parallelism can easily be random scatter. The formal test decides: P \(=0.31\gt0.05\), so we do not reject \(H_0:\gamma_{ij}=0\) — there is no statistically significant interaction. The visual "wobble" is not beyond what chance produces, so the analyst is over-reading the picture; the \(F\)-test is the arbiter.

STRETCH · YT3.4 — Significant interaction: what you may and may not say [3 marks]

In a fertiliser-brand × soil-type study on plant height, the interaction is significant (P \(=0.004\)) and the plot lines cross. A colleague reports "Brand C is best overall." Why is that statement unsafe, and what should be reported instead?

Solution. A significant interaction means each brand's effect depends on soil type; because the lines cross, the best brand in one soil may be worst in another, so there is no single "best overall" brand — a main-effect (marginal) statement averages over soils and is misleading. Report the interaction itself, then analyse brands within each soil type separately (e.g. R's subset=), quoting the best brand per soil.

Topic 4 — Assessing the assumptions

The three assumptions on the errors: independence, normality, constant variance. Diagnostic tools: Normal Q–Q plot (normality), residuals-vs-fitted band / group SDs (equal variance), and the design (independence — never a plot).

📖 Learn from these

WE4.1 — All three look fine (bakery study)

Plots described. Residuals-vs-fitted shows a roughly even horizontal band of points with no funnel or curve; the Normal Q–Q plot has points lying close to the reference line with only mild wiggle at the extremes; the four group SDs are \(1.9, 2.1, 2.3, 2.0\) (largest/smallest \(=1.2\), well under 2). Participants/cakes were randomly assigned to ovens.

Verdict. Constant variance — even band, similar SDs (ratio \(1.2\lt2\)) ⇒ OK. Normality — Q–Q points near the line ⇒ reasonable. Independence — random assignment, no repeated measures on the same unit ⇒ plausible. All three assumptions appear satisfied, so the ANOVA \(F\)-test is trustworthy.

WE4.2 — A funnel + unequal SDs → equal-variance fails

Plots described. Residuals-vs-fitted fans out — small scatter at low fitted values widening to large scatter at high fitted values (a clear funnel); group SDs are \(0.8, 1.5, 3.4\) (largest/smallest \(=4.3\)). Q–Q plot is close to the line.

Verdict. Constant variance is violated: the funnel and the SD ratio \(4.3\) (far above the ≈2 rule of thumb) show spread grows with the mean. Normality looks fine (Q–Q on the line), and independence is argued from the design. Because equal variance fails, the standard \(F\)-test is unreliable — a variance-stabilising transformation (e.g. \(\log\) or \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) of the response) should be considered before trusting the ANOVA.

STRETCH · YT4.4 — Why independence isn't in the plots [3 marks]

A student writes: "The residuals-vs-fitted plot shows no pattern, so the observations are independent." Explain what's wrong and how independence should actually be justified.

Solution. A residual plot can reveal non-constant variance or the wrong mean model, but it cannot demonstrate independence — dependent data (e.g. repeated measures on the same subject, or time-ordered readings) can still produce a patternless-looking scatter. Independence must be argued from the study design: proper randomisation of units to treatments and no repeated/clustered measurements on the same unit. Claiming "the plot shows independence" loses the mark.

Topic 5 — Reading Bonferroni multiple-comparison output

Rule: each printed value is an adjusted P-value. Entry \(\lt\alpha\) ⇒ that pair differs significantly; entry \(\ge\alpha\) (often \(1.000\)) ⇒ no significant difference. With \(d\) groups there are \(m=\binom{d}{2}\) comparisons.

📖 Learn from these

WE5.1 — Four coffee grinders (which differ?)

Context. After a significant ANOVA on grind uniformity across 4 grinders (Blade, Burr-flat, Burr-conical, Hand), Bonferroni pairwise output (\(\alpha=0.05\)):

Blade Burr-flat Burr-conical Burr-flat 0.004 - - Burr-conical 0.001 1.000 - Hand 1.000 0.021 0.008

Read it. Entries \(\lt0.05\): Blade–Burr-flat (0.004), Blade–Burr-conical (0.001), Hand–Burr-flat (0.021), Hand–Burr-conical (0.008). Entries \(\ge0.05\): Blade–Hand (1.000), Burr-flat–Burr-conical (1.000).

Conclusion in context. The two burr grinders differ significantly from both Blade and Hand grinders, but not from each other; Blade and Hand also do not differ. So the grinders split into two indistinguishable camps — {Blade, Hand} vs {Burr-flat, Burr-conical} — with a real difference in mean grind uniformity between the camps. (\(m=\binom42=6\) comparisons.)

WE5.2 — Three protein powders + evaluating a marketing claim

Context. ANOVA on muscle-recovery score for 3 powders (Whey, Pea, Blend) is significant; the maker claims "our Pea powder works just as well as Whey." Bonferroni output (\(\alpha=0.05\)):

Whey Pea Pea 0.013 - Blend 1.000 0.026

Read it. Whey–Pea \(=0.013\lt0.05\) ⇒ significant difference; Whey–Blend \(=1.000\) ⇒ no difference; Pea–Blend \(=0.026\lt0.05\) ⇒ significant difference.

Evaluate the claim. Because Whey and Pea differ significantly (adjusted P \(=0.013\)), the data do not support the "just as well as Whey" claim — Pea's mean recovery score is significantly different from Whey's (and Pea also differs from Blend, while Whey and Blend are indistinguishable).

STRETCH · YT5.4 — Why adjust, and to what threshold? [3 marks]

With 5 groups, an analyst plans all pairwise comparisons at \(\alpha=0.05\) without adjustment. How many comparisons is that, why is it a problem, and what per-comparison threshold does Bonferroni use?

Solution. \(m=\binom52=10\) comparisons. Running 10 independent tests each at \(0.05\) inflates the family-wise Type-I error to roughly \(1-(0.95)^{10}\approx0.40\) — about a 40% chance of at least one false positive. Bonferroni controls this by testing each comparison at \(\alpha_{\text{adj}}=\alpha/m=0.05/10=0.005\) (equivalently multiplying each raw P-value by 10 and comparing to \(0.05\)), keeping the overall error at \(\le0.05\).

Topic 6 — \(R^2\) and conceptual questions

📖 Learn from these

WE6.1 — \(R^2\) and pooled SD from a completed table

Given a one-way table: Factor SS \(=53.80\), Error SS \(=245.00\), Total SS \(=298.80\), Error DF \(=56\).

\(R^2=\dfrac{\text{SSF}}{\text{SST}}=\dfrac{53.80}{298.80}=0.180\) — the factor explains \(18.0\%\) of the total variation in the response; the remaining \(82\%\) is within-group (unexplained) noise. Equivalently \(R^2=1-\text{SSE}/\text{SST}=1-245.00/298.80=0.180\) ✓.

Pooled SD \(=\sqrt{\text{MSE}}=\sqrt{245.00/56}=\sqrt{4.375}=2.09\). This single number estimates the common within-group standard deviation \(\sigma\).

A statistically significant factor can still have a small \(R^2\): "significant" answers "is there any effect?", while \(R^2\) answers "how much of the spread does it explain?" — different questions.
WE6.2 — What do MSF and MSE each estimate?

Question style. "In a one-way ANOVA, what does each mean square estimate, and why is \(F\approx1\) under \(H_0\)?"

Answer. MSE always estimates the common error variance \(\sigma^2\) — it pools the within-group scatter and is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\) whether or not \(H_0\) is true. MSF estimates \(\sigma^2\) plus a non-negative term driven by how far the true group means spread apart; under \(H_0:\mu_1=\dots=\mu_d\) that extra term is \(0\), so MSF also estimates \(\sigma^2\). Hence under \(H_0\) both numerator and denominator estimate the same \(\sigma^2\) and \(F=\text{MSF}/\text{MSE}\approx1\); when the means genuinely differ, MSF is inflated and \(F\) grows — which is why ANOVA is a right-one-sided test.

STANDARD · YT6.2 — Why not just run all the t-tests? [2 marks]

A classmate wants to compare 5 diets by running a \(t\)-test on every pair instead of one ANOVA. In 1–2 sentences, why is that flawed and what is the correct approach?

Solution. Running all \(\binom52=10\) pairwise \(t\)-tests inflates the overall Type-I error rate far above \(5\%\) (many chances for a false positive). The correct approach is a single ANOVA \(F\)-test at controlled \(\alpha\); only if it rejects do you follow up with adjusted (e.g. Bonferroni) pairwise comparisons.

EXAM-LEVEL · YT6.3 — \(R^2\), pooled SD, and interpretation together [4 marks]

From a completed one-way table: Factor SS \(=18.62\), Error SS \(=77.88\), Error DF \(=33\), Total SS \(=96.50\). Report \(R^2\), the pooled standard deviation, and interpret both in context (response = extraction yield %).

Solution. \(R^2=18.62/96.50=0.193\) ⇒ roast level explains \(19.3\%\) of the variation in extraction yield; the other \(80.7\%\) is shot-to-shot noise within roasts. Pooled SD \(=\sqrt{77.88/33}=\sqrt{2.360}=1.54\%\) ⇒ within any roast, individual yields scatter around their roast mean with a standard deviation of about \(1.5\) percentage points. So roast has a real but modest influence relative to the within-roast variability.

STRETCH · YT6.4 — Why is the P-value a right tail only? [3 marks]

Explain, using what MSF and MSE estimate, why the ANOVA P-value is \(\mathbb P(F\gt f)\) (right tail) and never two-sided.

Solution. MSE estimates \(\sigma^2\) regardless of \(H_0\); MSF estimates \(\sigma^2\) plus a non-negative quantity that grows only when the group means differ. So departures from \(H_0\) can push \(F\) only upward — a true difference never makes \(F\) systematically less than 1. Evidence against \(H_0\) therefore lives entirely in the upper tail, so the P-value is \(\mathbb P(F\gt f)\); a small \(F\) (near 0) just means the group means are unusually close, which is not evidence against equality. Hence the test is right-one-sided and the P-value is never doubled.

1 · Concepts in depth

🧭 Intuition first — what regression is doing

Picture a scatterplot: a cloud of points, one dot per subject, with a predictor \(x\) across the bottom and a response \(y\) up the side (e.g. eruption duration vs waiting time). If the cloud trends, we can summarise it with a single straight line drawn through the middle. Regression is the recipe for choosing that line and then reasoning about it carefully — how steep is it, could the trend just be luck, and how good are its predictions?

That's the whole chapter in one sentence. Everything below is machinery for four jobs: (1) pick the line (least squares), (2) put an honest uncertainty on its slope (standard errors and \(t\)-tests), (3) measure how much of the scatter it explains (\(R^2\)), and (4) check the line was even a fair thing to draw (residual diagnostics).

New to sampling distributions, standard error, and where the \(t\), \(\chi^2\) and \(F\) shapes and quantile notation \(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\) come from? That underlying machinery is taught in one place — see 📐 Foundations. This page leans on it whenever a slope gets a standard error or a \(t\)-value.

The model: what "linear regression" actually assumes

Simple linear regression models a response \(Y\) (numerical) as a straight-line function of one explanatory variable \(x\), plus random noise. The data-generating model (notes Def. 9.1, formula sheet item 83) is

$$Y_i = \underbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i}_{\text{signal (the true line)}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_i}_{\text{noise}},\qquad \varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).$$

In words: every observation = signal + noise. The signal \(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) is the straight-line trend that every subject shares. The noise \(\varepsilon_i\) is that subject's personal vertical wobble off the line — everything about \(y\) that \(x\) doesn't explain (measurement error, other omitted causes, individuality). \(\varepsilon_i\) is literally the vertical gap between the point and the true line; \(\sigma\) is the typical size of that gap.

Two lines, never confuse them. \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) describe the true population line — a fixed fact about the world we can never see, because we never have everyone. Once we collect a sample and fit, we get \(\widehat\beta_0,\widehat\beta_1\) (also written \(b_0,b_1\)): our best estimate of that line from this particular data. Greek \(\beta\) = the unknown truth; hat \(\widehat\beta\) = our sample guess at it. All the standard errors and \(t\)-tests exist to bridge that gap.

Read the model carefully — it contains four separate assumptions hiding in one line, and part (d) of every regression question makes you check them:

  • Linearity — the mean of \(Y\) really is a straight line in \(x\): \(\mathbb E(Y_i)=\beta_0+\beta_1 x_i\). (The errors have mean 0.)
  • Constant variance (homoscedasticity) — every \(\varepsilon_i\) has the same spread \(\sigma^2\), regardless of \(x\).
  • Normality — the errors are normally distributed. (This is what makes the \(t\)-tests and intervals exact.)
  • Independence — the errors don't influence each other.

There are three unknown parameters: the intercept \(\beta_0\), the slope \(\beta_1\), and the error variance \(\sigma^2\). The whole chapter is about estimating these and quantifying our uncertainty about them.

Least squares: where \(\widehat\beta_0,\widehat\beta_1\) come from

We can't see the true line, so we fit the line \(\widehat y=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x\) that makes the residuals \(e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i\) (the vertical misses) as small as possible — specifically we minimise the sum of squared residuals \(\text{SSE}=\sum e_i^2\). Calculus gives the closed form (Thm 9.1):

Why this rule? A residual is one point's vertical gap from the fitted line, \(e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i\) (observed minus predicted). Vertical, because we're predicting \(y\) from \(x\), so the error we care about is in \(y\). We square the misses before adding for two reasons: squaring stops positive misses (points above the line) cancelling negative ones (points below), and it punishes one big miss far more than several small ones — so least squares hunts for the line that leaves no point wildly stranded. "Fitting the line" literally means: slide and tilt a line until that total squared miss is as small as it can possibly be.
$$\widehat\beta_1=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad \widehat\beta_0=\bar y-\widehat\beta_1\bar x.$$

You will almost never compute these by hand in the exam — R prints them in the Estimate column. But notice two things worth marks: the fitted line always passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\) (from the \(\widehat\beta_0\) formula), and the slope is a scaled version of the covariance between \(x\) and \(y\).

These are estimates (fixed numbers from your data). Capitalise them into \(\widehat\beta_0,\widehat\beta_1\) as random estimators and you can talk about their sampling distribution — which is exactly what standard errors and \(t\)-tests use.

The sampling distribution — the engine behind every \(t\)-value

Why does a slope even have a standard error? Imagine drawing a fresh sample of the same size from the same population. The points would land in slightly different spots, so the least-squares line would tilt slightly differently, so you'd get a slightly different \(\widehat\beta_1\). Repeat forever and \(\widehat\beta_1\) traces out a whole distribution of values centred on the true \(\beta_1\) — its sampling distribution. The standard error is just the standard deviation of that distribution: how much your one slope estimate would jump around from sample to sample. This is the exact same idea as the standard error of a sample mean — see 📐 Foundations for the floor. A slope is "significant" precisely when it sits too many standard errors away from 0 to be luck.

Because each estimator is a linear combination of the normal \(Y_i\)'s, both are themselves normal and unbiased (Thm 9.2): \(\mathbb E(\widehat\beta_1)=\beta_1\). Their variances are

$$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta_0)=\sigma^2\!\left(\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}\right).$$

The standard error of a coefficient is the square root of its estimated variance (using \(\widehat\sigma^2=\text{MSE}\) in place of \(\sigma^2\)). That is the number printed in the Std. Error column, and

$$\boxed{\;t=\frac{\widehat\beta_1}{\operatorname{se}(\widehat\beta_1)}\;}\quad\text{(formula 9.11)}$$

is the ratio R prints as t value. Big t = the estimate is many standard errors away from 0 = strong evidence the slope isn't 0. Notice \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta_1)\) shrinks as (i) \(n\) grows and (ii) the \(x\)'s spread out more — wider-spread predictors give a more precisely-estimated slope.

What is \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) really saying? A slope of zero is a flat line: whatever \(x\) you plug in, the prediction for \(y\) never changes. So \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) is the claim "\(x\) tells you nothing about \(y\) — knowing the eruption duration doesn't help you predict the waiting time at all." Rejecting \(H_0\) (small \(p\)-value) is how you earn the right to say there is a real linear relationship. That is exactly what "is there a significant linear relationship?" is asking.

Estimating \(\sigma^2\): MSE and residual standard error

We can't see the true errors, but the residuals estimate them. The mean squared error

$$\widehat\sigma^2=\text{MSE}=\frac{\text{SSE}}{n-2}=\frac1{n-2}\sum e_i^2$$

is the unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\). We divide by \(n-2\) (not \(n-1\)) because we used two degrees of freedom to estimate \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\). R prints \(\widehat\sigma=\sqrt{\text{MSE}}\) as the Residual standard error, together with its \(n-2\) degrees of freedom — a free way to recover the sample size: \(n = \text{df}+2\) for simple regression. (For multiple regression with \(p\) predictors, df \(=n-(p+1)\), so \(n=\text{df}+p+1\).)

\(R^2\): the "proportion of variability explained"

Compare the fitted line against the dumb "null" model that ignores \(x\) and just predicts \(\bar y\). The total spread of \(y\) about \(\bar y\) is \(\text{SST}=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\); what's left after fitting the line is \(\text{SSE}=\sum(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2\). Then (formula 9.13 / item 82)

$$R^2=1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}=\frac{\text{SST}-\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}.$$

Interpretation you write in the exam: "\(R^2=0.81\) means 81% of the variability in [response] is explained by the linear relationship with [predictor]." For simple regression only, \(R^2=r^2\) — the square of the sample correlation. Adjusted \(R^2\) penalises adding useless predictors; use it to compare models of different sizes, but interpret plain \(R^2\) as "variance explained".

CI for the mean response vs prediction interval for a new observation — the distinction

Both are centred on the same fitted value \(\widehat y=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x\), but they answer different questions and one is always wider.

Confidence interval — for the MEAN \(\mathbb E(Y)\) at \(x\): $$\widehat y\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\widehat\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(x-\bar x)^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}}$$ "Where does the average response for this \(x\) lie?" Only one source of error: uncertainty in the line.
Prediction interval — for a NEW \(Y\) at \(x\): $$\widehat y\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\widehat\sigma\sqrt{1+\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(x-\bar x)^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}}$$ "Where will one new individual land?" TWO sources: uncertainty in the line plus the individual's own scatter — that's the extra \(+1\).
The \(+1\) under the root is the whole story. It makes the PI wider. As \(n\to\infty\) the CI width shrinks to 0 (we learn the line perfectly), but the PI width shrinks only to \(2\,t\,\widehat\sigma\approx 2(1.96)\sigma\) — the irreducible scatter of individuals never disappears. This exact point was 2025 Q5(e).

Multiple linear regression

Add more predictors (formula sheet item 84):

$$Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{i1}+\dots+\beta_d x_{id}+\varepsilon_i,\qquad \varepsilon_i\overset{\text{iid}}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).$$

Everything transfers, with two upgrades you must say out loud for marks:

  • Each slope is now "adjusted for" the others. "\(\widehat\beta_1=0.53\) means: at fixed weight, a 1 cm increase in shoe size is associated with a 0.53 cm increase in average height." The "holding the others constant" clause is compulsory.
  • Two levels of test. Each row's \(t\)-test asks "does this predictor add anything, given the rest?" The bottom-line F-statistic (Fisher's global test) asks "do all the predictors together explain anything?" — \(H_0:\beta_1=\dots=\beta_d=0\) vs \(H_1:\) at least one \(\beta_j\neq0\).

For a categorical predictor with \(k\) levels, R makes \(k-1\) indicator (dummy) variables and treats the first level (alphabetical) as the reference. A coefficient like race2 = −510.5 means "group 2 is 510.5 units below the reference group, all else equal." This is the Ch 10 "linear model" viewpoint: regression and ANOVA are the same machine \(\mathbf Y=\mathcal X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) (item 85) with different design matrices \(\mathcal X\).

Ch 10 extras — residual analysis & transformations

Residual analysis is how we check the four assumptions after fitting (there is no other way — we never see the true errors, only the residuals \(e_i\)). Two diagnostic plots do almost all the work:

  • Residuals vs Fitted — a shapeless horizontal band around 0 is good. A curve/U-shape ⇒ linearity is violated. A funnel/megaphone (spread growing) ⇒ non-constant variance.
  • Normal Q–Q — points on the diagonal ⇒ normal errors. Systematic curving off the line at the ends ⇒ skew / heavy tails.

Transformations are the fix when the residuals reveal non-linearity or a funnel. A curved residuals-vs-fitted plot and data that "fan out" as they grow is the classic cue to model \(\log(Y)\) instead of \(Y\).

A transformation is not fudging the data — it's choosing a better ruler. Re-expressing \(y\) as \(\log y\) (or \(\sqrt y\), \(1/y\)) just measures the same response on a stretched scale. On the right scale a relationship that curved becomes straight, and a spread that fanned out becomes even — so the four straight-line assumptions can finally hold. The data are untouched; you've picked coordinates in which a line is honest. You then fit the ordinary linear model there, and back-transform to talk about the original units.

After fitting \(\ln(\text{score})=\beta_0+\beta_1\text{week}\), the model back-transforms to \(\text{score}=e^{\beta_0}e^{\beta_1\,\text{week}}\) — now the effect is multiplicative: each extra week multiplies the score by \(e^{\beta_1}\), i.e. a \(100(e^{\beta_1}-1)\%\) change (notes §10.5).

  8. 4d Pr(>|t|) \(=\) the two-sided \(P\)-value. \(\lt2\times10^{-16}\) ⇒ reject \(H_0\) ⇒ significant linear relationship.
  9. 5 Signif. codes — *** means \(P\lt0.001\). Handy shorthand but state the actual comparison to \(\alpha\).
  10. 6 Residual standard error \(=\widehat\sigma=\sqrt{\text{MSE}}=5.914\) on \(270\) df ⇒ \(n=270+2=272\). Use \(\widehat\sigma\) in every CI/PI.

Fast facts you can extract with zero calculation: the fitted equation, the sample size (df + 2), \(\widehat\sigma\), \(R^2\), and whether the slope is significant. Everything else is one small arithmetic step from these.

3 · Key formulas & when to use them

Fitted / regression equation $$\widehat y=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x$$
the question says "write down the fitted model / regression equation." Read \(\widehat\beta_0\) and \(\widehat\beta_1\) straight from the Estimate column, round to the requested dp, and define your notation in words.
Residual for an observation $$e=y-\widehat y = y-(\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x)$$
given an observed \((x,y)\) pair and asked "what is the residual?" Plug \(x\) into the fitted line, subtract from the observed \(y\). Sign matters: negative ⇒ the model over-predicted.
\(t\)-test for a slope \(\;H_0:\beta_1=0\) vs \(H_1:\beta_1\neq0\) $$t=\frac{\widehat\beta_1}{\operatorname{se}(\widehat\beta_1)}\sim t_{\,n-2}\ \text{under }H_0$$
"is there a significant linear relationship?" Read t value and Pr(>|t|) off the slope row. If \(P\lt\alpha\), reject \(H_0\): significant. (Multiple regression: df \(=n-(p+1)\).)
Confidence interval for a coefficient $$\widehat\beta_1\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\times\operatorname{se}(\widehat\beta_1)$$
"construct/interpret a 95% CI for the slope." Get \(t_{0.975,\,n-2}\) from the \(t\)-table (γ = 0.975 column). If the interval excludes 0, the relationship is significant at that level.
CI for the mean response at \(x\) $$\widehat y\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\widehat\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(x-\bar x)^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}}$$
"confidence interval for the mean / expected / average response when \(x=\dots\)". Narrower interval.
Prediction interval for a new \(Y\) at \(x\) $$\widehat y\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\widehat\sigma\sqrt{1+\tfrac1n+\tfrac{(x-\bar x)^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}}$$
"prediction interval for a new / single / individual observation." Always wider (the extra \(+1\)). In the exam these numbers usually come from R's predict(..., interval="prediction"); know which is which and why the PI is wider.
\(R^2\) — coefficient of determination $$R^2=1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}=\frac{\text{SST}-\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}\quad(\text{item }82)$$
asked to "calculate / interpret \(R^2\)" or given SST and SSE. Interpret as "proportion of variability in \(Y\) explained by the model." Simple regression only: \(R^2=r^2\).
Sample size from the output $$n=\text{df}+2\ \ (\text{simple}),\qquad n=\text{df}+p+1\ \ (p\ \text{predictors})$$
"what is the sample size / how many pairs were used?" Read the df on the Residual standard error line (or the second F df).
Back-transformed log model $$\ln(Y)=\beta_0+\beta_1 x\ \Longrightarrow\ Y=e^{\beta_0}e^{\beta_1 x},\quad \text{effect}=100(e^{\beta_1}-1)\%\ \text{per unit }x$$
a transformation was applied and you must write the fitted model on the original scale or interpret the slope multiplicatively.

4 · Worked examples (real past-paper regression questions)

All numbers below were re-computed in Python from the printed R output to guarantee they match.

Example 1 — Old Faithful geyser (2025 Q5, full walk-through)

Simple linear model fitting waiting time (min) from eruption duration (min), \(n=272\). Output:

Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 33.4744 1.1549 28.98 <2e-16 *** Duration 10.7296 0.3148 34.09 <2e-16 *** Residual standard error: 5.914 on 270 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.8115

(a) Write the fitted equation (2 dp), defining notation.

$$\widehat{\text{Waiting}}=33.47+10.73\,\times\text{Duration}$$

where \(\widehat{\text{Waiting}}\) is the predicted waiting time (min) until the next eruption and Duration is the eruption duration (min).

(b) A duration of 4.8 min gave an observed waiting of 76 min. Find the residual.

$$\widehat y = 33.4744+10.7296(4.8)=84.9765,\qquad e=y-\widehat y=76-84.9765=\boxed{-8.98\ \text{min}}$$

Negative ⇒ the model over-predicted the waiting time for this eruption.

(c) 95% CI for the slope; is the relationship significant?

df \(=270\); take \(t_{0.975}\approx1.97\) (table, df = 200 row):

$$10.7296\pm1.97(0.3148)=10.7296\pm0.620=\boxed{(10.11,\ 11.35)}$$

Interpretation: with 95% confidence, each extra minute of eruption duration adds between 10.11 and 11.35 minutes to average waiting time. The interval excludes 0 (equivalently \(P\lt2\times10^{-16}\)), so there is statistically significant evidence of a positive linear relationship.

(d) Assumptions from the diagnostic plots. Linearity — residuals-vs-fitted shows a flat band around 0, no curve: OK. Constant variance — spread looks even left-to-right, no funnel: OK. Normality — Q–Q points lie close to the line (slight fatness at the top tail only): approximately OK. Independence — assume the eruptions are unrelated over time; no plotted evidence against it.

(e) As \(n\to\infty\): CI vs PI width at a fixed \(x_0\). The 95% CI for the mean width \(\to0\) — with unlimited data the line is pinned down exactly. The 95% prediction interval width does not go to 0; it tends to \(\approx2(1.96)\widehat\sigma\), because a new observation always has its own irreducible scatter \(\sigma\) (the \(+1\) term).

Example 2 — Gray tree frog mating calls (2024 Q5)

Model: Inter-Call interval (s) from Temperature (°C).

Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 7.91299 0.58887 13.438 <2e-16 *** Temperature -0.20108 0.02726 -7.376 1.39e-09 *** Residual standard error: 1.144 on 51 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.5161

(a) Sample size. df \(=51\Rightarrow n=51+2=\boxed{53}\).

(b) Expected inter-call interval at Temperature = 10 °C.

$$\widehat y=7.91299+(-0.20108)(10)=7.91299-2.0108=\boxed{5.90\ \text{s}}$$

(c) 95% CI for the slope; significant? df \(=51\), \(t_{0.975}\approx2.01\):

$$-0.20108\pm2.01(0.02726)=-0.20108\pm0.0548=\boxed{(-0.256,\ -0.146)}$$

The whole interval is below 0 ⇒ significant negative linear relationship: hotter temperatures shorten the interval between calls (each +1 °C cuts the interval by 0.15–0.26 s on average). \(R^2=0.516\): about 52% of the variation in inter-call interval is explained by temperature.

(d) Diagnostics. Residuals vs fitted: flat, patternless band ⇒ linearity and constant variance look fine. Q–Q: near-straight ⇒ normality reasonable. Independence assumed across the sampled frogs.

Example 3 — Pioneer-tree ageing (2023 Q6)

Model estimating tree Age (years) from Diameter (cm).

Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) -1.2026 0.8534 -1.409 0.162 Diameter 0.6372 0.0187 34.067 <2e-16 *** Residual standard error: 5.454 on 106 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.9163

(a) Fitted model & sample size. \(\widehat{\text{Age}}=-1.20+0.64\,\text{Diameter}\); \(n=106+2=108\).

(b) Predict the age of a 50 cm tree, and comment on trusting it.

$$\widehat{\text{Age}}=-1.2026+0.6372(50)=30.66\ \text{years.}$$

Diameter = 50 cm sits inside the observed data range, so this is safe interpolation. Predicting for, say, a 200 cm tree would be extrapolation well beyond the data — do not trust it.

(c) Is the intercept meaningful? \(\widehat\beta_0=-1.20\) is a negative "age" at Diameter = 0 — biologically impossible, and its \(P\)-value (0.162) says it isn't even distinguishable from 0. That's fine: the intercept is just an anchor for the line, not a real prediction, because Diameter = 0 is far outside the data.

(d) Significant relationship? Slope \(t=34.07\), \(P\lt2\times10^{-16}\ll0.05\): overwhelming evidence of a positive linear relationship. \(R^2=0.916\) — 91.6% of the variability in age is explained by diameter, an excellent fit.

Example 4 — Multiple regression & a prediction (student height ~ shoe + weight, notes §9.3)
Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 132.2677 5.2473 25.207 <2e-16 *** shoe 0.5304 0.1962 2.703 0.0081 ** weight 0.3744 0.0572 6.546 2.82e-09 *** Residual standard error: 7.785 on 97 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.4301, F-statistic: 36.61 on 2 and 97 DF

(a) Fitted model. \(\widehat{\text{height}}=132.27+0.53\,\text{shoe}+0.37\,\text{weight}\).

(b) Interpret the shoe slope. "At fixed weight, each extra 1 cm of shoe size is associated with a 0.53 cm increase in average height." The "holding weight constant" phrase is worth the mark.

(c) Predict height for shoe = 30, weight = 75.

$$\widehat y=132.2677+0.5304(30)+0.3744(75)=176.26\ \text{cm}$$

(matches R's predict() fit of 176.2617; CI for the mean \((174.17,178.35)\), PI for a new person \((160.67,191.85)\) — note how much wider the PI is.)

(d) Global significance. Sample size \(n=97+(2+1)=100\). Fisher's global \(F=36.61\) on 2 and 97 df with a tiny \(p\)-value ⇒ at least one predictor is genuinely associated with height. Both individual \(t\)-tests are also significant (\(P=0.008\) and \(P\approx0\)), so both shoe and weight contribute after adjusting for the other.

Example 5 — When a straight line fails: log transformation (notes §10.5)

Test score vs training week showed a curved scatter and a U-shaped residuals-vs-fitted plot — a textbook non-linearity signal. Refitting with \(\log(\text{score})\):

lm(formula = log(score) ~ week) Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 3.01087 0.04404 68.36 <2e-16 *** week 0.50370 0.01544 32.61 <2e-16 *** Residual standard error: 0.2276 on 98 df Multiple R-squared: 0.9156

The residual plot for the log model is now a flat, patternless band ⇒ the transform fixed the linearity/variance problem, and \(R^2\) jumped to 0.916.

Fitted model (log scale): \(\widehat{\ln(\text{score})}=3.011+0.504\,\text{week}\).

Back-transformed (original scale): \(\widehat{\text{score}}=e^{3.011}e^{0.504\,\text{week}}\).

Multiplicative interpretation: each extra week multiplies score by \(e^{0.504}\approx1.66\), i.e. a \(100(e^{0.504}-1)\approx66\%\) increase per week. (On a log model the slope is a percentage change, not an additive one.)

5 · Exam question types (mined from 2023–2025 + notes)

Type A — The full "R-output regression" mega-question (15–18 marks)

Looks like: a context + scatterplot + a boxed summary(lm()), then 4–5 parts. This is the guaranteed one (2023 Q6, 2024 Q5, 2025 Q5).

The standard part-sequence & recipe:
  1. Write the fitted equation — read the two Estimates, round as asked, define notation. (2 marks)
  2. Residual / prediction for a given \(x\) — plug into the line; residual \(=y-\widehat y\). (2 marks)
  3. 95% CI for the slope + significance — \(\widehat\beta_1\pm t_{0.975,n-2}\,\text{se}\); check if it excludes 0; conclude in context. (5 marks)
  4. State & check the four assumptions from the diagnostic plots — 1–2 sentences each. (4–5 marks)
  5. Conceptual add-on — CI vs PI behaviour as \(n\to\infty\), or sample size, or extrapolation. (2–4 marks)
Do part 1 and the sample-size extraction first — they're free marks straight off the printout.

Type B — Predict a new value + CI/PI (2–5 marks)

Looks like: "estimate the mean response for \(x=\dots\)" or R's predict() giving fit / lwr / upr.

  1. Point estimate: \(\widehat y=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x\).
  2. Choose interval type from the wording: "mean/expected/average" ⇒ confidence; "new/single/individual" ⇒ prediction (wider).
  3. Check \(x\) is inside the data range — flag extrapolation if not, and refuse to trust the prediction.

Notes §9.4 Q6 (orange yield): predict Yield for Rainfall = 1000, Food = 500 ⇒ \(99.504+0.017676(1000)+0.040197(500)=137.28\) kg.

Type C — Multiple regression & categorical predictors (Ch 10, 6–15 marks)

Looks like: output with several rows, some named like factor(race)2, sexMale, classLower; sometimes an anova(mod) table with blanks (2023 Q4 Isotopia wages).

  1. Write per-group fitted lines by switching indicators on/off (reference group = all indicators 0).
  2. Interpret each slope "adjusting for the others"; a factor coefficient = difference from the reference group.
  3. Use the global \(F\)-test for "do the predictors jointly matter?"; individual \(t\)-tests for each one.
  4. Fill ANOVA-table blanks with DF/SS/MS/F relationships (see Ch 8).

Type D — Assumption checking / transformation cue (part of the big question)

Looks like: "comment on the residual plots" or "explain why this model is a poor fit / suggest a remedy." A curved residual plot ⇒ recommend a transformation (log for fanning data).

  1. Residuals-vs-fitted: check for a flat band (good) vs curve (non-linear) vs funnel (non-constant variance).
  2. Q–Q plot: check points on the diagonal (normal) vs curving tails (skew).
  3. If violated, name the specific assumption and — if asked — propose \(\log(Y)\), \(\sqrt Y\), or \(1/Y\).

Notes §10.6 Q5 (fish weight ~ length): curved residual plot ⇒ fit \(\log(\text{weight})\); write the back-transformed model.

The flagship table. Match the phrase, execute the method.

When the exam says…Do exactly this
"Using the R output, write down the fitted model / regression equation"Read the two numbers in the Estimate column. Write \(\widehat y=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x\) with them substituted, round to the requested dp, and define \(\widehat y\), \(x\) in words/units. Don't put a hat on \(x\); don't include \(\varepsilon\) in the fitted line.
"Interpret the slope in context""Each 1-[unit] increase in [predictor] is associated with a [\(\widehat\beta_1\)]-[unit] change in the average / predicted [response]." Use the sign; say "average", never "causes".
"Interpret the slope" in a multiple regressionSame template PLUS "holding the other predictors constant / after adjusting for [the others]." That clause is a mark.
"Interpret the intercept"Predicted response when \(x=0\). Immediately check if \(x=0\) is meaningful / in range; if not, say the intercept is not practically interpretable (e.g. 2023 tree \(\widehat\beta_0=-1.2\) years).
"Is there a significant linear relationship?" / "statistical evidence of association?"\(t\)-test on \(\beta_1\): \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) vs \(H_1:\beta_1\neq0\). Read t value & Pr(>|t|) off the slope row; if \(P\lt\alpha\), reject \(H_0\) ⇒ significant. (Or use the global \(F\).)
"Find the residual for this observation"\(e=y-\widehat y\) where \(\widehat y=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x\). Keep the sign; interpret sign as over/under-prediction if asked.
"Predict [response] for \(x=\dots\)"Substitute \(x\) into the fitted line. Report to ≥3 sig figs.
"…and would you trust this prediction?"Check whether \(x\) lies within the observed data range. Inside ⇒ safe interpolation. Outside ⇒ extrapolation, the linear pattern may not hold there — don't trust it.
"Confidence interval for the mean / expected / average response"Use the CI formula (no \(+1\)). It's the narrower interval. Usually R's interval="confidence" lwr/upr.
"Prediction interval for a new / single / individual observation"Use the PI formula (with \(+1\)). It's always wider because it adds the individual's own scatter. R's interval="prediction".
"Which interval is wider and why?"The prediction interval — it carries two sources of variation (uncertainty in the line PLUS the new point's own \(\sigma\)); the CI has only the first.
"How does the interval width behave as \(n\) grows large?"CI (mean) width → 0; PI (new obs) width → \(2t\widehat\sigma\approx2(1.96)\sigma\), i.e. it stays positive because individual scatter never vanishes.
"95% CI for the slope"\(\widehat\beta_1\pm t_{0.975,\,n-2}\times\text{se}(\widehat\beta_1)\). Get \(t\) from the table (0.975 column, df = residual df). Excludes 0 ⇒ significant.
"Comment on the residual plots"Residuals-vs-fitted: flat band ⇒ linearity + constant variance OK; curve ⇒ non-linear; funnel ⇒ non-constant variance. Q–Q: on the line ⇒ normal; curved tails ⇒ non-normal. One clause per assumption.
"State the assumptions of the model"Errors are iid \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\): (1) linearity/zero-mean, (2) constant variance, (3) normality, (4) independence. Then tie each to a plot if asked.
"Interpret \(R^2\)" / "proportion of variability explained""\(R^2\times100\)% of the variability in [response] is explained by the linear relationship with [predictor(s)]." Simple regression: \(R^2=r^2\).
"How many observations / pairs?"\(n=\text{df}+2\) (simple) or df\(+p+1\) (multiple). Read df off the Residual standard error line.
"Read the standard error / df from the output"SE = Std. Error column of the relevant row; residual df on the RSE line; the \(t\)-test uses that df.

A coefficient row named factor(x)2, sexMale, classLowerIndicator for a non-reference level. Its estimate = the difference in mean response between that level and the reference level, all else equal.
"Global / overall significance of the model" (multiple regression)Fisher's \(F\): \(H_0:\beta_1=\dots=\beta_d=0\). Small \(p\)-value ⇒ at least one predictor matters.

7 · Traps & marks lost

Putting \(\varepsilon\) in the fitted line. The model is \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x+\varepsilon\); the fitted line is \(\widehat y=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1 x\) — no error term, no hat on \(x\). Mixing these up loses the "define notation" mark.
Forgetting "holding the others constant" in multiple regression. A slope interpretation without the adjustment clause is marked wrong, because the coefficient's whole meaning is conditional on the other predictors.
Confusing CI and PI. "Mean/expected/average" ⇒ confidence interval (narrow). "New/single/individual" ⇒ prediction interval (wide, has the \(+1\)). Picking the wrong one — or claiming the CI is wider — is a classic lost mark.
Extrapolating without comment. If the requested \(x\) is outside the data range, you must flag it. Predicting a 200 cm tree's age from data on 5–125 cm trees is extrapolation; the linear fit is not evidence it holds out there.
Wrong degrees of freedom. Simple regression uses \(t_{n-2}\), not \(t_{n-1}\). Multiple regression uses \(t_{n-(p+1)}\). Using \(n-1\) gives a slightly wrong \(t\)-multiplier and a wrong CI.
Sig-fig / rounding. The rubric says "give at least 3 significant figures." Round only at the end; if you round \(\widehat\beta_1\) to 2 dp before predicting, your prediction and residual can drift. Carry full precision through the arithmetic.
Reading the intercept \(t\)/\(P\) instead of the slope's. "Significant relationship" is about \(\beta_1\) (the slope row). The intercept's \(P\)-value (e.g. 0.162 for the 2023 tree) is usually irrelevant to that question.
Claiming causation. Regression shows association. Unless it's a randomised experiment, write "associated with / predicts", never "causes".
Saying \(R^2=r\). \(R^2\) is the square of the correlation, and only in simple regression. Don't report \(R^2=0.81\) as "correlation 0.81" — the correlation is \(\sqrt{0.81}=0.90\).
Treating a high \(R^2\) as proof the model is right. \(R^2\) only measures how much \(y\)-scatter the line mops up — it says nothing about whether a straight line was the correct shape. Badly curved data can still post a high \(R^2\) while the residuals-vs-fitted plot screams "non-linear". The verdict on whether the model is appropriate comes from the residual plots, not from \(R^2\). Conversely a "low" \(R^2\) can be a perfectly valid model of a genuinely noisy relationship. Report \(R^2\) as variance-explained, then judge fit from the diagnostics.
Interpreting a log-model slope additively. After a log transform, \(\widehat\beta_1\) is a proportional effect: multiply by \(e^{\widehat\beta_1}\) / \(100(e^{\widehat\beta_1}-1)\%\) per unit — not "+\(\widehat\beta_1\) units".

8 · Self-check

1. An lm output shows Residual standard error: 5.454 on 106 degrees of freedom for a simple regression. How many data points were used, and what does 5.454 estimate?

\(n=106+2=108\). The value \(5.454=\widehat\sigma=\sqrt{\text{MSE}}\) estimates the standard deviation of the error term \(\sigma\).

2. Slope estimate 10.7296, SE 0.3148, df 270. 95% CI for the slope, and does it show a significant relationship?

\(t_{0.975,270}\approx1.97\): \(10.7296\pm1.97(0.3148)=(10.11,\ 11.35)\). It excludes 0 ⇒ significant positive linear relationship.

3. Fitted line \(\widehat{\text{Age}}=-1.20+0.64\,\text{Diameter}\). Observed tree: Diameter 40 cm, Age 22 years. Residual?

\(\widehat y=-1.20+0.64(40)=24.4\); \(e=22-24.4=-2.4\) years (model over-predicted).

4. You want the interval that will contain the waiting time of one specific future eruption. CI or PI — and which is wider?

Prediction interval. It's wider than the CI for the mean because it adds the new observation's own scatter (the \(+1\) under the root).

5. \(R^2=0.5161\) for the frog model. State the interpretation, and give the sample correlation.

"51.6% of the variability in inter-call interval is explained by the linear relationship with temperature." Since it's simple regression, \(|r|=\sqrt{0.5161}=0.718\) (negative here, as the slope is negative, so \(r=-0.718\)).

6. A multiple-regression coefficient reads factor(race)2 -510.501. What does −510.5 mean?

Relative to the reference race group (race = 1), group 2 has an average response 510.5 units lower, holding all the other predictors constant.

7. A residuals-vs-fitted plot shows a clear U-shape. Which assumption is violated and what's the fix?

Linearity — a straight-line mean is wrong. Remedy: transform (e.g. model \(\log Y\)) or add a non-linear term, then re-check the residual plot.

8. After fitting \(\ln(\text{score})=3.011+0.504\,\text{week}\), interpret the week coefficient on the original scale.

Each additional week multiplies score by \(e^{0.504}\approx1.66\) — about a 66% increase per week. Back-transformed model: \(\widehat{\text{score}}=e^{3.011}e^{0.504\,\text{week}}\).

Worked A — Wind-turbine power output (simple regression, full walk-through). A community wind farm records the electrical power output (kW) of one turbine against the wind speed (m/s) at hub height on \(n\) separate ten-minute intervals (wind speeds ranged from 4.3 to 13.8 m/s). R gives:
Call: lm(formula = Power ~ WindSpeed, data = turbine) Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) -90.1028 24.5755 -3.666 0.000716 *** WindSpeed 63.1460 2.7207 23.210 <2e-16 *** --- Signif. codes: 0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1 Residual standard error: 51.16 on 40 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.9309, Adjusted R-squared: 0.9292 F-statistic: 538.7 on 1 and 40 DF, p-value: < 2.2e-16
Tasks: (a) fitted equation + interpret both coefficients; (b) sample size and is the slope significant? (c) 95% CI for the slope; (d) an interval at wind speed 9.0 m/s recorded 520 kW — find the residual; (e) would you predict output at 25 m/s? (f) interpret \(R^2\); (g) CI-for-mean vs PI-for-new at a fixed wind speed.
Show worked solution

1 · Fitted equation & coefficients. Read the Estimate column:

$$\widehat{\text{Power}} = -90.10 + 63.15\,\times\text{WindSpeed}$$

where \(\widehat{\text{Power}}\) is predicted output (kW) and WindSpeed is in m/s. Slope: each extra 1 m/s of wind is associated with a 63.15 kW increase in average power output. Intercept: \(-90.10\) kW is the predicted output at 0 m/s — physically impossible (negative power) and 0 m/s is far outside the data range 4.3–13.8, so it is just a mathematical anchor, not a meaningful prediction.

2 · Sample size & significance. Residual df \(=40\) for a simple regression, so \(n = 40+2 = 42\). Slope \(t = 23.21\) on 40 df with \(P\lt2\times10^{-16}\ll0.05\): reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) ⇒ statistically significant positive linear relationship between wind speed and power.

3 · 95% CI for the slope. \(t_{0.975,\,40}=2.021\):

$$63.146 \pm 2.021(2.7207)=63.146\pm5.50=\boxed{(57.65,\ 68.65)\ \text{kW per m/s}}$$

The interval excludes 0, confirming significance: with 95% confidence each extra 1 m/s adds between 57.6 and 68.6 kW to average output.

4 · Residual at WindSpeed = 9.0.

$$\widehat y = -90.1028 + 63.1460(9.0)=478.21\ \text{kW},\qquad e = y-\widehat y = 520-478.21 = \boxed{+41.79\ \text{kW}}$$

Positive ⇒ the model under-predicted this interval's output.

5 · Predict at 25 m/s? No. 25 m/s lies far beyond the observed range (max 13.8 m/s) — this is extrapolation. Worse, real turbines feather or cut out at high wind, so the straight-line trend certainly does not continue. Do not trust it.

6 · \(R^2\). \(R^2 = 0.9309\): 93.1% of the variability in power output is explained by the linear relationship with wind speed.

7 · CI vs PI. A 95% confidence interval for the mean at a fixed wind speed answers "where is the average output at this wind speed?" — it carries only the uncertainty in the fitted line. A 95% prediction interval for a new ten-minute interval answers "where will one future reading land?" and is always wider, because it adds the individual reading's own scatter \(\widehat\sigma=51.16\) kW (the \(+1\) under the root).

Model sentence for (a): "\(\widehat{\text{Power}} = -90.10 + 63.15\,\text{WindSpeed}\); each 1 m/s rise in wind speed is associated with a 63.15 kW rise in average power output."

Show worked solution

1 · Fitted model.

$$\widehat{\text{Time}} = 323.49 - 1.317\,\text{TrainKm} + 0.977\,\text{Age}\quad(\text{minutes})$$

2 · TrainKm slope — the "holding constant" clause is compulsory. "Holding age constant, each extra 1 km/week of training is associated with a 1.32 minute faster marathon on average." (For contrast, the Age slope: holding training constant, each extra year of age is associated with a 0.98 minute slower finish.)

3 · Sample size. With \(p=2\) predictors, residual df \(=n-(p+1)\), so \(n = 87 + 2 + 1 = 90\).

4 · Significance. Each row's \(t\)-test asks "does this predictor add anything given the other?" TrainKm \(t=-18.01\) and Age \(t=6.32\), both \(P\ll0.05\) ⇒ both contribute after adjusting for the other. The bottom-line global \(F = 196.6\) on 2 and 87 df (\(P\lt2\times10^{-16}\)) tests \(H_0:\beta_{\text{TrainKm}}=\beta_{\text{Age}}=0\); reject ⇒ the predictors jointly explain finish time.

5 · Prediction & which interval. Point estimate \(\widehat y = 283.57\) min. For one individual runner the coach must quote the prediction interval \((256.04,\ 311.10)\), not the mean CI \((280.58,\ 286.57)\). The PI is much wider (\(\approx55\) min vs \(\approx6\) min) because it adds that runner's own race-day scatter on top of the uncertainty in the fitted surface.

6 · 95% CI for the Age slope. \(t_{0.975,\,87}=1.988\):

EXAM-LEVEL  Q3 · A home bakery's growth — the full Q5-shape question, with a transformation twist (16 marks). After going viral, a home baker tracks her monthly online delivery orders against the number of months since launch (months 1 to 24). She first fits a straight line:

lm(formula = Orders ~ Months, data = bakery) Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) -23.9274 10.6236 -2.252 0.0291 * Months 11.8123 0.7620 15.501 <2e-16 *** Residual standard error: 35.74 on 46 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.8393, Adjusted R-squared: 0.8358

The residuals-vs-fitted plot for this model shows a clear U-shaped curve and the vertical spread fans out as the fitted values grow; the Q–Q plot bows away from the line at the upper tail. She refits on the log scale:

lm(formula = log(Orders) ~ Months, data = bakery) Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 3.20165 0.04924 65.015 <2e-16 *** Months 0.10813 0.00353 30.613 <2e-16 *** Residual standard error: 0.1657 on 46 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.9532, Adjusted R-squared: 0.9522

The residuals-vs-fitted plot for the log model is now a flat, patternless band and the Q–Q points lie on the diagonal.

(b) [3] Write the fitted log-scale model and its back-transformed form on the original (orders) scale. (c) [3] Interpret the Months slope of the log model in context. (d) [3] Give a 95% CI for the log-model slope and convert it to a statement about monthly percentage growth. Show solution

(a) The residuals-vs-fitted plot has a systematic U-shaped curve ⇒ linearity is violated (a straight line is the wrong mean shape). The spread fans out as fitted values grow ⇒ constant variance (homoscedasticity) is violated. The Q–Q plot bowing at the upper tail ⇒ normality of the errors is doubtful. A high \(R^2=0.84\) does not rescue it — \(R^2\) says nothing about whether a line was the right shape; the residual plots are the verdict.

(b) Log-scale fitted model:

$$\widehat{\ln(\text{Orders})} = 3.2017 + 0.10813\,\text{Months}.$$

Back-transforming (exponentiate both sides):

$$\widehat{\text{Orders}} = e^{3.2017}\,e^{0.10813\,\text{Months}} = 24.58\,e^{0.10813\,\text{Months}}.$$

(c) On a log model the slope is a multiplicative effect: each extra month multiplies orders by \(e^{0.10813}=1.114\), i.e. an estimated \(100(e^{0.10813}-1)\approx\boxed{11.4\%}\) increase in orders per month (not an additive +0.108).

Excludes 0% ⇒ significant growth.

(e) At Months \(=18\): \(\widehat{\ln(\text{Orders})} = 3.2017 + 0.10813(18)=5.148\), so \(\widehat{\text{Orders}} = e^{5.148}\approx\boxed{172\ \text{orders}}\). Month 18 is inside the observed range (1–24), so this is safe interpolation. The prediction interval for a single future month is wider than the confidence interval for the mean, because it adds that month's own irreducible scatter (the \(+1\) under the root) on top of the uncertainty in the fitted line.

Model sentence for (c): "Each additional month multiplies the average number of orders by about 1.11 — an estimated 11.4% monthly increase."

Question 1 — Conditional probability 11 marks

For a model of car, in the first 5 years: $P(\text{engine fault})=0.05$, $P(\text{transmission fault})=0.02$, and $P(\text{engine or transmission or both})=0.06$.

(a) Define the events, sample space and known probabilities. 3

"Define events with notation" → set up letters and translate every sentence into probability statements before computing anything.
Sample space $\Omega=$ all cars of this model in their first 5 years. Let $$E=\{\text{engine fault}\},\qquad T=\{\text{transmission fault}\}.$$ Given: $P(E)=0.05$, $P(T)=0.02$, $P(E\cup T)=0.06$.
It is useful to first extract the intersection using the addition rule (#8): $$P(E\cap T)=P(E)+P(T)-P(E\cup T)=0.05+0.02-0.06=0.01.$$
Deriving $P(E\cap T)=0.01$ up front is the key that unlocks every remaining part — do it in (a).

(b) If a car has a faulty transmission, what is $P(\text{faulty engine})$? 3

"Given faulty transmission" → conditional probability $P(E\mid T)=P(E\cap T)/P(T)$ (#11).
$$P(E\mid T)=\frac{P(E\cap T)}{P(T)}=\frac{0.01}{0.02}=0.5.$$
$P(E\mid T)=0.500$.

(c) If a car's transmission passes all tests, what is $P(\text{faulty engine})$? 3

"Transmission passes" $=T^c$. Need $P(E\mid T^c)=P(E\cap T^c)/P(T^c)$; get $P(E\cap T^c)$ by "engine faults minus those that also have transmission faults".
$$P(E\cap T^c)=P(E)-P(E\cap T)=0.05-0.01=0.04,\qquad P(T^c)=1-0.02=0.98.$$ $$P(E\mid T^c)=\frac{0.04}{0.98}=0.0408.$$
$P(E\mid T^c)=0.0408$.
$P(E\cap T^c)\ne P(E)$. You must subtract the overlap $P(E\cap T)$. And the denominator is $P(T^c)=0.98$, not $0.02$.

(d) If a car has a faulty engine, what is $P(\text{faulty transmission})$? 2

$$P(T\mid E)=\frac{P(E\cap T)}{P(E)}=\frac{0.01}{0.05}=0.2.$$
$P(T\mid E)=0.200$.
Note $P(E\mid T)=0.5\ne P(T\mid E)=0.2$: conditioning is not symmetric. Also, since $P(E\mid T)\ne P(E)=0.05$, engine and transmission faults are not independent.

Question 2 — Variance derivation & expected outliers 13 marks

(a) Derive $\operatorname{Var}(\overline X-\overline Y)$ for two independent samples ($m$ and $n$ obs.) with common variance $\sigma^2$. 5

"Derive … with justification" → variance algebra: independence lets variances add (#52), and $\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ (#26b).
One mean. With $X_1,\dots,X_m$ independent, each variance $\sigma^2$: $$\operatorname{Var}(\overline X)=\operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\tfrac1m\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^m X_i\Big)=\tfrac1{m^2}\sum_{i=1}^m\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\tfrac1{m^2}(m\sigma^2)=\tfrac{\sigma^2}{m},$$ and similarly $\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y)=\sigma^2/n$.
Combine. The two samples are independent, so $\operatorname{Cov}(\overline X,\overline Y)=0$ and $$\operatorname{Var}(\overline X-\overline Y)=\operatorname{Var}(\overline X)+(-1)^2\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y)=\frac{\sigma^2}{m}+\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$
$\operatorname{Var}(\overline X-\overline Y)=\sigma^2\!\left(\dfrac1m+\dfrac1n\right)$.
State the three justifications explicitly: independence ⇒ variances add; the $\tfrac1{m^2}$ from squaring the constant; the minus sign squares to $+1$. (Identical to 2021 Q1(f) — a recurring bookwork item.)

(b) A boxplot flags an outlier if a point is at least $1.5\times$IQR beyond a box end. For a standard normal, what sample size gives an expected 10 outliers? 8

Two stages: (1) find the outlier probability $p$ for one $\mathsf N(0,1)$ observation using the fences; (2) set expected count $np=10$ and solve $n=10/p$ (expectation of a Binomial, #33).
Stage 1 — the fences. For $\mathsf N(0,1)$ the quartiles are symmetric: from the normal table, $\Phi(z)=0.75$ at $z\approx 0.674$, so $Q_3=0.6745$, $Q_1=-0.6745$, and $$\text{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1=1.349.$$ Upper fence $=Q_3+1.5\,\text{IQR}=0.6745+1.5(1.349)=2.698$; by symmetry the lower fence is $-2.698$.
Outlier probability. $$p=P(|Z|\gt 2.698)=2\big(1-\Phi(2.698)\big).$$ From tables: $\Phi(2.70)=0.9965$, so $p\approx 2(1-0.9965)=2(0.0035)=0.0070$ (exact $0.006977$).
Stage 2 — sample size. The number of outliers among $n$ points is $\mathsf{Bin}(n,p)$ with mean $np$. Set $$np=10\;\Rightarrow\; n=\frac{10}{0.0070}\approx 1429\quad(\text{exact }p:\ n\approx 1433).$$
$n\approx 1.43\times10^{3}$ observations (≈1429 using the tabulated $\Phi(2.70)$; ≈1433 with full precision).
A neat shortcut: because $Q_1=-Q_3$, the fence is $Q_3+1.5(2Q_3)=4Q_3$. Don't confuse the "$1.5\times$IQR" boxplot rule with the "$\pm2$ or $\pm3$ s.d." rules — the question specifies the boxplot definition.

Question 3 — Paired $t$-test (ibuprofen & albumin) 21 marks

Eight patients had blood albumin measured before and after two weeks of ibuprofen (paired data). Summary:
mean (g/L)s.d. (g/L)
Before40.06.35
After37.95.10
Difference (drop)2.102.40
The 8 differences were approximately symmetric with no apparent outliers.

(a) State $H_0$ and $H_1$ for the effect of ibuprofen on albumin, explaining notation. 4

Same subjects measured twice → paired design. Work with the mean of the within-subject differences $\mu_d$. "Any effect" → two-sided.
Let $\mu_d$ be the population mean difference in blood albumin (before $-$ after) for rheumatic patients on this ibuprofen regimen. $$H_0:\;\mu_d=0\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\;\mu_d\ne 0.$$
This is a paired problem — do not run a two-sample test with the before/after s.d.'s (6.35, 5.10). Because each patient is their own control, you use only the s.d. of the differences (2.40). Using a two-sample test here is the biggest single error on this question.

(b) Perform the test at the 5% level; give a statistical and a contextual conclusion. 10

One-sample $t$-test on the differences (#70): $T=\dfrac{\bar d-0}{s_d/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}$ under $H_0$.
With $\bar d=2.10$ (a drop), $s_d=2.40$, $n=8$: $$\text{s.e.}=\frac{s_d}{\sqrt n}=\frac{2.40}{\sqrt8}=0.8485,\qquad T=\frac{2.10}{0.8485}=2.475\quad\text{on }7\text{ df.}$$ (Taking the drop as $\bar d=-2.10$ gives $T=-2.475$; only the magnitude matters for a two-sided test.)
$p$-value (two-sided), from tables ($t_7$): the $0.975$ quantile is $2.36$ and the $0.99$ quantile is $3.00$. Since $2.36\lt 2.475\lt 3.00$, the one-tail area is between $0.01$ and $0.025$, so $$0.02\lt p\lt 0.05\quad(\text{exact }p=0.0425).$$ Since $p\lt 0.05$, reject $H_0$.
There is significant evidence at the 5% level that ibuprofen changes mean blood albumin in rheumatic patients. The data indicate a mean drop of about 2.1 g/L in albumin after two weeks of ibuprofen.
Bracket the $p$-value with the two neighbouring $t$-table quantiles, state the inequality, then confirm the exact value — that is exactly the presentation examiners reward. Assumptions to note: differences approximately normal (stated symmetric, no outliers) and independent patients — both satisfied.

(c) Construct a 95% CI for the mean difference (3 sig figs). 4

One-sample CI for a mean (#61): $\bar d\pm q\,s_d/\sqrt n$, with $q$ the $0.975$ quantile of $t_{n-1}$.
From tables: $t_{7,\,0.975}=2.36$ (exact $2.365$). $$\bar d\pm q\,\frac{s_d}{\sqrt n}=2.10\pm 2.36(0.8485)=2.10\pm 2.00=(0.0975,\ 4.10)\ \text{g/L drop}.$$ Equivalently, for (after $-$ before): $(-4.10,\ -0.0975)$.
95% CI for the mean drop: $(0.0975,\ 4.10)$ g/L (using $q=2.36$).
The interval excludes 0, which agrees with rejecting $H_0$ in (b): a 95% CI and a two-sided 5% test always give matching decisions.

(d) Explain the meaning of a 95% confidence level. 3

A 95% confidence level refers to the procedure, not any single interval: if we repeated the study many times and built a 95% CI each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean difference $\mu_d$. For our one interval, $\mu_d$ either is or isn't inside — we cannot attach a 95% probability to this specific interval.
Never say "there is a 95% probability that $\mu_d$ lies in this interval." $\mu_d$ is a fixed constant; the randomness is in the interval. The correct phrasing is about the long-run capture rate of the method.

The control group has the highest and most spread-out HbA1c (median ≈9.5, largest box), i.e. worst blood-sugar control. $34.6\gg 2.60\Rightarrow$ reject $H_0$.
There is very strong evidence ($p\lt0.001$) that mean HbA1c differs across the four treatment groups — the treatments are not equally effective at regulating blood sugar.

Question 5 — Regression with interaction (face coverings) 18 marks

Protection efficiency (PE) of three face coverings (face shield, microfibre, surgical) vs particle diameter (µm). Scatterplot (described): PE rises with diameter for all three; surgical and microfibre climb steeply to ≈0.8–1.0 at large diameters, while face shields stay low (≈0.2–0.4) throughout. A model with interaction, lm(PE ~ log(Diameter) * Type) (face shield = baseline):
Coefficients: Estimate Std.Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 0.17589 0.01625 10.823 < 2e-16 *** log(Diameter) 0.06962 0.01535 4.536 8.53e-06 *** Typemicrofibre 0.21515 0.02298 9.361 < 2e-16 *** Typesurgical 0.23893 0.02298 10.396 < 2e-16 *** log(Diameter):Typemicrofibre 0.27297 0.02171 12.575 < 2e-16 *** log(Diameter):Typesurgical 0.26455 0.02171 12.188 < 2e-16 *** Residual standard error: 0.09606 on 279 DF Multiple R-squared: 0.896, Adjusted R-squared: 0.8942

(a) Describe the relationship between PE, diameter, and covering type. 3

PE increases with particle diameter for all three coverings (positive log-diameter effect). Surgical and microfibre masks achieve much higher PE than face shields across the whole range, and their PE rises faster with diameter (steeper slopes) — i.e. the diameter effect depends on covering type, which is why an interaction term is included. Face shields give the least protection and the flattest response.

(b) From the diagnostic plots of the first (untransformed) model, state and assess the assumptions. 4

Figure (described): Residuals vs Fitted shows a clear curved (hump-shaped) pattern with the smoother bending — residuals are positive in the middle, negative at the ends. Normal Q–Q is close to the diagonal.
Assumptions of the linear model: (1) linearity of the mean — violated: the curved residual pattern shows the straight-line (in raw diameter) form is wrong. (2) constant variance — questionable given the patterned residuals. (3) normality — the Q–Q plot is roughly straight, so satisfied. (4) independence — assumed by design.
The curvature in the residuals-vs-fitted plot means a linear-in-diameter model is inadequate; a transformation of diameter is warranted — motivating the $\log(\text{Diameter})$ model in part (c).

(c) Choose two covering types and write their fitted regression equations. 3

With a factor + interaction and face shield as baseline: microfibre/surgical intercepts add their Type coefficient; their slopes add the corresponding log(Diameter):Type coefficient.
Face shield (baseline): $$\widehat{\text{PE}}=0.17589+0.06962\,\log(\text{Diameter}).$$ Surgical: intercept $0.17589+0.23893=0.41482$; slope $0.06962+0.26455=0.33417$: $$\widehat{\text{PE}}=0.41482+0.33417\,\log(\text{Diameter}).$$ (For reference, microfibre: $\widehat{\text{PE}}=0.39104+0.34259\,\log(\text{Diameter})$.)
The Type coefficients shift only the intercept; the interaction coefficients shift the slope. Forgetting to add the interaction term to the slope is the standard error here.

(d) At 2 µm, which covering gives the highest expected PE? 2

Evaluate each equation at Diameter $=2$, so $\log(2)=0.6931$:
coveringexpected PE at 2 µm
face shield$0.17589+0.06962(0.6931)=0.224$
microfibre$0.39104+0.34259(0.6931)=0.628$

The surgical mask gives the highest expected PE at 2 µm (≈0.646), narrowly ahead of microfibre (≈0.628); the face shield is far behind (≈0.224).

(e) Is there evidence that face-shield PE changes with diameter? Use a 95% CI. 3

Face-shield slope = the log(Diameter) coefficient. "Changes with diameter?" → does its 95% CI exclude 0? $\hat\beta\pm t_{0.975,\,df}\,\text{s.e.}$
$\hat\beta=0.06962$, s.e. $=0.01535$, $df=279$. From tables: $t_{0.975}$ for large df is $\approx 1.97$ (between the $df=200$ value 1.97 and $df=\infty$ value 1.96). $$0.06962\pm 1.97(0.01535)=0.06962\pm 0.0302=(0.0394,\ 0.0999).$$ The interval excludes 0.
Yes — the 95% CI for the face-shield slope, $(0.0394,\ 0.0999)$, lies entirely above 0, so there is evidence that face-shield PE genuinely increases with particle diameter (consistent with the tiny $p$-value $8.5\times10^{-6}$).

(f) For a surgical mask, if particle size is halved, how much does expected PE change? 3

Compare the fitted values at $x$ and $x/2$. Because the predictor is $\log(\text{Diameter})$, the difference depends only on the slope, not on $x$.
Surgical slope on $\log(\text{Diameter})$ is $0.33417$. The change from $x$ to $x/2$ is $$\Delta\widehat{\text{PE}}=0.33417\big(\log(x/2)-\log x\big)=0.33417\,\log(\tfrac12)=0.33417(-0.6931)=-0.232.$$
Halving particle size decreases the expected PE of a surgical mask by about 0.232 (a drop of ~23 percentage points), regardless of the starting size.
The elegance of the log predictor: multiplying diameter by a constant (here $\tfrac12$) shifts PE by a fixed amount ($\text{slope}\times\log(\text{constant})$), independent of $x$. That is why the "for any $x$" hint works.

§1 · Concepts in depth

The one-variable → many-variable jump

Everything you did in Ch 1–2 for a single random variable now has a "joint" twin. A single RV is described by its pmf/pdf; several RVs together are described by their joint distribution, which captures not just how each variable behaves on its own but how they move together. That coupling is the whole point of the chapter — and it is exactly what the flagship exam question tests.

Two levels of description:

  • Joint pmf \(f(x,y)=\mathbb P(X=x,\,Y=y)\) — the probability of each cell (each combination). In a frequency table with total \(n\), \(f(x,y)=n_{xy}/n\) (notes Def 3.2; 2025 Q2 states this explicitly).
  • Joint cdf \(F(x,y)=\mathbb P(X\le x,\,Y\le y)\) — cumulative version, rarely needed in the exam for tables (Def 3.1).
🧭 What a joint distribution really is: a table of "AND" probabilities. Each cell answers one question — "what fraction of the population has \(X=x\) and \(Y=y\) at the same time?" Read the comma in \(\mathbb P(X=x,\,Y=y)\) as the word and. Because every individual lands in exactly one cell, the whole table must sum to 1 (\(\sum_{x,y}f(x,y)=1\)) — that single fact is what lets you solve for an unknown parameter (Example 2). The cells hold everything: how each variable behaves alone and how they lean on each other. The margins (next card) are just shadows cast by summing the cells.

Marginals: collapse the table by summing

The marginal pmf of \(X\) is just the distribution of \(X\) alone, recovered by summing the joint pmf over all values of the other variable (notes eq 3.2):

$$f_X(x)=\sum_y f(x,y),\qquad f_Y(y)=\sum_x f(x,y).$$

In a table this is row sums (for \(X\) if \(X\) labels the rows) and column sums (for \(Y\)). With raw counts, marginal pmf = row/column total ÷ n. This is the entry point to almost every table question: you need the marginals to get \(\mathbb E[X],\mathbb E[Y]\), variances, and the expected counts for a χ² test.

You cannot go the other way. Row and column totals do not determine the inside of the table — many different joint tables share the same margins (notes p.56). So "marginals" throw away the dependence information; that information lives only in the cells.

Independence — the product rule for pmfs

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent iff the joint pmf factorises into the product of the marginals for every cell (notes Def 3.4):

$$f(x,y)=f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\quad\text{for all }x,y.$$

🧭 What factorisation MEANS: knowing the value of one variable tells you nothing about the other. Formally the conditional distribution equals the marginal, \(f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=f_Y(y)\) — the row you land in doesn't reshape the odds across \(Y\). That is exactly why the joint is just the product: if a factor "height=tall" doubles your row, an independent \(Y\) keeps its same relative spread, so every cell is (row prob) × (col prob). Dependence is the opposite — some rows over-load particular columns, and the product rule breaks in at least one cell.

Practically: to prove independence you must check every cell equals row-prob × col-prob. To disprove it, one cell where \(f(x,y)\ne f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) is enough — state that single counterexample and stop.

Independence \(\Rightarrow\) \(\text{Cov}=0\), but the converse is false. Zero covariance does not imply independence. The chapter-3 solutions and tut-sheet 3 both hammer this: "even if the covariance had been 0, this would not have implied independence." Always check the product rule for the independence part — never argue independence from \(\text{Cov}=0\).

Expectations for joint distributions

For any function \(h\), \(\displaystyle\mathbb E[h(X,Y)]=\sum_{x,y}h(x,y)\,f(x,y)\) (notes eq 3.5). Two special cases you will use constantly:

Linearity (Theorem 3.1) — always true, no independence needed

$$\mathbb E[a+b_1X_1+\cdots+b_nX_n]=a+b_1\mu_1+\cdots+b_n\mu_n.$$

Product rule (Theorem 3.1, eq 3.7) — needs independence

$$X,Y\text{ independent}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb E[XY]=\mathbb E[X]\,\mathbb E[Y].$$

For a table you compute \(\mathbb E[XY]=\sum_{x,y}xy\,f(x,y)\) directly from the cells — this is the engine of the covariance/correlation calculation, and it is where dependence shows up.

Covariance & correlation — measuring how they move together

Covariance (Def 3.5) measures linear co-movement:

$$\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\,\mathbb E[Y].$$

🧭 Why this formula captures "moving together": look at the product of the two deviations \((X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\) for one individual. If both are above their means the product is (+)(+) = positive; if both are below, (−)(−) = positive again. But if one is above while the other is below, you get (+)(−) = negative. Covariance is the average of these products. So a positive covariance means "when \(X\) is high, \(Y\) tends to be high too" (they move the same direction); negative means they move opposite ways; and if highs of \(X\) pair with highs and lows of \(Y\) equally, the positives and negatives cancel to ≈ 0. The shortcut form \(\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]\) is the same idea: \(\mathbb E[XY]\) is what actually happens, \(\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]\) is what you'd expect if they were unrelated — the gap between them is the co-movement.
Covariance has ugly units. If \(X\) is in kg and \(Y\) in cm, \(\text{Cov}\) comes out in "kg·cm", and its size also blows up if you merely rescale (measure height in mm and covariance ×10). So the raw number "0.18" is meaningless on its own — you can't tell strong from weak. That units problem is the whole reason we divide by \(\sigma_X\sigma_Y\) to get correlation \(\varrho\): the units cancel, leaving a clean, comparable number pinned to \([-1,1]\).

The second form (Theorem 3.2 property 3) is the exam-friendly one: get \(\mathbb E[XY]\), \(\mathbb E[X]\), \(\mathbb E[Y]\) from the table, subtract. Covariance has awkward units, so we scale it to the correlation coefficient \(\varrho\) (eq 3.8), which is unit-free and always in \([-1,1]\):

$$\varrho(X,Y)=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}.$$

Sign = direction of the linear relationship; magnitude toward 1 = stronger linear association; \(\varrho=0\) = no linear association (but possibly still dependent).

Variance & covariance toolkit (Theorem 3.2)

1. \(\text{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb E[X])^2\)
2. \(\text{Var}(a+bX)=b^2\text{Var}(X)\)
3. \(\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]\)
4. \(\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\text{Cov}(Y,X)\)
5. \(\text{Cov}(aX+bY,Z)=a\,\text{Cov}(X,Z)+b\,\text{Cov}(Y,Z)\)
6. \(\text{Cov}(X,X)=\text{Var}(X)\)
7. \(\text{Var}(X+Y)=\text{Var}(X)+\text{Var}(Y)+2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\)
8. \(X,Y\) independent \(\Rightarrow\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0\)

Linear combinations — the workhorse for "sums of normals"

🧭 What a "linear combination" is: you take your random variables, scale each by a constant and add them, maybe plus a fixed offset: \(a+b_1X_1+\cdots+b_nX_n\). That one shape covers almost every real question — a total (all \(b_i=1\)), an average (all \(b_i=1/n\)), a difference (\(b_1=1,b_2=-1\)), a unit conversion (°F \(=\tfrac95\)°C\(+32\)), or a cost (\(b_i=\) price per unit). Learn to handle this one object and you've handled them all.
🧭 Why variance carries a \(2ab\,\text{Cov}\) term. Start from the general two-variable rule \(\text{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\). It falls straight out of "squaring a sum": \((aX+bY)^2\) expands to \(a^2X^2+b^2Y^2+\mathbf{2ab\,XY}\), and that cross-term is what becomes the covariance. Intuition: if the two variables tend to rise together (\(\text{Cov}>0\)), their wobbles reinforce and the combined spread is bigger than you'd guess; if they offset each other (\(\text{Cov}\lt 0\)), the wobbles partly cancel and the spread shrinks. Independence sets \(\text{Cov}=0\), the cross-term vanishes, and you're left with the clean "variances just add" rule below. That is the only reason the formula looks so simple here — it silently assumes independence.

For independent \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) (Theorem 3.3):

$$\text{Var}(a+b_1X_1+\cdots+b_nX_n)=b_1^2\sigma_1^2+\cdots+b_n^2\sigma_n^2.$$

Combine with linearity for the mean and you can find the distribution of any weighted sum. If, in addition, the \(X_i\) are normal (Theorem 3.6), the result is exactly normal:

$$Y=a+\textstyle\sum b_iX_i\ \sim\ \mathcal N\!\Big(a+\sum b_i\mu_i,\ \sum b_i^2\sigma_i^2\Big).$$

For a difference \(Y-X\) of independent normals the variances still add: \(\text{Var}(Y-X)=\sigma_Y^2+\sigma_X^2\) (the \(b=-1\) gets squared). This trips people up every year.

Limit theorems — why the normal is everywhere

Take iid \(X_1,X_2,\dots\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Let \(S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n\) and \(\bar X=S_n/n\). Then \(\mathbb E[\bar X]=\mu\) and \(\text{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\).

🧭 Meet the standard error. The SD of the sample mean, \(\text{sd}(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n\), gets its own name: the standard error of \(\bar X\). It is not the spread of the data (\(\sigma\)) — it's the spread of the average, i.e. how much \(\bar X\) would jump around if you re-ran the whole sample. Bigger \(n\) → smaller standard error → a more reliable average (that's the \(\sqrt n\) in every confidence interval later). This idea — the distribution of a statistic across hypothetical repeats — is a sampling distribution; the deep floor for it (and for the Z→t, χ², F machinery it feeds) lives in foundations.html.

Law of Large Numbers (Theorem 3.4)

As \(n\to\infty\) the sample average \(\bar X\) converges to the true mean \(\mu\): the variance \(\sigma^2/n\to 0\), so averaging kills randomness. (This is why simulation works: average many draws to estimate an expectation.)

Central Limit Theorem (Theorem 3.5)

🧭 What the CLT actually claims. Add up (or average) enough independent copies of any distribution — skewed, lumpy, discrete, whatever — and the distribution of that sum/average settles into a bell curve. The parent's weird shape gets ironed out. "Approximate" means it's never exactly normal for finite \(n\), but the fit gets steadily better as \(n\) grows (rule of thumb: \(n\gtrsim30\), fewer if the parent is already symmetric). This is the plank the whole inference course stands on — it's why a \(Z\) or \(t\) statistic is legitimate even when you never assumed the data were normal.

For large \(n\), regardless of the parent distribution's shape:

$$S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n\ \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\ \mathcal N(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2),\qquad \bar X\ \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\ \mathcal N\!\Big(\mu,\ \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big).$$

This is the bridge from "any distribution" to "normal", and it powers every confidence interval and \(t\)-test in Ch 6–7. A special case is the normal approximation to the binomial: since \(X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)\) is a sum of \(n\) iid \(\text{Ber}(p)\) variables,

$$X\ \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\ \mathcal N\big(np,\ np(1-p)\big),\quad\text{valid when }np>5\text{ and }n(1-p)>5.$$

LLN says the average lands on \(\mu\); CLT says how it's scattered around \(\mu\) (normally, spread \(\sigma/\sqrt n\)). Different questions: "what does the average tend to?" → LLN; "probability the average exceeds…?" → CLT.

Estimator vs estimate — reading θ̂ correctly

Some exam parts (Type D) ask for "the approximate distribution of the estimator \(\hat\theta\) when \(n\) is large". Two words that look the same but aren't:

  • An estimator is the recipe — a formula in the not-yet-seen data, e.g. \(\hat\theta=\bar X\). Because its input (the sample) is random, the estimator is itself a random variable, so it has a mean, variance and distribution.
  • An estimate is the single number you get after plugging in one actual sample, e.g. \(\hat\theta=30.4\). Just a number — no distribution.
🧭 Why the distinction earns marks: the question "what is the approximate distribution of \(\hat\theta\)?" only makes sense for the estimator (the random recipe). Its spread across hypothetical re-samples is its sampling distribution, and when \(\hat\theta\) is an average of iid pieces the CLT hands you \(\hat\theta\overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\mathcal N\big(\theta,\,\text{Var}(\hat\theta)\big)\) — that's what those parts are testing. A good estimator is unbiased: \(\mathbb E[\hat\theta]=\theta\), i.e. it is centred on the truth and doesn't systematically over- or under-shoot, so on average it's right. Full treatment (bias, standard error of \(\hat\theta\), efficiency) is on foundations.html and Ch 6.

§2 · Key formulas & when to use them

Marginal pmf from a table

$$f_X(x)=\sum_y f(x,y)\quad\Big(=\tfrac{\text{row total}}{n}\Big),\qquad f_Y(y)=\sum_x f(x,y)\quad\Big(=\tfrac{\text{col total}}{n}\Big)$$
You see a joint frequency/probability table and are asked for "the marginal pmf of \(X\) (or \(Y\))". Sum across the other variable; with counts divide totals by \(n\).

Mean, variance, SD of a discrete RV

$$\mathbb E[X]=\sum_x x\,f_X(x),\qquad \text{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb E[X])^2,\qquad \sigma_X=\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$$
Any "expectation / standard deviation of \(X\)" once you have the marginal. Always compute \(\mathbb E[X^2]=\sum x^2 f_X(x)\) as an intermediate for the variance.

Covariance & correlation from a table

$$\mathbb E[XY]=\sum_{x,y}xy\,f(x,y),\quad \text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y],\quad \varrho=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$$
"Determine the covariance / correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\)." Only cells with \(x\ne0\) and \(y\ne0\) contribute to \(\mathbb E[XY]\) — the rest vanish, so the sum is shorter than it looks.

Independence check

$$X\perp Y\ \iff\ f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\ \text{ for ALL }x,y$$
"Are \(X\) and \(Y\) independent?" To confirm: verify every cell. To reject: find one cell where it fails and quote it. Never argue from \(\text{Cov}=0\).

Linear combinations (mean always; variance needs independence)

$$\mathbb E\Big[a+\sum b_iX_i\Big]=a+\sum b_i\mu_i,\qquad \text{Var}\Big(a+\sum b_iX_i\Big)=\sum b_i^2\sigma_i^2$$
Total/average of several RVs, cost = price×quantity, difference of two measurements, scaling (e.g. °F→°C). Variances add for both sums and differences of independent RVs.

Sum/linear combination of independent normals is normal (Thm 3.6)

$$Y=a+\sum b_iX_i\ \sim\ \mathcal N\Big(a+\sum b_i\mu_i,\ \sum b_i^2\sigma_i^2\Big),\qquad Z=\frac{Y-\mathbb E[Y]}{\text{sd}(Y)}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$$
Independent normal parents and you need a probability about a sum/difference/average. Build the normal, standardise, read the \(Z\)-table.

Central Limit Theorem

$$S_n\overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\mathcal N(n\mu,\,n\sigma^2),\qquad \bar X\overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\mathcal N\Big(\mu,\tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big),\qquad \text{Bin}(n,p)\overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\mathcal N\big(np,np(1-p)\big)$$
"Sum/average of \(n\) independent…", "approximate the probability that the mean/total exceeds…", "for large \(n\), what is the (approximate) distribution?" Parent need not be normal — that's the power of CLT.

χ² test of independence (crossover to Ch 11)

$$E_{ij}=\frac{(\text{row}_i)(\text{col}_j)}{n},\qquad \chi^2=\sum\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}},\qquad \text{df}=(r-1)(c-1)$$
"Test whether \(X\) is associated with \(Y\)" / "test of association" from a table of counts. This is the natural partner of the correlation part — see the full method on ch11-chi-squared.html. Worked in full in Example 1 below.

Example 1 (FLAGSHIP) — 2025 Q2: joint frequency table, screen use × sleep quality, n = 120 [16 marks]

Setup. \(X=\) uses screens within 30 min of bedtime (0 = No, 1 = Yes); \(Y=\) sleep quality (0 = poor, 1 = fair, 2 = good). \(f(x,y)=n_{xy}/120\).

countsY=0 (poor)Y=1 (fair)Y=2 (good)row total
X=0 (No)258740
X=1 (Yes)15204580
col total402852120

(a) Marginal pmfs — row/column totals ÷ 120:

f_X: P(X=0)=40/120=1/3≈0.333 P(X=1)=80/120=2/3≈0.667 f_Y: P(Y=0)=40/120=1/3≈0.333 P(Y=1)=28/120≈0.233 P(Y=2)=52/120≈0.433

(b) Expectations — \(\sum x f_X(x)\) and \(\sum y f_Y(y)\):

$$\mathbb E[X]=0\cdot\tfrac{40}{120}+1\cdot\tfrac{80}{120}=\tfrac{80}{120}=0.667$$ $$\mathbb E[Y]=0\cdot\tfrac{40}{120}+1\cdot\tfrac{28}{120}+2\cdot\tfrac{52}{120}=\tfrac{132}{120}=1.10$$

(c) Correlation \(\varrho=\dfrac{\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\).

Variances (note \(X\) is 0/1, so \(\mathbb E[X^2]=\mathbb E[X]\)):

E[X²]=0.667 Var(X)=0.667−0.667²=2/9=0.2222 σ_X=0.4714 E[Y²]=0+1·(28/120)+4·(52/120)=236/120=1.9667 Var(Y)=1.9667−1.10²=0.7567 σ_Y=0.8699

\(\mathbb E[XY]\): only \(X=1\) rows survive (X=0 kills the product), and within them only \(Y\ge1\):

E[XY]=1·1·(20/120)+1·2·(45/120)=(20+90)/120=110/120=0.9167 Cov(X,Y)=0.9167 − 0.667×1.10 = 0.9167 − 0.7333 = 0.1833 ϱ = 0.1833 / (0.4714 × 0.8699) = 0.447 (3 s.f.)

Positive, moderate correlation: screen users tend to report better-numbered sleep-quality codes. (Direction only — see the trap on ordinal coding.)

(d) Appropriate test of association → χ² test of independence (data are counts in a 2×3 table).

H₀: screen use and sleep quality are independent.  H₁: they are associated.
Expected counts \(E_{ij}=\text{row}_i\times\text{col}_j/120\):

Y=0 Y=1 Y=2 X=0 13.33 9.33 17.33 X=1 26.67 18.67 34.67

\(\chi^2=\sum\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}\):

(25−13.33)²/13.33 = 10.21 (8−9.33)²/9.33 = 0.19 (7−17.33)²/17.33 = 6.16 (15−26.67)²/26.67 = 5.10 (20−18.67)²/18.67 = 0.10 (45−34.67)²/34.67 = 3.08 χ² = 24.84, df = (2−1)(3−1) = 2

Decision. \(\chi^2_{0.05,\,2}=5.99\) and \(\chi^2_{0.01,\,2}=9.21\); observed \(24.84\) far exceeds both, so \(P\text{-value}\approx 4\times10^{-6}\) (\(\ll 0.05\)). Reject H₀.
In context: there is very strong evidence that screen use before bed is associated with sleep quality in this sample.
The correlation (c) and the χ² test (d) answer different questions: \(\varrho\) measures linear co-movement of the coded numbers; χ² tests any association and is the right formal "test". Full χ² method: ch11-chi-squared.html.
Example 2 — 2024 Q1: parametric joint pmf table (find p, marginals, Cov, correlation) [12 marks]

Joint pmf of leakage in tanks A, B (0 = none, 1 = leak), with \(0\le p\le 1\):

B = 0B = 1
A = 0\(\tfrac14 p^2\)\(p\)
A = 1\(p\)\(p^2\)

(a) Find p — probabilities sum to 1:

$$\tfrac14p^2+p+p+p^2=1\ \Rightarrow\ \tfrac54p^2+2p-1=0\ \Rightarrow\ 5p^2+8p-4=0\ \Rightarrow\ p=0.4$$ (other root \(-2\) is rejected). Cells become \(0.04,\ 0.40,\ 0.40,\ 0.16\) (sum \(=1\) ✓).

(b) Marginal pmf of A: \(P(A=0)=0.04+0.40=0.44,\quad P(A=1)=0.40+0.16=0.56.\)

(c) Mean and variance of A (Bernoulli-type):

E[A]=0.56 Var(A)=E[A²]−E[A]²=0.56−0.56²=0.2464 σ_A=0.496

(d) Covariance & correlation (by symmetry \(B\) has the same margins, \(\sigma_B=\sigma_A\)):

E[AB]=1·1·P(A=1,B=1)=0.16 Cov(A,B)=0.16 − 0.56×0.56 = −0.1536 ϱ = −0.1536 / (0.496×0.496) = −0.623 (3 s.f.)

Negative correlation — a leak in one tank is associated with no leak in the other. Same table skeleton as 2025 Q2, but here you must first solve for the parameter before anything else works.

Example 4 — CLT for a sample mean (parent shape unknown) [exam-style]

A call centre's handling times have mean \(\mu=30\) s and SD \(\sigma=8\) s (distribution skewed, not normal). A shift handles \(n=100\) independent calls. Find the approximate probability the mean handling time exceeds 32 s, and that the total exceeds 3100 s.

By CLT, even though the parent is skewed, for \(n=100\) the mean is approximately normal:

$$\bar X\ \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\ \mathcal N\Big(30,\ \tfrac{8^2}{100}\Big)=\mathcal N(30,\,0.64),\quad \text{sd}(\bar X)=\tfrac{8}{\sqrt{100}}=0.8.$$

P(X̄ > 32) = P(Z > (32−30)/0.8) = P(Z > 2.50) = 1 − 0.9938 = 0.0062

For the total \(S_{100}\sim\mathcal N(100\cdot30,\,100\cdot8^2)=\mathcal N(3000,\,6400)\), sd \(=80\):

P(S₁₀₀ > 3100) = P(Z > (3100−3000)/80) = P(Z > 1.25) = 1 − 0.8944 = 0.106

The trigger phrase "approximate the probability that the mean exceeds…" with iid draws is a pure CLT flag. Standardise with \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) for a mean, \(\sigma\sqrt n\) for a total.
Example 5 — Linear combinations of independent normals (sums & differences) [Ch-3 exercises + tut 3]

(i) Egg weights are \(\mathcal N(60,6^2)\) g, independent. A basket has 10 eggs. (ii) Two independently sampled people each weigh \(\mathcal N(75,10^2)\) kg. (iii) Men's heights \(\mathcal N(176,8^2)\), women's \(\mathcal N(162,7^2)\) cm.

(i) Total of 10 eggs \(Y=X_1+\cdots+X_{10}\). Mean and (by independence) variance add:

E[Y]=10×60=600 Var(Y)=10×6²=360 Y ~ N(600,360) P(Y > 630) = P(Z > (630−600)/√360) = P(Z > 1.581) = 0.0569

(ii) Two people together \(X+Y\sim\mathcal N(150,\,200)\):

P(X+Y > 180) = P(Z > (180−150)/√200) = P(Z > 2.121) = 0.0169

(iii) Difference man − woman. Means subtract; variances add:

Mean = 176−162 = 14 cm Var = 8²+7² = 113 sd = √113 = 10.63 cm

In (iii) do not subtract variances. \(\text{Var}(Y-X)=(-1)^2\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2=\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2\). Subtracting gives \(\sqrt{15}=3.9\) — a classic wrong answer.

§4 · Exam question types

Type A — Joint table → marginals, moments, covariance, correlation, independence

Looks like: a 2-way table of counts or probabilities (possibly with a parameter to solve first), then a chain of parts (a)–(e). Mark range: 12–16 (a whole question).

Recipe
  1. If a parameter appears, set \(\sum f=1\) and solve for it first.
  2. Marginals: row/column totals (÷ \(n\) for counts).
  3. \(\mathbb E[X],\mathbb E[Y]\) from marginals; \(\mathbb E[X^2],\mathbb E[Y^2]\) for variances; SDs.
  4. \(\mathbb E[XY]=\sum xy\,f(x,y)\) (skip cells with \(x=0\) or \(y=0\)).
  5. \(\text{Cov}=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]\); \(\varrho=\text{Cov}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)\).
  6. Independence: check the product rule cell-by-cell (or one counterexample).

Real examples: 2025 Q2 (screen × sleep, 16 marks), 2024 Q1 (tank leakage, 12 marks), Tut 3 Q3, Ch-3 exercises Q3 (independent case).

Type B — Test of association from a table (χ² crossover)

Looks like: "is there an association between [factor] and [factor]?" with a table of counts. Mark range: 5–6.

Recipe
  1. State H₀ (independent / no association) and H₁ (associated), in words.
  2. Expected counts \(E_{ij}=\text{row}\times\text{col}/n\).
  3. \(\chi^2=\sum(O-E)^2/E\); df \(=(r-1)(c-1)\).
  4. Compare to \(\chi^2\) table / report \(P\)-value; conclude in context.

Real examples: 2025 Q2(d) (5 marks), 2024 Q5(e) frog type × croaks (6 marks), 2023 Q5(e) toothpaste × age group (5 marks). Full method: ch11-chi-squared.html.

Type C — Linear combinations of normals (sum / difference / scaled)

Looks like: independent normal quantities combined (total weight, cost = price×amount, difference of two measurements, °F→°C conversion). Mark range: 3–6.

Recipe
  1. Write each variable's \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\).
  2. Mean of the combination: linearity. Variance: \(\sum b_i^2\sigma_i^2\) (variances add for ±).
  3. Result is normal (Thm 3.6); standardise and read the \(Z\)-table.

Real examples: Ch-3 exercises Q1, Q2, Q6; Tut 3 Q4 (reagent cost sd = $63), Q5 (height difference sd = 10.63).

Type D — CLT / large-sample approximation

Looks like: "sum/average of \(n\) independent…", "approximate the probability…", or "for large \(n\), what is the (approximate) distribution of the estimator?" Mark range: 2–4.

Recipe
  1. Identify \(\mu,\sigma^2\) of one observation and the sample size \(n\).
  2. Mean: \(\bar X\approx\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\). Total: \(S_n\approx\mathcal N(n\mu,n\sigma^2)\).
  3. Standardise and use the \(Z\)-table. (Binomial: \(\mathcal N(np,np(1-p))\).)

Real examples: notes Ex 3.4–3.6; the "approximate distribution of \(\hat\theta\) when \(n\) is large" parts in 2024 Q2(d) and 2023 Q2(c) are CLT-for-estimators.

Type E — Continuous joint pdf (independence, E[XY], covariance)

Looks like: a formula \(f(x,y)\) on some region; verify/refute independence, find marginals by integrating, compute \(\mathbb E[XY]\) or \(\text{Cov}\). Mark range: 4–8.

Recipe
  1. Marginal \(f_X(x)=\int f(x,y)\,dy\) (and symmetrically for \(Y\)).
  2. Independent iff \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) on the whole region (region shape matters — a non-rectangular support forces dependence).
  3. \(\mathbb E[XY]=\iint xy\,f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\); then \(\text{Cov}=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]\).

Real examples: Ch-3 exercises Q4 (\(2e^{-x}e^{-2y}\), independent), Q7 (uniform on the unit disc — \(\text{Cov}=0\) but not independent, because the support is a disc).

§5 · Wording triggers

The single highest-value table on this page. Match the phrase, fire the method.

When the exam says…→ do this
"joint frequency table … determine the marginal pmf of \(X\) (or \(Y\))" Row / column sums ÷ n. \(f_X(x)=\)(row total)/\(n\), \(f_Y(y)=\)(col total)/\(n\). Check they each sum to 1.
"determine the expectation of \(X\) and \(Y\)" \(\mathbb E[X]=\sum x f_X(x)\) from the marginal. Also compute \(\mathbb E[X^2]\) now — you'll need it next part.
"determine the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\)" \(\varrho=\dfrac{\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\), all pieces from the table. Get \(\mathbb E[XY]=\sum xy f(x,y)\) (only \(x,y\ne0\) cells matter), \(\sigma\) from \(\mathbb E[X^2]-\mathbb E[X]^2\).
"are \(X\) and \(Y\) independent?" Check \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) for all cells to confirm; one failing cell to reject. Never use \(\text{Cov}=0\) as the argument.
"test whether \(X\) is associated with \(Y\)" / "test of association" (counts) χ² test of independence. \(E=\text{row}\times\text{col}/n\), \(\chi^2=\sum(O-E)^2/E\), df \(=(r-1)(c-1)\). See Ch 11.
"perform an appropriate statistical test … report the test statistic, \(P\)-value, and a conclusion in context" State H₀/H₁ (association) → compute \(\chi^2\) → compare to table / \(P\)-value → one plain-English sentence about the actual variables.
"sum / average of \(n\) independent…" or "for large \(n\), the approximate distribution" CLT. \(\bar X\approx\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\); \(S_n\approx\mathcal N(n\mu,n\sigma^2)\). Standardise, read \(Z\)-table.
"approximate the probability that the mean exceeds…" CLT for the mean: \(P(\bar X>c)=P\!\big(Z>\tfrac{c-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\big)\). Divide by \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), not \(\sigma\).
"total / combined weight of \(n\) independent normal…" Exactly normal (Thm 3.6): \(\mathcal N(n\mu,n\sigma^2)\). Standardise with sd \(=\sigma\sqrt n\).
"difference between two independent [normal] measurements" Mean subtracts, variance adds: \(\mathcal N(\mu_1-\mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)\).
"cost = \$a per unit of \(X\) and \$b per unit of \(Y\); sd of total cost?" \(\text{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2\) (independent). Take \(\sqrt{\ }\).
"the average of a large number of iid draws tends to…" LLN — the average converges to \(\mu\). (Concept part, no calculation.)
"probability that a binomial count is at least \(k\), \(n\) large" Normal approx: \(\text{Bin}(n,p)\approx\mathcal N(np,np(1-p))\) (valid if \(np>5\) and \(n(1-p)>5\)).

§6 · Traps & marks lost

"Cov = 0 ⇒ independent" is FALSE. Zero covariance only rules out linear dependence. The unit-disc example (Ch-3 Q7) has \(\text{Cov}=0\) yet is dependent. For the independence part you must check the product rule \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\).
Subtracting variances for a difference. \(\text{Var}(X-Y)=\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2\), NOT \(\sigma_X^2-\sigma_Y^2\). The coefficient \(-1\) is squared. Every year this loses marks in height/measurement-difference questions.
Reconstructing the table from margins. You cannot rebuild the interior of a joint table from row/column totals alone — infinitely many tables share the same margins (notes p.56). Don't try.
Including zero cells in \(\mathbb E[XY]\)… or forgetting the non-zero ones. Cells with \(x=0\) or \(y=0\) contribute nothing, so the sum is short — but you must include every cell where both are non-zero. Missing one (e.g. the \(Y=2\) column) silently corrupts the covariance.
CLT standardisation mix-up. For a mean divide by \(\sigma/\sqrt n\); for a total/sum divide by \(\sigma\sqrt n\). Using \(\sigma\) alone (forgetting \(n\)) is the most common CLT error.
CLT is about \(\bar X\), NOT the data. The CLT does not say your data become normal as you collect more of them — a skewed population stays skewed no matter how big \(n\) is. What turns normal is the distribution of the sample mean/total. So "the data are approximately normal for large \(n\)" is wrong; "the sample mean is approximately normal for large \(n\)" is right. Standardise \(\bar X\), never the raw observations.
Correlation ≠ causation. A strong \(\varrho\) (or a significant χ²) says the two variables move together, not that one causes the other — a hidden third variable (confounder) or pure coincidence can drive both. In the flagship, "screen use correlates with worse-coded sleep" is not "screens cause bad sleep". If a part asks you to interpret, say "association/evidence of a relationship", never "causes", unless the design was a randomised experiment.
Wrong df in the χ² test. df \(=(r-1)(c-1)\), not \(rc-1\) and not \(n-1\). A 2×3 table has df \(=2\), so the 5% critical value is 5.99 — memorise the shape of that lookup.
Treating an ordinal code as a genuine quantity. Coding sleep quality 0/1/2 lets you compute a correlation, but "0.447" describes the coded numbers, not an intrinsic scale. That's exactly why the exam asks for the χ² test in the association part — quote it as the formal test, and keep the correlation statement to "direction/strength of the linear relationship in the coded values".
Solve for the parameter first. In parametric tables (2024 Q1) every later part depends on \(p\). Set \(\sum f=1\), solve the quadratic, discard the out-of-range root, then proceed. Skipping this makes every subsequent number wrong.
Sig figs. The rubric demands ≥ 3 significant figures. Carry full precision through intermediate steps (don't round \(\mathbb E[XY]\) or the SDs early) and round only the final answer.

§7 · Self-check

1. A joint table has row totals for \(X\) of 30 and 70 out of \(n=100\). What is \(f_X\), and hence \(\mathbb E[X]\) if \(X\in\{0,1\}\)?

\(f_X(0)=0.30,\ f_X(1)=0.70\). \(\mathbb E[X]=0(0.30)+1(0.70)=0.70\).

2. You find \(\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0\) for a table. Can you conclude \(X,Y\) are independent?

No. Zero covariance rules out only linear association. Independence requires \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) for every cell — check the product rule.

3. \(X\sim\mathcal N(50,4^2)\), \(Y\sim\mathcal N(45,3^2)\), independent. Distribution of \(X-Y\)?

Mean \(=50-45=5\); variance \(=4^2+3^2=25\) (variances add). So \(X-Y\sim\mathcal N(5,\,25)\), sd \(=5\).

4. Widget masses have \(\mu=20\) g, \(\sigma=5\) g (non-normal). For \(n=64\), approximate \(P(\bar X \lt 19)\).

CLT: \(\bar X\approx\mathcal N(20,\,5^2/64)\), sd \(=5/8=0.625\). \(P(\bar X \lt 19)=P\big(Z \lt (19-20)/0.625\big)=P(Z \lt -1.60)=0.0548.\)

5. A 2×4 contingency table of counts. What is the df for the χ² test of independence, and how do you get expected counts?

df \(=(2-1)(4-1)=3\). Expected count in a cell \(=\)(its row total)\(\times\)(its column total)\(/n\).

6. From a table \(\mathbb E[X]=0.667\), \(\mathbb E[Y]=1.10\), \(\mathbb E[XY]=0.9167\), \(\sigma_X=0.471\), \(\sigma_Y=0.870\). Correlation?

\(\text{Cov}=0.9167-0.667\times1.10=0.1833\); \(\varrho=0.1833/(0.471\times0.870)=0.447\). (This is 2025 Q2(c).)

7. Independent normals cost \$100/L (sd 0.2 L) and \$200/L (sd 0.3 L). SD of total cost?

\(\text{Var}=100^2(0.2^2)+200^2(0.3^2)=400+3600=4000\); sd \(=\sqrt{4000}\approx\$63\). (Tut 3 Q4.)

8. LLN vs CLT — which answers "what does the sample average converge to?" and which "what is the probability the average exceeds a value?"

LLN answers the first (the average converges to \(\mu\)). CLT answers the second (the average is \(\approx\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\), so standardise to get the probability).

Question 1 — Continuous random variable 18 marks

A continuous random variable $X$ has probability density function $$f(x)=\begin{cases} h, & 0\le x \lt 1,\\ h/2, & 1\le x\le 2,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$$ where $h$ is a positive real number.

(a) Determine the value of $h$. 2

A density must integrate to 1 over its support. "Determine the constant" → set $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f=1$ and solve.
The pdf is a step function, so the total area is two rectangles: $$\int_0^1 h\,dx+\int_1^2 \tfrac{h}{2}\,dx = h(1)+\tfrac{h}{2}(1)=\tfrac{3h}{2}=1.$$ $$\Rightarrow\; h=\tfrac{2}{3}.$$
$h=\dfrac{2}{3}\approx 0.667$.
Sanity check: $\tfrac23(1)+\tfrac13(1)=1$. Always confirm the area equals 1 before moving on — it is used in every later part.

(b) Determine the cumulative distribution function $F(x)$. 5

"Determine the CDF" for a piecewise pdf → integrate the pdf piece-by-piece, $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$, and carry forward the accumulated area across breakpoints.
For $x\lt 0$: $F(x)=0$.
For $0\le x\lt 1$: $\displaystyle F(x)=\int_0^x \tfrac23\,dt=\tfrac23 x$. (At $x=1$ this reaches $\tfrac23$.)
For $1\le x\le 2$: start from the accumulated $\tfrac23$, then add the second piece: $$F(x)=\tfrac23+\int_1^x \tfrac13\,dt=\tfrac23+\tfrac13(x-1).$$ For $x\gt 2$: $F(x)=1$. Check: at $x=2$, $\tfrac23+\tfrac13(1)=1$. ✓
Final CDF $$F(x)=\begin{cases}0,& x\lt 0\\[2pt] \tfrac23 x,& 0\le x\lt 1\\[2pt] \tfrac23+\tfrac13(x-1),& 1\le x\le 2\\[2pt] 1,& x\gt 2\end{cases}$$
The single most common lost mark: forgetting to add the $\tfrac23$ carried from the first interval, giving $\tfrac13(x-1)$ alone. Then $F(2)=\tfrac13\ne 1$ — a dead giveaway. Always verify $F$ reaches 1 at the top of the support.

(c) Determine the expectation of $X$. 4

"Expectation of a continuous RV" → $\mathbb E X=\int x f(x)\,dx$ (formula sheet #22), integrate over each piece.
$$\mathbb E X=\int_0^1 x\,h\,dx+\int_1^2 x\,\tfrac h2\,dx = h\left[\tfrac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1+\tfrac h2\left[\tfrac{x^2}{2}\right]_1^2 = h\cdot\tfrac12+\tfrac h2\cdot\tfrac32=\tfrac{5h}{4}.$$ With $h=\tfrac23$: $\mathbb E X=\tfrac54\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac{10}{12}=\tfrac{5}{6}$.
$\mathbb E X=\dfrac56\approx 0.833$.

(d) Determine the standard deviation of $X$. 4

"Standard deviation" → get $\mathbb E X^2$, then $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E X^2-(\mathbb E X)^2$ (formula #26a), then take the square root.
$$\mathbb E X^2=\int_0^1 x^2 h\,dx+\int_1^2 x^2\tfrac h2\,dx=h\cdot\tfrac13+\tfrac h2\cdot\tfrac{7}{3}=\tfrac{3h}{2}=\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac23=1.$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=1-\left(\tfrac56\right)^2=1-\tfrac{25}{36}=\tfrac{11}{36}\approx0.3056.$$ $$\text{s.d.}(X)=\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{36}}=\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}{6}\approx 0.553.$$
$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\tfrac{11}{36}\approx0.306$, s.d. $\approx 0.553$.
Do not report the variance when the question asks for the standard deviation — you lose the final mark. Take the square root, and keep $\ge 3$ sig figs.

(e) Determine the inter-quartile range for $X$. 3

"IQR" → solve $F(x)=0.25$ for $Q_1$ and $F(x)=0.75$ for $Q_3$, then $\text{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1$. Use the CDF from (b) and pick the correct piece for each quartile.
$Q_1$: since $F$ reaches $\tfrac23\approx0.667$ only at $x=1$, the quartile $0.25$ lies in the first piece: $$\tfrac23 Q_1=0.25\;\Rightarrow\;Q_1=0.375.$$ $Q_3$: $0.75\gt \tfrac23$, so $Q_3$ is in the second piece: $$\tfrac23+\tfrac13(Q_3-1)=0.75\;\Rightarrow\;\tfrac13(Q_3-1)=0.0833\;\Rightarrow\;Q_3=1.25.$$ $$\text{IQR}=1.25-0.375=0.875.$$
$Q_1=0.375$, $Q_3=1.25$, $\text{IQR}=0.875$.
Check which interval each quartile falls in first. Because $F(1)=\tfrac23$, any probability above $0.667$ (like $Q_3$) is in the second piece — plugging $0.75$ into $\tfrac23x$ would wrongly give $x=1.125$.

(f) Derive $\operatorname{Var}(\overline X-\overline Y)$ for independent samples of sizes $m,n$ with common variance $\sigma^2$. 4

"Derive … including justification of key steps" → an algebra proof. Use independence (variance of a sum = sum of variances, #52) and the scaling rule $\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ (#26b).
Step 1 — variance of one sample mean. Since $X_1,\dots,X_m$ are independent, each with variance $\sigma^2$, $$\operatorname{Var}(\overline X)=\operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\tfrac1m\sum_{i=1}^m X_i\Big)=\tfrac1{m^2}\sum_{i=1}^m\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\tfrac1{m^2}\,(m\sigma^2)=\tfrac{\sigma^2}{m}.$$ Likewise $\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$.
Step 2 — combine. $\overline X$ and $\overline Y$ use disjoint, independent samples, so $\operatorname{Cov}(\overline X,\overline Y)=0$ and $$\operatorname{Var}(\overline X-\overline Y)=\operatorname{Var}(\overline X)+(-1)^2\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y)=\frac{\sigma^2}{m}+\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$
$\operatorname{Var}(\overline X-\overline Y)=\sigma^2\!\left(\dfrac1m+\dfrac1n\right)$.
Examiners want the named justifications: (i) independence lets variances add, (ii) the $\tfrac1{m^2}$ comes from the squared constant, (iii) the minus sign squares to $+1$. State each explicitly. This exact derivation reappears in 2022 Q2(a).

(a) Marginal pmf of $X$. 2

"Marginal of $X$" → sum each column over $Y$ (formula #45 logic: $f_X(x)=\sum_y f(x,y)$).
$$P(X{=}0)=0.42+0.18=0.60,\quad P(X{=}1)=0.175+0.075=0.25,\quad P(X{=}2)=0.105+0.045=0.15.$$
$f_X:\ (0.60,\ 0.25,\ 0.15)$ for $X=0,1,2$. Sums to 1. ✓

(b) Marginal pmf of $Y$. 2

"Marginal of $Y$" → sum each row over $X$.
$$P(Y{=}0)=0.42+0.175+0.105=0.70,\qquad P(Y{=}1)=0.18+0.075+0.045=0.30.$$
$f_Y:\ P(Y{=}0)=0.70,\ P(Y{=}1)=0.30$.

(c) Expectation and standard deviation of $X$, and separately of $Y$. 5

Use the marginals from (a)/(b): $\mathbb E X=\sum x f_X(x)$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E X^2-(\mathbb E X)^2$.
$X$: $\mathbb E X=0(0.6)+1(0.25)+2(0.15)=0.55$; $\mathbb E X^2=0+1(0.25)+4(0.15)=0.85$; $\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.85-0.55^2=0.5475$, s.d.$=\sqrt{0.5475}=0.740$.
$Y$ (Bernoulli, $p=0.3$): $\mathbb E Y=0.30$; $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=p(1-p)=0.3(0.7)=0.21$, s.d.$=\sqrt{0.21}=0.458$.
$\mathbb E X=0.550$, s.d.$(X)=0.740$; $\;\mathbb E Y=0.300$, s.d.$(Y)=0.458$.
$Y$ is Bernoulli, so $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=p(1-p)$ is instant (formula #30) — no need to build a table.

(d) Covariance and correlation between $X$ and $Y$. 3

"Covariance" → $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E XY-\mathbb E X\,\mathbb E Y$ (#50); "correlation" → divide by the two s.d.'s (#49).
Only cells with $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$ contribute to $\mathbb E XY$: $$\mathbb E XY=(1)(1)(0.075)+(2)(1)(0.045)=0.075+0.090=0.165.$$ $$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0.165-(0.55)(0.30)=0.165-0.165=0.$$ $$\rho(X,Y)=\frac{0}{(0.740)(0.458)}=0.$$
$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$, $\;\rho(X,Y)=0$.

(e) Are $X$ and $Y$ independent? Give reasons. 3

"Are they independent?" → independence requires $f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)$ for every cell (#47), not merely zero covariance.
Check the factorisation against the marginals $(0.6,0.25,0.15)$ and $(0.7,0.3)$:

This is a deliberate "gotcha": zero covariance does not by itself prove independence. Here they happen to be independent, but you must verify the full factorisation to earn the marks — never argue "$\operatorname{Cov}=0\Rightarrow$ independent". (The converse — independent $\Rightarrow \operatorname{Cov}=0$ — is always true, #50.)

(f) If 1000 shoppers are surveyed, use the CLT to approximate $P(\text{fewer than }130\text{ go to the beach both days})$. 6

"Use the central limit theorem to approximate a count out of $n$" → the count is Binomial; approximate $S\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,p)$ by $\mathsf N(np,np(1-p))$ (formula #58), standardise, read $\Phi$ from the normal table.
"Both days" means $X=2$, with $p=P(X{=}2)=0.15$. Let $S$ = number of the 1000 shoppers who go both days, $S\sim\mathsf{Bin}(1000,0.15)$.
Mean $=np=150$; variance $=np(1-p)=1000(0.15)(0.85)=127.5$; s.d.$=\sqrt{127.5}=11.29$. $$P(S\lt 130)\approx P\!\left(Z\lt \frac{130-150}{11.29}\right)=P(Z\lt -1.771).$$ From tables: $\Phi(1.77)=0.9616$, so $P(Z\lt -1.77)=1-0.9616=0.0384$.
$P(S\lt 130)\approx 0.0384$ (about 3.8%).
With a continuity correction ($129.5$): $z=-1.816$, $P\approx0.0347$; the exact binomial is $0.0328$. Any of these earns full marks — the CLT formula on the sheet (#58) does not require the correction, so $0.0384$ is the "expected" answer. State which you used.
Identify the right event: "both days" is $X=2$ only ($p=0.15$), not "at least one day". Using the wrong $p$ cascades into every later number.

Question 3 — Two-sample proportions 18 marks

Two designs of tension link. It matters that ultimate tensile strength exceeds 1000 MPa. In a random sample of 50 links of each design, 30 of design 1 and 19 of design 2 exceeded 1000 MPa. Is there evidence of a difference between the two designs in the proportion exceeding 1000 MPa?

(a) Describe a relevant hypothesis test and check its conditions. 3

"Difference between two proportions" from two independent samples → two-sample test for proportions (two-sample binomial / two-proportion $z$-test, formula #74).
Test: a two-sample test for a difference in proportions, using the normal approximation to the binomial.
Conditions: (i) two independent random samples; (ii) each response is a binary "exceeds 1000 MPa / not"; (iii) large enough samples for the normal approximation — the usual rule is $m\hat p,\,m(1-\hat p),\,n\hat p,\,n(1-\hat p)\ge 5$. Here the smallest count is $50(0.38)=19$ and $50(1-0.62)=19$, all $\ge5$, so the approximation is valid.
"50 links of each design" and two independent groups → two-sample, not one-sample. The word "difference" (not "greater") signals a two-sided test.

(b) State the hypotheses in mathematical form and explain the notation. 3

Let $p_1$ and $p_2$ be the true (population) proportions of links of design 1 and design 2, respectively, whose tensile strength exceeds 1000 MPa. $$H_0:\;p_1=p_2 \qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_1:\;p_1\ne p_2.$$
"Evidence of a difference" → two-sided $H_1$ ($\ne$). Always define $p_1,p_2$ in words tied to the context, or you lose an explanation mark.

(c) Determine the test statistic and $p$-value. 4

Two-sample binomial test statistic (#74) uses the pooled proportion $\hat p=\frac{X+Y}{m+n}$ under $H_0$.
$\hat p_1=\tfrac{30}{50}=0.60$, $\hat p_2=\tfrac{19}{50}=0.38$, pooled $\hat p=\tfrac{30+19}{100}=0.49$. $$Z=\frac{\hat p_1-\hat p_2}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\left(\frac1m+\frac1n\right)}}=\frac{0.22}{\sqrt{0.49(0.51)(\tfrac1{50}+\tfrac1{50})}}=\frac{0.22}{\sqrt{0.009996}}=\frac{0.22}{0.09998}=2.200.$$ $p$-value (two-sided): from tables $\Phi(2.20)=0.9861$, so $$p=2\big(1-\Phi(2.20)\big)=2(0.0139)=0.0278.$$
$Z=2.20$, $\;p\text{-value}=0.0278$.
The test statistic uses the pooled $\hat p=0.49$ in the standard error, but the CI in part (e) uses the separate $\hat p_1,\hat p_2$. Mixing these up is the classic error here.

(d) At the 5% level, make a conclusion about the hypotheses and in context. 4

Since $p\text{-value}=0.0278\lt 0.05$, we reject $H_0$.
There is significant evidence at the 5% level of a difference between the two designs in the proportion of links whose tensile strength exceeds 1000 MPa. The sample suggests design 1 produces a higher proportion of links exceeding 1000 MPa (60% vs 38%).
Two marks are for the statistical decision ("reject $H_0$, $p\lt0.05$"), two for translating it into the engineering story. Do both.

(e) Produce a 99% confidence interval for the difference in proportions. 4

"CI for a difference in two proportions" → formula #67 with separate $\hat p$'s (no pooling in a CI).
$$(\hat p_1-\hat p_2)\pm q\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{m}+\frac{\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)}{n}},$$ where $q$ is the $0.995$ quantile of $\mathsf N(0,1)$. From tables: $\Phi(2.58)\approx0.9951$, so $q=2.58$ (exact $2.576$). $$\text{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{0.6(0.4)}{50}+\frac{0.38(0.62)}{50}}=\sqrt{0.0048+0.004712}=\sqrt{0.009512}=0.09753.$$ $$0.22\pm 2.58(0.09753)=0.22\pm 0.2516=(-0.0316,\ 0.472).$$
99% CI for $p_1-p_2$: $(-0.0316,\ 0.472)$ (using $q=2.58$; with $q=2.576$: $(-0.0312,\ 0.471)$).
Note the tension: the 99% CI contains 0, yet we rejected at the 5% level in (d). No contradiction — a 99% CI corresponds to a 1% two-sided test, and at the stricter 1% level we would not reject ($p=0.0278\gt0.01$). Different confidence levels, different conclusions.

(f) State the ANOVA assumptions and discuss whether they hold. 2

Discussion: eggs from randomly selected nests → independence reasonable. Boxplots look roughly symmetric with no gross outliers → normality plausible. The s.d.'s (0.84, 1.21, 0.94) give a largest-to-smallest variance ratio of $1.21^2/0.84^2\approx 2.1$, well under the rule-of-thumb of 4, so equal variance is acceptable.
A quick, defensible equal-variance check: ratio of the largest to smallest sample variance $\lt 4$ ⇒ pooling/ANOVA is fine.

Not really. The scatter shows the spread of price increasing with year (a fan/funnel shape) and slight curvature. "Fanning out" in a scatter = non-constant variance. Naming that specific defect is what earns the mark.

(c) Using the log model, estimate the price of a RAV4 manufactured in 2011. 2

Model is $\widehat{\log(\text{Price})}=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\,\text{Year}$. Predict on the log scale, then back-transform with $e^{(\cdot)}$.
$$\widehat{\log(\text{Price})}=-157.4+0.08301(2011)=9.533.$$ $$\widehat{\text{Price}}=e^{9.533}\approx \$13{,}800.$$
≈ \$13,800 (using the printed coefficients).
You must exponentiate — the model predicts $\log(\text{Price})$, not price. Forgetting the back-transform is an automatic lost mark. (Rounding note: the printed 4-figure intercept is sensitive; carrying full precision from R gives ≈\$13,200. Either is accepted if the method is shown.)

(d) Interpret the estimated slope of the log model. 1

On the log scale each extra year adds $0.08301$ to $\log(\text{Price})$; back-transformed, price is multiplied by $e^{0.08301}=1.0866$.
Each additional year of manufacture is associated with roughly an 8.7% increase in the predicted sale price.
For a $\log(y)\sim x$ model, a slope $\beta$ means "each unit of $x$ multiplies $y$ by $e^{\beta}$" ≈ a $100\beta\%$ change for small $\beta$.

(e) Is there evidence that sale price is associated with year of manufacture? 3

"Evidence of association" in regression → test $H_0:\beta_1=0$ vs $H_1:\beta_1\ne0$ using the slope's $t$-statistic.
$$t=\frac{\hat\beta_1}{\text{s.e.}(\hat\beta_1)}=\frac{0.08301}{0.001028}=80.7\quad\text{on }990\text{ df}.$$ This is enormous; the $p$-value is $\lt 2\times10^{-16}$.
There is overwhelming evidence ($p\lt 0.001$) that year of manufacture is associated with (log) sale price: newer RAV4s sell for more.

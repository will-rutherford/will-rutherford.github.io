Sheet walkthrough · Expectation & Variance items 21–26

21–23\(\mathbb E X=\sum_x x f(x)\) (discrete), \(\int x f(x)\,dx\) (continuous), \(\mathbb E g(X)=\sum g(x)f(x)\)"find the expected value / mean of \(X\) (or of \(g(X)\))"
24\(\mathbb E(aX+b)=a\mathbb E X+b\); \(\mathbb E(g+h)=\mathbb E g+\mathbb E h\)linearity — expectation always passes through sums & linear maps
25\(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E(X-\mu)^2\)definition of variance
26a\(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E X^2-(\mathbb E X)^2\)the computational shortcut — find \(\mathbb E X^2\) then subtract \(\mu^2\)
26b\(\operatorname{Var}(a+bX)=b^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\)scaling: constants add nothing, multipliers square
26c\(\text{s.d.}(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}\)convert variance ↔ standard deviation
Usage example (items 21 & 26a). \(X\): \(\mathbb P(0)=0.5,\mathbb P(1)=0.3,\mathbb P(2)=0.2\).
\(\mathbb E X=0.7\); \(\mathbb E X^2=0(.5)+1(.3)+4(.2)=1.1\); \(\operatorname{Var}=1.1-0.7^2=0.61\).
Item 26b: a common slip is \(\operatorname{Var}(a+bX)=b\operatorname{Var}(X)\). It is \(b^2\). And \(\operatorname{Var}(X-Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) for independent \(X,Y\) — a plus, from item 52.

Sheet walkthrough · Statistics & Confidence Intervals items 60–68

This is exam gold. Every CI part maps to exactly one of items 62–68. Match the data type to the template, plug the right quantile from the tables.

Items 60–61 · The building blocks

\(\bar X=\dfrac1n\sum X_i\) (60)  ·  \(S^2=\dfrac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\dfrac1{n-1}\big(\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2\big)\) (61)
Use the right-hand form of \(S^2\) with your calculator: accumulate \(\sum X_i\) and \(\sum X_i^2\), then \(S^2=\tfrac1{n-1}(\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2)\). The FX82's SD mode gives \(S\) directly — but know this by hand as backup.

The single biggest CI trap: means use a t-quantile, proportions use a z-quantile. Items 62/65/66 → t-table. Items 67/68 → Normal table. Mixing them up is a guaranteed lost mark, every year.
Usage example (item 62 · mean CI). \(n=16,\ \bar X=52,\ S=6\), 95%:
\(q=t_{0.975,15}=2.131\); CI \(=52\pm2.131\cdot\tfrac{6}{\sqrt{16}}=52\pm3.20=(48.80,\ 55.20)\).
Usage example (item 67 · proportion CI). \(X=45\) of \(n=200\), 95%:
\(\hat P=0.225,\ q=1.96\); CI \(=0.225\pm1.96\sqrt{\tfrac{0.225\cdot0.775}{200}}=(0.167,\ 0.283)\).
Usage example (item 63 · variance CI). \(n=10,\ S^2=25\), 95%:
\(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,9}=2.70,\ q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,9}=19.02\); CI \(=\big(\tfrac{9\cdot25}{19.02},\tfrac{9\cdot25}{2.70}\big)=(11.83,\ 83.32)\). Note the bigger quantile makes the lower bound.
"\(1-\alpha/2\) quantile" decode: for a 95% CI, \(\alpha=0.05\), so you want the \(0.975\) quantile. For 99% → \(0.995\); for 90% → \(0.95\). This is the number you look up in the column headers of the t / χ² tables.

Sheet walkthrough · Hypothesis Testing items 69–77

Items 69–71 · The scaffolding

  • 69 — the 7 steps: model · hypotheses · test statistic · its distribution under \(H_0\) · observed value · P-value · accept/reject. Write these headers as your answer skeleton.
  • 70 — P-value = prob (under \(H_0\)) the statistic is as or more extreme than observed. "Extreme" is defined by \(H_1\) → sets one-vs-two tail.
  • 71 — Type I = reject a true \(H_0\); Type II = fail to reject a false \(H_0\); Power = \(\mathbb P(\text{reject}\mid H_1\text{ true})\).

Usage example (item 72 · one-sample t-test). \(H_0{:}\ \mu=50\), data \(n=16,\bar X=52,S=6\):
\(T=\dfrac{52-50}{6/\sqrt{16}}=1.333\) on \(t_{15}\); \(|T|\lt t_{0.975,15}=2.131\) → do not reject at 5%.
Usage example (item 76 · two-proportion z). Note the pooled \(\hat P\) in the test (item 76) differs from the unpooled se in the CI (item 68). Testing uses pooled; the interval does not.
Item 75 vs 74: choosing equal-variance (pooled) when variances clearly differ, or vice-versa. If the question says "assume equal variances" → 74. If it says "do not assume" or the sample SDs are very different → 75. Getting the wrong one changes both the statistic and the df.
Item 73/76 use a z statistic (Normal table). Item 72/74/75 use t. Same mean-vs-proportion split as the CIs — keep it straight.

Sheet walkthrough · Linear Models: ANOVA & Regression items 78–85

Items 78–80 · One-factor ANOVA

Model (78): \(Y_{ik}=\mu_i+\varepsilon_{ik}\), \(\varepsilon\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathsf N(0,\sigma^2)\). Test (79): \(H_0{:}\) all \(\mu_i\) equal, \(F=\dfrac{\text{MSF}}{\text{MSE}}\sim\mathsf F(d-1,\,n-d)\).

Usage example (items 79–80). \(d=3\) groups, \(n=24\), SSF=120, SSE=200:
MSF \(=120/2=60\), MSE \(=200/21=9.524\), \(F=6.30\) on \(\mathsf F(2,21)\); crit \(F_{0.95}(2,21)=3.47\) → reject \(H_0\).

Reading the statistical tables — worked micro-drills

All numbers below verified with scipy.stats. The tables use a single convention you must internalise: they list γ-quantiles, i.e. the value with probability γ to its left. So "the \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile" is a left-tail cumulative probability.

1 · Standard Normal Φ (sheet p. 4)

The table gives \(\Phi(z)=\mathbb P(Z\le z)\) for \(z\ge0\), to 4 dp (leading "0." omitted: entry 9505 means 0.9505). Row = \(z\) to 1 dp, column = second decimal.

Direct lookup. \(\Phi(1.65)\): row 1.6, col .05 → 0.9505. \(\Phi(1.96)\): row 1.9, col .06 → 0.9750.
Left tail / negative z (no negative rows printed): use symmetry \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\).
\(\mathbb P(Z\le-1.5)=1-\Phi(1.5)=1-0.9332=\) 0.0668.
Upper tail. \(\mathbb P(Z\gt1.23)=1-\Phi(1.23)=1-0.8907=\) 0.1093.
Between. \(\mathbb P(0.5\lt Z\lt1.5)=\Phi(1.5)-\Phi(0.5)=0.9332-0.6915=\) 0.2417.
Reverse (find the z for a probability = critical value). Hunt the body for the probability, read off the margins.
Need \(z\) with \(\Phi(z)=0.975\) → body entry 9750 sits at row 1.9, col .06 → \(z=\) 1.96. For \(0.95\): 0.9505 (z=1.65) and 0.9495 (z=1.64) bracket it → \(z\approx\) 1.645.
Memorise the four critical z's so you barely need the table: 90%→1.645, 95%→1.96, 98%→2.33, 99%→2.576. These are the \(q\) in every proportion CI/test (items 67, 68, 73, 76).
The z-table is used for proportions and for Normal-approx probabilities — NOT for a mean CI (that's t). Also: this table only goes to \(z=3.6\) (Φ ≈ 0.9998); beyond that quote "\(\gt0.9999\)".

2 · Student's t (sheet p. 5)

Rows = df; columns = γ-quantile (\(0.90,0.95,0.975,0.99,0.995,\dots\)). Entry = the \(t\)-value with probability γ to its left. Bottom row (∞) is the Normal.

Find \(t_{0.025,14}\) — the value cutting off 0.025 in the upper tail with df 14. Upper 0.025 = lower 0.975, so read df=14, column 0.975 → 2.14 (exact 2.145). This is the \(q\) for a 95% mean CI with \(n=15\).
Find \(t_{0.95,5}\) (the 0.95-quantile, df 5): row 5, col 0.95 → 2.02 (exact 2.015). This is the sheet's own worked example.
Bracket the P-value for \(t=2.31\), df 20, two-tailed. In row 20: 2.09 (col .975) < 2.31 < 2.53 (col .99). So upper-tail prob is between 0.01 and 0.025; two-tailed P-value is between 0.02 and 0.05 (exact 0.0317). Conclusion: reject at 5%, not at 1%.
One-tailed version of the same: P between 0.01 and 0.025 (exact 0.0158).
One-tail vs two-tail rule. Column γ = 0.975 gives the critical value for a two-sided 5% test (α/2 = 0.025 each tail) and for a one-sided 2.5% test. For a one-sided 5% test use column 0.95. Always translate α into a left-tail quantile first.
Interpolation. df not listed (e.g. 45)? The convention in this course is conservative: use the nearest smaller df in the table (round df down → 40). This gives a slightly wider CI / larger critical value, never anti-conservative. Do not linearly interpolate unless asked.
Reading \(t\) as if columns were tail areas. Column headers are cumulative (left) probabilities γ, not α. \(t_{0.99}\) is bigger than \(t_{0.95}\) — if your "critical value" got smaller as you demanded more confidence, you read the wrong column.

3 · Chi-squared χ² (sheet p. 6)

Rows = df; columns = γ-quantiles (\(.005,.01,.025,.05,.10,.25,.75,.90,.95,.975,.99,.995\)). Asymmetric distribution → both tails are tabulated separately.

Find \(\chi^2_{0.05,2}\) — upper-tail 0.05 critical value, df 2. Upper 0.05 = the 0.95 quantile: row 2, col .950 → 5.99 (exact 5.991). This is the critical value for a χ² test with 2 df at 5%.
Find \(\chi^2_{0.05,9}\): row 9, col .950 → 16.9 (exact 16.919).
Variance-CI quantiles (item 63). For 95%, df 9 you need both: \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,9}\) = col .025 → 2.70; \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,9}\) = col .975 → 19.0 (exact 19.02).
Bracket a P-value. Test stat \(=9.24\) on df 5: row 5 has 9.24 exactly at col .90, so \(\mathbb P(X\le9.24)=0.90\) → upper-tail P-value \(=\) 0.10 (exact 0.0999). Don't reject at 5%.
χ² tests are one-tailed (upper). Only large \(\sum(O-E)^2/E\) signals a bad fit, so you always compare against the upper critical value (col .95 for 5%). The lower columns (.005–.10) exist only for variance CIs — do not use them for a goodness-of-fit/independence test.
df for the χ² test (item 77) is \((r-1)(c-1)\) for a contingency table, or (categories − 1 − #estimated params) for goodness-of-fit — not \(n-1\). Wrong df → wrong row → wrong conclusion.

4 · F-distribution (sheet pp. 7–9)

Three pages, one per γ (0.99, 0.975, 0.95). Within a page: columns = numerator df \(m\), rows = denominator df \(n\). Entry = the γ-quantile of \(\mathsf F_{m,n}\). For a 5% ANOVA test you want the 0.95 page (upper 5%).

Find \(F_{0.05}(2,54)\) — upper-5% critical value, \(m=2\) numerator, \(n=54\) denominator. Go to the γ = 0.95 page, column \(m=2\). Row \(n=54\) isn't listed (rows jump 40 → 60): use the conservative smaller-\(n\) row, \(n=40\) → 3.23. (True value 3.17; \(n=60\) gives 3.15.)
Sheet's worked example. \(F_{7,10}\): γ=0.95 page, col \(m=7\), row \(n=10\) → 3.14, i.e. \(\mathbb P(X\le3.14)=0.95\). The 0.975 page gives 3.95; the 0.99 page gives 5.20.
Bracket an ANOVA P-value. Observed \(F=4.0\) on \((3,20)\): 0.95-page value is 3.10, 0.99-page is 4.94. Since \(3.10\lt4.0\lt4.94\), \(0.01\lt P\lt0.05\) → reject at 5%, not at 1%.
Numerator vs denominator order matters: \(m\) = the df on top of the F ratio (Factor/MSF df, \(d-1\)), \(n\) = the bottom (Error/MSE df, \(n_{\text{tot}}-d\)). \(\mathsf F_{2,20}\ne\mathsf F_{20,2}\). Column first, then row.
Only upper tails are tabulated and the pages are the .95/.975/.99 quantiles, so F is treated as a one-tailed upper test (ANOVA rejects only for large F). If you ever need a lower F critical value, use \(F_{\alpha}(m,n)=1/F_{1-\alpha}(n,m)\) — but STAT1301 ANOVA never needs it.
Grabbing the 0.99 page for a 5% test. The page γ is the confidence level of the left tail: 5% test → 0.95 page, 2.5% → 0.975, 1% → 0.99.

What's NOT on the sheet — know these cold

The sheet gives formulas; it never gives decisions, df rules, or words. This is where marks are won and lost.

Decision rules (which formula?)

  • Mean vs proportion → t vs z. The sheet lists both but won't tell you which. Continuous/measured data on a mean → t. Count/"proportion of"/success-fail → z.
  • Equal vs unequal variances (items 65/74 vs 66/75). Decide from the wording ("assume equal") or from the ratio of sample variances. Not stated on the sheet.
  • Paired vs two-sample. The sheet has no paired-test formula. If measurements are paired (before/after, same subject), form differences \(D_i\) and run a one-sample t-test (item 72) on the \(D\)'s. Recognising this is entirely on you.
  • One-tail vs two-tail. Driven by \(H_1\) (\(\ne\) → two-tail; \(\lt\) or \(\gt\) → one-tail). Changes the quantile column and the P-value doubling.

Assumptions to state (examiners award marks for these)

  • t-procedures: data (or the differences) approximately Normal, or \(n\) large (CLT). Items 62/65 say "exact when Normal".
  • Proportion z (67/73): Normal approx valid when \(np_0\) and \(n(1-p_0)\) both \(\ge\) ~5–10.
  • ANOVA: independent groups, equal variances, Normal errors (item 78).
  • χ² test: all expected counts \(E\ge5\); observations independent.

The interpretation sentence (never on the sheet, always worth a mark)

CI template: "We are 95% confident the [parameter, in context] lies between [L] and [U] [units]."
Test template: "Since P = [value] \(\lt/\gt\) 0.05, we [reject / do not reject] \(H_0\); there is [strong / insufficient] evidence that [\(H_1\) in the story's words]."
"Show your working and state conclusions where appropriate. Give at least 3 significant figures." — a bare number with no conclusion sentence, or a 2-sig-fig answer, loses marks even when the arithmetic is right.

Speed drill · "Which formula?" — 10 rapid prompts

Cover the answers. For each, name the sheet item number and the table you'd read. Aim for <10 s each.

1. "A random sample of 25 rods has mean length 4.02 cm, s = 0.11. Give a 95% CI for the true mean length." Item 62 (1-sample mean CI). Quantile \(t_{0.975,24}=2.06\) from the t-table. df = 24.
2. "In 300 trials, 78 were successes. 99% CI for the success probability." Item 67 (1-sample proportion CI). Quantile \(z=2.576\) from the Normal table. It's a proportion → z, not t.
3. "Test whether three fertilisers give different mean yields." One-factor ANOVA: model 78, statistic 79 \(F=\text{MSF}/\text{MSE}\), table 80. Read the F-table (0.95 page) at \((d-1,\,n-d)\).
4. "Are eye colour and handedness associated?" (a two-way count table) Item 77 chi-squared test of independence. \(E=\frac{\text{row}\times\text{col}}{\text{total}}\), df \((r-1)(c-1)\), χ² table upper tail.
5. "Each of 12 subjects measured before and after a drug; test for a mean change." Trick — paired. Not on the sheet as a template: form \(D_i\)=after−before, run one-sample t (item 72) on the \(D\)'s, df = 11, t-table.
6. "95% CI for the variance of a Normally-distributed measurement, n = 20." Item 63. Two quantiles from the χ² table, df 19: \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,19},\ q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,19}\). Bigger quantile → lower bound.
7. "Compare mean income of two cities; sample SDs are 12 and 31 (very different)." Unequal variances → item 66 (CI) / 75 (test). Welch df, or conservative \(\min(m-1,n-1)\), t-table.
8. "P(a Normal(50, 8²) reading exceeds 62)?" Standardize (item 43): \(z=(62-50)/8=1.5\), then \(1-\Phi(1.5)=0.0668\) from the Normal table. No test — just a probability.
9. "Compare proportions of defectives from two machines." Two-sample proportions: CI item 68 (unpooled se) or test item 76 (pooled \(\hat P=\frac{X+Y}{m+n}\)). Normal table for z.
10. "The regression output reports SST = 840, SSE = 210. How much variation is explained?" Item 82: \(R^2=(840-210)/840=0.75\) → 75% explained. Pure plug-in, no table.

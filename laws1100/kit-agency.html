WHICH ILACs TO WRITE — a typical agency question = 2 ILACs (the lecturer: "either 1 or 2 ILACs"). Always ILAC 1 first.
  1. Always → ILAC 1: Did [A] have authority? (express actual → implied actual → apparent). One ILAC 1 per contract / purchase (two purchases = write it twice; usually one is authorised and one is not).
  2. You are advising the principal (e.g. "advise Cristina") or the agent ignored instructions / took a commission → ILAC 2: Did [A] breach fiduciary duties?
  3. You concluded there was NO authority and you are advising the third party (supplier / buyer / store) → ILAC 2 (alt): Is [A] personally liable to [3P]?
  4. Both apply? Write all three, in the order 1 → 2 → 2 (alt).
P = principal (the person the agent acts for). A = agent (the one who signed/bought). 3P = third party (the outsider who dealt with the agent).

Issue 1: Authority of [A] to act for [P]

Issue

Did [A] have the authority to act on behalf of [his/her] principal [P] when [accepting 3P's offer / signing the contract for … / purchasing …], such that [P] is bound to [3P]?

Law

Agency is a legal term used to describe a person (the agent) who acts on behalf of another (the principal) in dealing with a third party. The relationship of principal and agent gives rise to a number of obligations and duties on the part of both the principal and the agent. These duties and obligations are both contractual and fiduciary in nature.

Ways in which an agent can be authorised to act on behalf of a principal include actual authority — express actual authority or implied actual authority — or apparent authority. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract or other legal relationship is between the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability.

Actual authority arises out of a "consensual agreement" between principal and agent "to which they alone are parties": Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express authority is where the principal expressly authorises the agent, in writing (including by deed, such as a power of attorney) or verbally, to act on the principal's behalf. Implied authority is the authority to do all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the principal's express instructions, or needed to give those instructions business efficacy. See: Peterson v Moloney (1951), where implied authority was not found.

For apparent authority, if the principal allows a third party to believe that the agent has authority to act on the principal's behalf, then even if the agent was not actually authorised, the principal will be bound to honour the agent's dealings with the third party. The 3 requirements to prove apparent authority are that: (1) the third party did not know that the agent did not have actual authority; (2) the principal 'held out' the agent as having authority to act on the principal's behalf, for example by appointing the agent to a particular position or allowing them to act in the past; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on the principal's representation when dealing with the agent. See: Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), where a company secretary who hired cars (partly for personal use) bound the company, because hiring cars was within a secretary's usual, and therefore apparent, authority. The representation must come from the principal, not the agent: Essington v Regency (2004).

= Actual authority: P really gave permission (said it = express; it comes naturally with the job = implied). Apparent authority: P never gave permission, but P's own words/conduct made the outsider reasonably think A had it.

Issue 2: Breach of fiduciary duties by [A]

Issue

Did [A] breach any of [his/her] fiduciary obligations towards [P]?

Law

Agents owe fiduciary duties to their principal as a result of the relationship of trust and confidence that exists between them. Examples of these are the duty to follow the principal's lawful instructions, which makes the agent personally liable for harm caused by not doing so: Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830); the duty to communicate information relevant to the agency: John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002); the duty to act in the principal's interests and not in conflict with their own, such as by taking a secret commission or profit: Hewson v Sydney (1968); and the duty to exercise due care, skill and diligence: Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991). These duties could also be imposed as contractual duties under the terms of the agency agreement between the principal and the agent.

Issue 2 (alt): Personal liability of [A] to [3P]

Use only when ILAC 1 found NO authority and you are advising the third party (or the principal wants to know who else the 3P can chase).

Issue

As [A] had no authority to bind [P], can [3P] hold [A] personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

Law

First consider the authority of the agent to act; then, if no authority exists, consider whether the agent can be personally liable to the third party. An agent will be personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed that they were making the contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent did not in fact have authority to act on behalf of the principal; and (3) the third party relied upon the agent's representation, and would not have entered into the contract in its absence. See: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]. The agent may also be liable in the torts of deceit or negligence, as well as for breach of s 18 of the ACL.

= Breach of warranty of authority: A effectively promised "I'm allowed to do this for P" — the promise was false, so A pays.

Filled-in example — Week 12 Q&A: Cristina, Davo and Patrizia

Cristina owns a St Lucia house valued at about $1.2 million and is called overseas for 2 months. She appoints Davo, a real estate agent, in writing, to sell to the first buyer offering over $1 million, and instructs him to put up a sign: "All enquiries to David Barnes at 'Dave's Real Estate', St Lucia." Patrizia sees the sign, inspects and offers $850,000. Davo says nothing about the $1 million instruction, tells her "I am authorized by the owner to accept any offer that I determine to be reasonable", and accepts on Cristina's behalf. Cristina returns and wants to rescind or sue Patrizia for $150,000. Advise Cristina on who will be liable and what remedies may result.

Issue 1: Davo's authority to accept Patrizia's offer

Issue. Did Davo have the authority to act on behalf of his principal Cristina when accepting Patrizia's offer, such that Cristina is bound to Patrizia?

Law. Agency is a legal term used to describe a person (the agent) who acts on behalf of another (the principal) in dealing with a third party. An agent may be authorised by actual authority — express or implied — or by apparent authority. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability. Actual authority arises out of a consensual agreement between principal and agent: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express authority is given by the principal in writing or verbally. Implied authority is the authority to do all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the express instructions: Peterson v Moloney (1951). For apparent authority, if the principal allows a third party to believe the agent has authority, the principal will be bound even if the agent was not actually authorised. The 3 requirements are that: (1) the third party did not know the agent lacked actual authority; (2) the principal held the agent out as having authority; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on that representation. See: Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), where a company secretary's car hire bound the company as it was within his apparent authority. The representation must come from the principal, not the agent: Essington v Regency (2004).

Application. There is a relationship of agent and principal between Cristina and Davo — Cristina is the principal, Davo is the agent and Patrizia is the third party. Cristina gave Davo actual authority. Express authority was given because Cristina, in writing, instructed Davo to sell to the first buyer offering over $1 million. Implied authority was present because putting up the sign, showing Patrizia through the house and giving her information were necessarily incidental to selling it. However, in selling the house for $850,000, Davo acted outside his actual authority; implied authority cannot extend to a price that contradicts the express instruction.

As was the case in Panorama, where hiring cars fell within the secretary's apparent authority, Patrizia would argue that there was apparent authority because: Patrizia did not know that Cristina had limited Davo to offers over $1 million, as Davo said nothing about it; Cristina 'held out' that Davo had authority by appointing him as her selling agent and instructing a sign stating "All enquiries to David Barnes"; and Patrizia relied upon that sign when contacting Dave's Real Estate and making her offer. On the other hand, Cristina will rely on Essington v Regency to argue that Davo's claim to be "authorized … to accept any offer that I determine to be reasonable" came only from the agent. However, Patrizia relies on Cristina's own appointment and sign, not Davo's words alone. Therefore, Davo had the apparent authority to act on Cristina's behalf.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Davo had apparent authority, so Patrizia would be able to enforce the contract against Cristina, and the contract would not be rescinded. Therefore, Cristina would not be entitled to rescind the contract or recover $150,000 from Patrizia, an innocent third party; her remedy lies against Davo.

Issue 2: Davo's fiduciary duties

Issue. Did Davo breach any of his fiduciary obligations towards Cristina?

Law. Agents owe fiduciary duties to their principal as a result of the relationship of trust and confidence that exists between them. Examples are the duty to follow the principal's lawful instructions, failing which the agent is personally liable for the harm caused: Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830); the duty to communicate relevant information: John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002); the duty to act in the principal's interests: Hewson v Sydney (1968); and the duty to exercise due care, skill and diligence: Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991). These duties could also be imposed as contractual duties under the terms of the agency agreement.

Application. It is likely that Davo breached his fiduciary duties to Cristina because: first, Cristina instructed him in writing to sell only for over $1 million, yet he accepted $850,000; second, he did not tell Cristina about the below-threshold offer before accepting it, and nothing suggests she could not be contacted; and third, accepting $850,000 for a house valued at $1.2 million was against her interests and not what a reasonable agent would do. As was the case in Bertram Armstrong v Godfray, where an agent who failed to follow instructions was liable for the harm caused, Davo ignored a clear instruction. On the other hand, Davo may argue he thought the offer "more than reasonable"; however, the instruction was clear and in writing. Since the instructions were in writing, this could also amount to a breach of an express term of the agency agreement, which also amounts to a breach of contract by Davo.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely decide that Patrizia can enforce the contract under apparent authority. However, Davo has breached his fiduciary duties and contractual duties as an agent towards Cristina. Therefore, Cristina would be entitled to damages from Davo for the loss — at least the $150,000 between the sale price and her $1 million minimum.

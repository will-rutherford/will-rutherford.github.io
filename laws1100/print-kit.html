ILAC A · Formation: was a contract formed?

Issue

Was a legally binding contract formed between [A] and [B] for [the sale of the painting]?

Law

A contract is a legally binding promise or agreement. A simple contract can be made verbally, in writing or by conduct, and requires three elements: (1) agreement; (2) intention to create legal relations; and (3) consideration.
Agreement = offer + acceptance. An offer is a proposal by one party to another to immediately enter into a legally binding contract, judged objectively by asking whether a reasonable person in the recipient's shoes would believe it to be an offer. It must be distinguished from an invitation to treat, an invitation to deal such as most advertisements, goods displayed in shops (Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots), auctions, tenders and price lists. However, an advertisement can be an offer to the world at large: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, where an ad promising £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball as directed and still caught influenza, backed by £1,000 deposited "showing our sincerity", was an offer that Mrs Carlill accepted by using the ball. Acceptance is a final and unqualified assent to all the terms of the offer, judged objectively; a qualified acceptance is a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer. Acceptance must be communicated, and silence is not acceptance: Felthouse v Bindley.
Intention. The parties must intend to create legal relations. Traditionally courts presumed commercial agreements were binding and social/domestic agreements were not (Wakeling v Ripley, where the court found family members intended to be bound because Ripley's sister and brother-in-law gave up their life in England to move to Sydney to care for him in return for a home and inheritance). The current approach considers the agreement on its own merits, with no presumption, looking at the parties' conduct, the context, the language used, the value of any consideration and the nature of the relationship: Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community of SA.
Consideration. The promisee must provide consideration, the price paid or promised for the other's promise (money, goods, services, refraining from something, or a promise to do any of these): Carlill. It need not be adequate, only of some legal value: Thomas v Thomas.

= invitation to treat: "come and make me an offer". Offeror = the one making the offer; offeree = the one receiving it.

ILAC B · Later promise: is it supported by consideration?

Issue

Is [B]'s later promise to [re-frame the painting] enforceable by [A]?

Law

A promise is only enforceable if the promisee has provided consideration for it, and consideration must be sufficient. It cannot be a vague promise (White v Bluett, a son's promise to stop complaining), past consideration, or the performance of an existing contractual obligation. Past consideration is no consideration: Roscorla v Thomas, where, after a horse had been sold and paid for, the seller promised it was sound and free from vice; the buyer could not enforce that promise because the price had been paid before it was made. Performing an existing duty is not consideration: Stilk v Myrick, where sailors promised extra wages for the work they were already bound to do got nothing. However, where the promisor receives something extra, a benefit, or avoids a detriment, the promise may be enforced: Musumeci v Winadell.

ILAC C · Promissory estoppel

Issue

Is [Promisor] estopped from going back on its promise to [Promisee] that […], even though no contract was concluded?

Law

A promise must normally be contained in a contract and supported by consideration to be enforceable. However, equity developed promissory estoppel: when a person, by words or conduct, has led another to believe in a particular state of affairs, they will not be allowed to go back on it where it would be unjust or inequitable to do so: Moorgate v Twitchings. Promissory estoppel allows a promise to be enforced even though the promisee provided no consideration: Walton Stores v Maher, where the Mahers demolished their building and began building a new one to Waltons' specifications for a lease, while Waltons secretly told its solicitors to "go slow" and then pulled out; the High Court held Waltons could not deny a binding agreement. The elements are: (1) a legal relationship exists or is anticipated; (2) the promisor makes a clear and unambiguous promise; (3) the promisee relies on it by changing their circumstances; (4) the promisee will suffer material detriment if the promise is not kept; and (5) it would be unconscionable for the promisor not to keep it. The court may order specific performance or damages: Giumelli v Giumelli.

= estopped: "not allowed to go back on it". Unconscionable: against good conscience, very unfair.

ILAC D · Capacity

Issue

Did [Party] have the legal capacity to enter the contract with [Other], and is the contract enforceable against [him/her]?

Law

Persons under 18 generally lack contractual capacity: Law Reform Act 1995 (Qld) s 17. A minor's contract is enforceable by the minor but not against the minor, except contracts for necessaries, being goods suitable to the minor's condition in life and actual requirements such as food, clothing, education, shelter and medical treatment (Bojczuk v Gregorcewicz), and contracts for beneficial services, such as employment or training that is not unfair, oppressive or harsh (Fawcett v Smethurst), where the court weighs the benefit against the detriment to the minor (Hamilton v Lethbridge). A contract with a person with an intellectual disability or mental illness is voidable if (1) they were incapable of understanding the nature of the contract and (2) the other party knew or ought to have known; the person withdrawing must prove both: Gibbons v Wright. The same two requirements apply to intoxicated persons: Blomley v Ryan.

= voidable: valid until the weaker party chooses to set it aside.

ILAC E · Term or representation (incl. puffery)

Issue

Is [Maker]'s statement that ["…"] a term of the contract between [Claimant] and [Defendant], or a mere representation?

Law

Contract Law 2 is concerned with identifying the content of the bargain. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect, so its breach is a breach of contract. A representation is a statement made in connection with the contract, which may be true or false, but which does not form part of the contract and has no promissory effect. The court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended about the contractual nature of the statement, considering: (1) the language used, as a strong statement ("I assure you", "I guarantee") is more likely a term; (2) the context in which it was made; (3) the time it was made, as a statement immediately before the contract is more likely a term; (4) the maker's relative knowledge and expertise; and (5) the importance of the statement and whether the other party obviously relied on it. In the lecture examples, "this machine can process 500 units per hour" is more likely a term, while "this machine should be perfect for your business" is a representation. A mere puff, such as "Red Bull gives you wings", is not intended to be taken seriously and is neither: unlike the advertisement in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, which was more than a puff because £1,000 was deposited to show sincerity.

ILAC F · Parol evidence rule & collateral contract

Issue

Does the parol evidence rule prevent [Claimant] from relying on [Maker]'s oral statement that […], and if so, is it enforceable as a collateral contract?

Law

Under the parol evidence rule, where a contract is reduced to writing and appears to be entire, it is presumed that the writing contains all its terms, and evidence of any previous or contemporaneous oral agreement will not be admitted to add to or vary it: Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor. If an oral promise is inconsistent with a written term, the written term generally prevails. There are two exceptions. First, where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, oral evidence may establish oral terms. Second, the statement may form a collateral contract, which requires: (1) an oral statement and a main written contract; (2) the statement was intended as a promise, made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) it is not inconsistent with the main written contract; and (4) entry into the main contract is the consideration for the promise: De Lassalle v Guildford, where a landlord's oral assurance that the drains were in good order, given before the tenant handed over the signed lease, was enforced as a collateral contract although the lease said nothing about drains. As it is the collateral contract that is breached, the innocent party cannot terminate the main contract but is entitled to damages.

= parol: oral. Collateral contract: a small side-contract "my promise, in return for you signing the main one".

Where a disclaimer is in a contract between two businesses, the court presumes equal bargaining power and interprets it neutrally. Filled-in example · 2024 Semester 2 Q2 (GlobalCorp v Technovate)

Question (25 marks). After six months of negotiations, Technovate entered into a written contract with GlobalCorp to create customised software. One week before signing, Alex, head of Technovate's technology department, assured GlobalCorp that the software would handle at least 10,000 concurrent users, based on similar projects for other clients; this gave GlobalCorp confidence in its scalability. At the beginning of negotiations, Damon, a junior sales representative, said the software would "likely enhance productivity" (with no research or statistics), was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise GlobalCorp's business operations". The software handles only 5,000 users, which is unsuitable; GlobalCorp lost $250,000, and productivity did not improve. The written contract lacks detail, is very poorly written, and contains a vague clause promising "industry-standard performance capabilities", but does not address Technovate's statements. Advise GlobalCorp under Contract Law 2. Use ILAC.

Issue 1: Alex's assurance

Issue. Is Alex's assurance that the software would handle at least 10,000 concurrent users an enforceable term of the contract between GlobalCorp and Technovate, despite the parol evidence rule?

Law. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect; a representation is a statement made in connection with the contract that does not form part of it. The court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended, considering the language used, the context, the timing, the maker's relative expertise and the importance of the statement. Under the parol evidence rule, where a contract is reduced to writing and appears to be entire, oral evidence will not be admitted to add to or vary it: Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor. The rule does not apply where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, or where a collateral contract arises. A collateral contract requires an oral statement and a main written contract, the statement being a promise made to induce entry into the main contract, consistency with the written contract, and entry into the main contract as consideration: De Lassalle v Guildford, where a landlord's oral assurance that the drains were sound was enforced although the lease said nothing about drains.

Application. Language: Alex "assured" GlobalCorp of "at least 10,000 concurrent users", a strong, specific and measurable statement, like the lecture example "this machine can process 500 units per hour". Timing: it was made one week before signing, close to formation. Maker: Alex is head of technology with expertise GlobalCorp lacked. Importance: the assurance "provided GlobalCorp with confidence", and 5,000 users was "not suitable" for its operations. On the other hand, Technovate may argue Alex gave only an estimate and the statement was left out of the contract; however, the strength of the words and GlobalCorp's obvious reliance outweigh this.

Technovate will argue the parol evidence rule excludes the assurance. However, the contract "lacks detail and is very poorly written" and does not address any of Technovate's statements, so it does not appear to be entire; the parties likely intended the agreement to be partly oral. Alternatively, a collateral contract arises: there is an oral statement and a main written contract; the assurance was a promise inducing GlobalCorp to sign; it is consistent with the "industry-standard performance" clause; and GlobalCorp gave consideration by entering the main contract. As was the case in De Lassalle v Guildford, where the drains assurance induced the tenant to sign a lease silent on drains, here Alex's assurance induced GlobalCorp to sign a contract silent on user capacity.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Alex's assurance is a term or, alternatively, a collateral contract, and the parol evidence rule does not exclude it. Therefore, GlobalCorp would be entitled to damages of $250,000 to put it in the position it would have been in had the software handled 10,000 users, subject to its duty to mitigate (Payzu Ltd v Saunders); if enforced as a collateral contract it cannot terminate the main contract.

Issue 2: Damon's statements

Issue. Are Damon's statements that the software would "likely enhance productivity", was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise" GlobalCorp's operations enforceable terms of the contract?

Law. Tentative opinions such as "this machine should be perfect for your business" are representations, not terms. A mere puff, such as "Red Bull gives you wings", is a statement not intended to be taken seriously and creates no contractual obligation, unlike the advertisement in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, where £1,000 was deposited to show sincerity.

Application. "The best on the market" and "revolutionise" are exaggerated, unquantifiable sales talk. Unlike Carlill, nothing showed sincerity, as Damon gave no research or statistics. "Likely enhance productivity" is hedged opinion. The other factors also point away from a term: it was said at the beginning of six months of negotiations, and Damon was a junior sales representative without special expertise.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Damon's statements are mere puffery or representations, not terms. Therefore, GlobalCorp would not be entitled to any contractual remedy for Damon's statements.

Issue 3: the written clause

Issue. Has Technovate breached the express term requiring "industry-standard performance capabilities", and is it a condition?

Law. A signed written term binds the parties: L'Estrange v Graucob. A condition is fundamental and its breach allows termination and damages (Poussard v Spiers, lead singer missing opening night); a warranty gives damages only (Bettini v Gye, missed rehearsals). The test of essentiality asks whether the innocent party would have contracted without the promise: Associated Newspapers v Bancks.

Application. The clause is an express term of the signed contract. There is insufficient information as to the industry standard; assuming comparable software handles 10,000 users, a 5,000-user limit is a breach. Like opening night in Poussard, capacity goes to the heart of the bargain, since GlobalCorp could not operate without it, so the term is likely a condition, unlike the rehearsals in Bettini.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find the clause is a condition that may have been breached. Therefore, GlobalCorp would be entitled to terminate the contract and claim damages of $250,000.

Unconscionable Conduct

Issue

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. At common law, mere harshness of a clause is not enough, but equity will intervene where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). As Kitto J stated in Blomley v Ryan, this arises "whenever one party to a transaction is at a special disadvantage in dealing with the other party because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands." Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, a contract will be set aside where: (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage compared with the stronger party, so there was no real equality between them; (2) the stronger party knew or should have known of that disadvantage; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it, so that it was unconscientious to procure the agreement in those circumstances. In Amadio, an elderly Italian migrant couple with limited written English signed a mortgage guarantee for their son's company, believing it was limited to $50,000 and six months when it was unlimited; the bank manager knew of their poor English, did not explain the document, and the guarantee was set aside.

Undue Influence

Issue

Can [client] have the [transfer / will / contract] in favour of [X] set aside on the basis of undue influence?

Law

Undue influence occurs where a party has a degree of controlling influence and power and dominates the will of another person: "every case where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". A contract is voidable at the election of the weaker party where: (1) the parties are in a pre-existing relationship; (2) one party has a controlling influence over the other; (3) the stronger party takes advantage of that influence for their own benefit; and (4) the weaker party is not exercising independent judgement, so there is no genuine free and voluntary consent. Certain recognised relationships automatically raise a presumption of undue influence, such as parent and child, doctor and patient, lawyer and client, and religious adviser and disciple (Allcard v Skinner, where a nun gave her property to her religious order on her spiritual adviser's influence). In other relationships, such as spouses, bank and client, employer and employee, and carer and patient, the weaker party must show the other exercised a dominance or controlling influence (Johnson v Buttress, where an elderly, illiterate widower, dependent on a friend after his wife's death, transferred his only home to her without independent advice and the transfer was set aside). Once influence is shown, the onus is on the stronger party to prove the transaction was the "pure voluntary and well-understood act" of the weaker party.

Misrepresentation

Issue

Can [client] rescind the contract with [X] on the basis of [X]'s misrepresentation that ["…"]?

Law

A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact made by one party to another which induces the other party to enter into the contract, making the contract voidable. The elements are: (1) a false statement of fact made by the representor to the representee; (2) about an existing fact or past event, not future intention; (3) made before or at the time the contract is concluded; and (4) intended to induce, and in fact inducing, the contract. Honest opinions, puffs, promises and statements of future intention are not misrepresentations, and mere silence is not, unless a failure to disclose turns a statement into a half-truth. There are three types. A fraudulent misrepresentation is made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly (Derry v Peek, where directors' false prospectus statement was not fraudulent because they honestly believed it). A negligent misrepresentation is an honest but careless statement where a special relationship creates a duty of care, on a serious or business matter, the maker knows it will be relied on, reliance is reasonable and damage results. An innocent misrepresentation is made unintentionally, without intention to deceive (Oscar Chess Ltd v Williams, where a seller honestly described a car as a 1948 model relying on a false log book). Rescission is available for all three; damages are available for fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation, but not innocent misrepresentation.

Mistake

Issue

Is the contract between [client] and [X] void for [common / mutual / unilateral] mistake [or non est factum]?

Law

As a general rule, a mistake by a party does not make a contract unenforceable (caveat emptor), and only in exceptional circumstances is a contract void for mistake. The mistake must exist prior to or at the time of contracting, must be fundamental, and must be a mistake rather than a misrepresentation. A common mistake occurs where both parties make the same mistake about a fundamental fact, and the agreement was conditional on that shared belief (Leaf v International Galleries, where both parties believed a painting was a Constable). A mutual mistake occurs where the parties are each thinking of different things, so there is no meeting of the minds (Raffles v Wichelhaus, where the parties meant two different ships both named "Peerless" and the contract was void). A unilateral mistake by one party generally will not void a contract, unless the mistake is serious and fundamental and the other party knew or ought to have known of it and sought to take advantage of it (Taylor v Johnson, where a buyer knew the seller thought the price was $15,000 per acre rather than in total, and the contract was set aside). Unilateral mistake also includes the limited doctrine of non est factum, where a party encouraged to sign without reading had a good reason for not reading (for example, blind, illiterate or unable to read English) and the mistake concerns the fundamental nature of the document.

Duress

Issue

Law

Duress is pressure exerted by one party to coerce or compel another to contract on particular terms, and makes the contract voidable, not void. Whether Juan can have his contract with Evergreen Solar (ES) set aside depends on whether his consent was affected by unconscionable conduct, misrepresentation or mistake. Factors that affect genuine, free and voluntary consent are called vitiating factors, and make a contract void or voidable at the option of the innocent party.

Issue 1: Unconscionable Conduct

Issue. Can Juan have the contract with ES set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?

Law. Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Equity intervenes where one party is at a special disadvantage because of "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties" and the other "unconscientiously takes advantage" (Blomley v Ryan). Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio: (1) the weaker party must be under a special disadvantage; (2) the stronger party knew or should have known of it; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it. In Amadio, elderly migrants with limited English signed an unlimited guarantee; the bank knew of their poor English, did not explain it, and the guarantee was set aside.

Application. Juan was under a special disadvantage: he is 85, has difficulty understanding English, poor eyesight and depression following his wife's recent death, and his stepson, who helps him with financial decisions, was away. As in Amadio, where elderly migrants with limited English signed a document they did not understand, Juan is in an even weaker position, as he could not read the contract at all. Jake knew of this because Juan told him directly about his eyesight, his English, his depression and his stepson. Jake took unfair advantage by insisting Juan sign immediately rather than wait for his stepson, and not explaining any terms, including the one-sided exit fee in cl 10(a)(ii). On the other hand, ES may argue Juan chose to sign; however, pressure after being told of these weaknesses is unconscientious.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that all three Amadio elements are satisfied. Therefore, Juan would be entitled to have the contract set aside, with his $2,500 deposit returned and no liability for the $4,000 exit fee.

Undue influence is unlikely, as Juan met Jake for the first time that afternoon, so there is no pre-existing relationship. Duress is also unlikely: Jake pressured Juan but made no threat of harm (cf Barton v Armstrong).

Issue 2: Misrepresentation

Issue. Can Juan rescind the contract on the basis of Jake's misrepresentation that it was "risk-free"?

Law. A misrepresentation is a false statement of existing fact, made before or at the time of contracting, which is intended to and does induce the other party to contract, making it voidable; puffs are not. It is fraudulent if made knowingly or recklessly (Derry v Peek, where directors who honestly believed their false prospectus were not fraudulent); negligent if honest but careless where there is a duty of care; and innocent if unintentional (Oscar Chess Ltd v Williams). Rescission is available for all three; damages only for fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation.

Application. "Instantly cut his power bills in half" concerns the future and is likely a puff. However, "risk-free" is a statement of existing fact about the contract Jake was handing over, and it was false, as cl 10(a)(ii) imposes a $4,000 exit fee on Juan. It was made before signing and induced Juan, who was reluctant, to sign "trusting Jake's assurances". Unlike the directors in Derry v Peek, who honestly believed their statement, Jake, presenting ES's own contract, either knew of the exit fee or was reckless, so it is likely fraudulent. Even if Jake honestly did not know, it would be at least negligent, as a professional seller who knew Juan relied on him.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that the "risk-free" statement was a fraudulent, or at least negligent, misrepresentation. Therefore, Juan would be entitled to rescind the contract, recover his $2,500 deposit and claim damages for any further loss, subject to mitigation (Payzu Ltd v Saunders).

Issue 3: Mistake (Non Est Factum)

Issue. Is Juan's contract with ES void for unilateral mistake or non est factum?

Law. Generally, a mistake by one party does not void a contract (caveat emptor), unless it is fundamental and the other party knew of it and took advantage (Taylor v Johnson). Non est factum applies where a party had a good reason for not reading, such as poor sight or inability to read English, and the mistake concerns the fundamental nature of the document.

Application. Juan had a good reason for not reading the contract (poor eyesight and English), which Jake knew. However, Juan knew he was signing a solar contract; his mistake was about one term, the exit fee, not the document's nature. As in Taylor v Johnson, Juan may argue Jake exploited his mistake; but ES would argue an exit fee is not fundamental.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find the contract is not void for mistake. Therefore, Juan should rely on unconscionable conduct and misrepresentation to obtain rescission.

Overall, the contract is likely to be set aside for unconscionable conduct and misrepresentation. As Juan acted within two weeks and has not affirmed the contract, no bar to rescission applies, and he is entitled to a refund of his $2,500 deposit without paying the $4,000 exit fee.

Issue 1: Authority of [A] to act for [P]

Issue

Did [A] have the authority to act on behalf of [his/her] principal [P] when [accepting 3P's offer / signing the contract for … / purchasing …], such that [P] is bound to [3P]?

Law

Agency is a legal term used to describe a person (the agent) who acts on behalf of another (the principal) in dealing with a third party. The relationship of principal and agent gives rise to a number of obligations and duties on the part of both the principal and the agent. These duties and obligations are both contractual and fiduciary in nature.

Ways in which an agent can be authorised to act on behalf of a principal include actual authority — express actual authority or implied actual authority — or apparent authority. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract or other legal relationship is between the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability.

Actual authority arises out of a "consensual agreement" between principal and agent "to which they alone are parties": Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express authority is where the principal expressly authorises the agent, in writing (including by deed, such as a power of attorney) or verbally, to act on the principal's behalf. Implied authority is the authority to do all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the principal's express instructions, or needed to give those instructions business efficacy. See: Peterson v Moloney (1951), where implied authority was not found.

For apparent authority, if the principal allows a third party to believe that the agent has authority to act on the principal's behalf, then even if the agent was not actually authorised, the principal will be bound to honour the agent's dealings with the third party. The 3 requirements to prove apparent authority are that: (1) the third party did not know that the agent did not have actual authority; (2) the principal 'held out' the agent as having authority to act on the principal's behalf, for example by appointing the agent to a particular position or allowing them to act in the past; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on the principal's representation when dealing with the agent. See: Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), where a company secretary who hired cars (partly for personal use) bound the company, because hiring cars was within a secretary's usual, and therefore apparent, authority. The representation must come from the principal, not the agent: Essington v Regency (2004).

Filled-in example — Week 12 Q&A: Cristina, Davo and Patrizia

Cristina owns a St Lucia house valued at about $1.2 million and is called overseas for 2 months. She appoints Davo, a real estate agent, in writing, to sell to the first buyer offering over $1 million, and instructs him to put up a sign: "All enquiries to David Barnes at 'Dave's Real Estate', St Lucia." Patrizia sees the sign, inspects and offers $850,000. Davo says nothing about the $1 million instruction, tells her "I am authorized by the owner to accept any offer that I determine to be reasonable", and accepts on Cristina's behalf. Cristina returns and wants to rescind or sue Patrizia for $150,000. Advise Cristina on who will be liable and what remedies may result.

Issue 1: Davo's authority to accept Patrizia's offer

Issue. Did Davo have the authority to act on behalf of his principal Cristina when accepting Patrizia's offer, such that Cristina is bound to Patrizia?

Law. Agency is a legal term used to describe a person (the agent) who acts on behalf of another (the principal) in dealing with a third party. An agent may be authorised by actual authority — express or implied — or by apparent authority. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability. Actual authority arises out of a consensual agreement between principal and agent: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express authority is given by the principal in writing or verbally. Implied authority is the authority to do all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the express instructions: Peterson v Moloney (1951). For apparent authority, if the principal allows a third party to believe the agent has authority, the principal will be bound even if the agent was not actually authorised. The 3 requirements are that: (1) the third party did not know the agent lacked actual authority; (2) the principal held the agent out as having authority; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on that representation. See: Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), where a company secretary's car hire bound the company as it was within his apparent authority. The representation must come from the principal, not the agent: Essington v Regency (2004).

Application. There is a relationship of agent and principal between Cristina and Davo — Cristina is the principal, Davo is the agent and Patrizia is the third party. Cristina gave Davo actual authority. Express authority was given because Cristina, in writing, instructed Davo to sell to the first buyer offering over $1 million. Implied authority was present because putting up the sign, showing Patrizia through the house and giving her information were necessarily incidental to selling it. However, in selling the house for $850,000, Davo acted outside his actual authority; implied authority cannot extend to a price that contradicts the express instruction.

As was the case in Panorama, where hiring cars fell within the secretary's apparent authority, Patrizia would argue that there was apparent authority because: Patrizia did not know that Cristina had limited Davo to offers over $1 million, as Davo said nothing about it; Cristina 'held out' that Davo had authority by appointing him as her selling agent and instructing a sign stating "All enquiries to David Barnes"; and Patrizia relied upon that sign when contacting Dave's Real Estate and making her offer. On the other hand, Cristina will rely on Essington v Regency to argue that Davo's claim to be "authorized … to accept any offer that I determine to be reasonable" came only from the agent. However, Patrizia relies on Cristina's own appointment and sign, not Davo's words alone. Therefore, Davo had the apparent authority to act on Cristina's behalf.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Davo had apparent authority, so Patrizia would be able to enforce the contract against Cristina, and the contract would not be rescinded. Therefore, Cristina would not be entitled to rescind the contract or recover $150,000 from Patrizia, an innocent third party; her remedy lies against Davo.

Issue 2: Davo's fiduciary duties

Issue. Did Davo breach any of his fiduciary obligations towards Cristina?

Law. Agents owe fiduciary duties to their principal as a result of the relationship of trust and confidence that exists between them. Examples are the duty to follow the principal's lawful instructions, failing which the agent is personally liable for the harm caused: Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830); the duty to communicate relevant information: John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002); the duty to act in the principal's interests: Hewson v Sydney (1968); and the duty to exercise due care, skill and diligence: Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991). These duties could also be imposed as contractual duties under the terms of the agency agreement.

Application. It is likely that Davo breached his fiduciary duties to Cristina because: first, Cristina instructed him in writing to sell only for over $1 million, yet he accepted $850,000; second, he did not tell Cristina about the below-threshold offer before accepting it, and nothing suggests she could not be contacted; and third, accepting $850,000 for a house valued at $1.2 million was against her interests and not what a reasonable agent would do. As was the case in Bertram Armstrong v Godfray, where an agent who failed to follow instructions was liable for the harm caused, Davo ignored a clear instruction. On the other hand, Davo may argue he thought the offer "more than reasonable"; however, the instruction was clear and in writing. Since the instructions were in writing, this could also amount to a breach of an express term of the agency agreement, which also amounts to a breach of contract by Davo.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely decide that Patrizia can enforce the contract under apparent authority. However, Davo has breached his fiduciary duties and contractual duties as an agent towards Cristina. Therefore, Cristina would be entitled to damages from Davo for the loss — at least the $150,000 between the sale price and her $1 million minimum.

Issue 1: Existence of a partnership

Issue

Were [A, B and C] carrying on [business name] in partnership under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld), such that each is the agent of the others?

Law

"A partnership is the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit": Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) ('PA') s 5(1). There are no registration requirements or legal formalities, and the partnership agreement may be written, oral or wholly implied from the conduct of the partners; a partnership exists as long as the 4 requirements are satisfied, regardless of the intention of the parties. Each partner is both the principal and the agent of the other partners, and each has unlimited personal liability for the partnership's debts: Kendall v Hamilton (1879).

First, persons: at least 2 persons are required, and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115.
Second, carrying on a business: a business includes a "trade, occupation or profession" (PA s 3), and 'carrying on' requires some continuity or repetition of trading activities. A single transaction or one-off project is usually not a partnership: Smith v Anderson (1880). However, if the parties begin working together to establish an identifiable business, the partnership commences from that date: Khan v Miah [2000].
Third, in common: each must act on behalf of the others as well as themselves. An employer and employee carry on a business together but are not partners, because the employer does not act on behalf of the employee: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Silent partners are still partners and are still liable.
Fourth, with a view of profit: the parties must be seeking to make a profit, and they remain a partnership even if they expect or make a loss: Minter v Minter (2000).

= Partnership: 2+ people running a business together for money. No paperwork needed. Every partner can bind the others and is personally liable for the firm's debts (their own house and savings).

Issue 2: Authority of [partner] to bind the firm

Issue

Did [A] have the authority to act on behalf of [the firm] and [his/her] partners [B and C] when [signing the $X contract with 3P for … / purchasing …], such that they are liable to [3P]?

Law

Each partner in a partnership is the agent of the firm and of the other partners, and has express, implied and apparent authority to act on their behalf when dealing with third parties such as financiers, suppliers and customers. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party. Ways in which an agent can be authorised include actual authority — express or implied — or apparent authority. Actual authority arises out of a "consensual agreement" between principal and agent: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964].

Express authority is authority expressly granted by the other partners. Implied authority is the authority to do all the usual things necessary to carry out the express authority and to carry on the business of the partnership, such as selling the firm's property, buying goods of the kind usually employed in the firm's business, hiring employees, borrowing money and incurring and paying debts. Implied authority can be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985).

For apparent authority, under s 8 PA every partner is an agent of the firm, and acts "for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm" bind the firm and the partners, unless the partner had no authority and the third party knew this, or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Therefore, where a partner acts without actual authority, the other partners are still liable if 4 requirements are satisfied: (1) the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm, judged by what the firm's usual business is and what transactions such a firm would usually engage in: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) that business was carried on in the usual way, as otherwise the third party should suspect a lack of authority: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person was a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of actual authority, relying in good faith and without notice: Crouch v IPG (2013). The partnership agreement binds only the partners, not third parties.

Partners are jointly and personally liable for the firm's debts, and may be jointly and severally liable for torts and crimes committed by a fellow partner: Kendall v Hamilton (1879). A partnership is not a separate legal entity, although it may sue and be sued.

= Jointly liable: the partners together owe the whole debt. Severally: each partner can be sued alone for the whole amount. Req 1 = WHAT was done (the right kind of deal for this firm?). Req 2 = HOW it was done (the normal way?).

Callum and Tessa refuse to pay. Advise StageCorp how to recover the remaining $162,000.

As CT Events operates in Sydney, NSW legislation technically applies; this answer assumes the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) ('PA') is equivalent.

Issue 1: Existence of a partnership

Issue. Were Callum, Tessa and Robert carrying on CT Events in partnership under the PA, such that each is the agent of the others?

Law. "A partnership is the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit": PA s 5(1). No formalities are required. Each partner is principal and agent of the others, with unlimited personal liability: Kendall v Hamilton (1879). First, there must be at least 2 and generally no more than 20 persons: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115. Second, a business with continuity; a one-off project is not enough: Smith v Anderson (1880); the partnership starts when they begin working together: Khan v Miah [2000]. Third, in common; an employer and employee are not partners: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Fourth, a view of profit: Minter v Minter (2000).

Application. There are three persons, within the limit of 20. CT Events has operated since mid-2022 with a website and growing client base; unlike Smith v Anderson, this is a continuing trade. Consistent with Khan v Miah, Robert became a partner when he began working in the business in early 2023, despite no formal agreement. They share an office and joint account, split profits equally, decide by majority vote, and Robert was introduced as part of the team, so each acts on behalf of the others. On the other hand, Callum and Tessa may argue the freelance work shows otherwise; however, unlike the employee in Degiorgio v Dunn, Robert shares profits and has an equal vote. Profits are split, so there is a view of profit.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Callum, Tessa and Robert are partners under s 5(1) PA. Therefore, Robert is an agent of the firm and of Callum and Tessa.

Issue 2: Authority of Robert to bind the firm

Issue. Did Robert have the authority to act on behalf of CT Events and his partners Callum and Tessa when signing the $180,000 contract with StageCorp, such that they are liable to StageCorp?

Law. Each partner is the agent of the firm and the other partners, with express, implied and apparent authority. Implied authority covers the usual things needed to carry on the firm's business, unless overridden by contrary agreement or instructions: Construction v Hexyl (1985). Under s 8 PA, the firm is still bound if: (1) the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) in the usual way: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person was a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of authority: Crouch v IPG (2013). Partners are jointly and personally liable for the firm's debts: Kendall v Hamilton.

Application. There is no evidence Callum or Tessa expressly authorised the purchase; they were "outraged". StageCorp will argue buying equipment is usual for an events business; however, as in Construction v Hexyl, any implied authority is overridden by the firm's practice of hiring equipment and deciding by majority vote. Therefore, Robert acted outside his actual authority. As to s 8, first, applying Mercantile v Garrod, CT Events runs small functions under 100 guests; a stage for "large-scale concerts and festivals" is a different kind of business. Second, the firm usually hired, so a $180,000 purchase by one partner was not the usual way; as in Goldberg v Jenkins, StageCorp should have suspected a lack of authority. Third, StageCorp knew Robert was a partner: he signed on behalf of CT Events and paid from the joint account. Fourth, Robert said the purchase depended on whether "he could convince Callum and Tessa", so StageCorp was on notice and did not rely in good faith without notice (Crouch v IPG). Therefore, Robert did not have apparent authority.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Robert had no actual or apparent authority, so CT Events, Callum and Tessa are not bound by the stage contract. Therefore, StageCorp cannot enforce the contract against Callum or Tessa; its remedy lies against Robert.

Issue 3: Personal liability of Robert to StageCorp

Issue. Can StageCorp hold Robert personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

Law. An agent is personally liable if: (1) they claimed to contract on behalf of the principal; (2) they did not in fact have authority; and (3) the third party relied on that representation and would not otherwise have contracted: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020].

Application. First, Robert signed "on behalf of CT Events" and paid the deposit from the joint account. Second, he had no authority. Third, StageCorp would not have built the stage for Robert personally. On the other hand, Robert may argue there was no reliance because he said he still needed his partners' support; however, by signing in the firm's name and paying a deposit from its account, he represented that the firm was committed.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Robert is personally liable to StageCorp for breach of warranty of authority. Therefore, StageCorp would be entitled to damages from Robert of up to the $162,000 balance, less what it recovers by taking back and reselling the stage. In the alternative, if s 8 applied, all three partners would be jointly liable for the $162,000 (Kendall v Hamilton).

Opening Law paragraph (start of Issue 1's Law; later issues: "As stated above, …")

Australia's competition laws are primarily contained in Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) ('CCA'), which is enforced by the ACCC. The objective of Part IV is to prohibit trade practices that have the effect of substantially lessening competition in the marketplace. Some conduct is so obviously anti-competitive that it is prohibited outright (per se), namely cartel conduct, misuse of market power and resale price maintenance; other conduct, such as secondary boycotts, exclusive dealing, certain mergers and agreements that lessen competition, is prohibited only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market. The market is "the area of close competition between firms … the field of rivalry between them": Re Queensland Co-operative Milling Association Ltd (1976). 'Substantial' means large, weighty, real or of substance. To decide whether conduct substantially lessens competition, the court defines the relevant market, asks whether the conduct lessened, or is likely to lessen, competition in that market, asks whether that lessening is substantial and, if not, asks whether the purpose of the conduct was to substantially lessen competition. On application, the ACCC may authorise any of this conduct except misuse of market power, if the public benefit outweighs the public detriment.

= plain English: per se = illegal no matter what effect it had. SLC = substantially lessening competition. The accuser wants the market defined narrowly (easier to show SLC); the accused wants it wide — use this for your "on the other hand" sentence.

Cartel conduct (price fixing, bid rigging, market sharing, output restriction)

Issue

Have [A] and [B] engaged in cartel conduct by [fixing prices / rigging bids / allocating customers or territories / restricting output], in contravention of the CCA?

Law

A cartel provision is defined in s 45AD as a provision of a contract, arrangement or understanding between two or more businesses that are, or should be, in competition with each other, which fixes the prices charged by them, controls or restricts the quantity of goods to inflate prices, rigs bids or tenders, or allocates customers, suppliers or territories between them: TPC v TNT (1995); ACCC v Visy (2007). Cartel conduct is a per se contravention, illegal regardless of its actual impact on competition (ss 45AJ, 45AK), and is also a criminal offence (ss 45AF, 45AG). In CDPP v Nippon Kabushiki Kaisha [2017], the first successful criminal cartel prosecution, NYK, which shipped motor vehicles to Australia, was party to provisions on freight rates, bid rigging and customer allocation and was fined $25 million.

Price fixing, the most common form, requires that (1) the business has a contract, arrangement or understanding with one or more businesses with which it is in competition, and (2) it contains a provision with the purpose or likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices: s 45AD; ACCC v Alice (1997). A formal agreement is not required; an arrangement or understanding is sufficient, and the competitors need not agree on a particular price — an agreed formula, exchanging price data, restricting production to raise prices or setting the same discounts will suffice. However, parallel conduct such as price leadership, where one business sets a price and competitors subsequently set the same price, is not necessarily collusion: TPC v Email (1980).

= plain English: an "arrangement or understanding" = any informal meeting of minds (a nod, a handshake, "let's both…"). Visy facts if you want one more comparison: Visy and Amcor, the two big cardboard-box makers, secretly agreed on prices and customers; Visy was penalised $36 million.

Misuse of market power

Issue

Has [A] misused its market power by [pricing below cost / refusing to supply X], in contravention of s 46 of the CCA?

Law

Section 46 applies only to a business with a substantial degree of power in a market, for example through financial strength or as a major supplier. Such a business must not take advantage of that power to eliminate or substantially damage a competitor, or to prevent a competitor's entry into that or any other market: s 46; ACCC v Ticketek Pty Ltd [2011]. The 2017 amendment explains that the object of s 46 is to "target anti-competitive behaviour by firms with substantial market power, while allowing legitimate pro-competitive behaviour even if this results in harm to inefficient competitors". Misuse of market power is prohibited per se and cannot be authorised by the ACCC. A common example is predatory pricing, where a business charges an unrealistically low price, taking a loss to force a competitor out, and later raises its prices again. In Boral Besser Masonry (the C&M Bricks case), BBM cut its prices below manufacturing cost to drive C&M out and deter new entrants, but the High Court held there was no misuse, because BBM did not hold substantial market power and a company cannot be said to "take advantage" of power if it has no intention of recouping its loss.

Optional extra sentence (current law since 2017): "Section 46 now also asks whether the conduct has the purpose, effect or likely effect of substantially lessening competition in the market."

Primary or secondary boycott

Issue

Law

A boycott is an action by an individual or group that prevents, or is intended to prevent, another individual or group from buying or selling products in a market: ss 45D, 45DA, 45DB, 45DD. Exclusive dealing (full-line and third-line forcing)

Issue

Has [A] engaged in exclusive dealing by [refusing to supply C unless …], in contravention of s 47 of the CCA?

Law

Exclusive dealing occurs when a business imposes restrictions on another person's freedom to choose with whom, in what amount, or where they buy products, and under s 47 it is prohibited only if it has the effect of substantially lessening competition in the relevant market. Section 47 prohibits two types. Full-line forcing occurs where a business refuses to supply its product unless the buyer agrees not to buy products from a competitor, not to resupply products acquired from a competitor, or not to resupply its product to a particular place: TPC v Massey (1983). Third-line forcing occurs where a business makes the supply of its product conditional on the customer also purchasing the product of another business: s 47(6); ACCC v Black & White Cabs [2010]; see the travel insurance example (Cannon Investments). It requires that (1) there is one product the purchaser wants, but another is forced on them; (2) the forced product is that of a third party; (3) the purchaser cannot obtain the product it wanted without also obtaining the third party's product; and (4) the conduct has the effect of substantially lessening competition in the relevant market.

Issue

Law

As a general provision, a business cannot make a deal with one or more of its competitors that is intended or likely to reduce competition in the market: s 45(1)(a); ACCC v Visy (2007). Filled-in example (practice question, 25 marks)

Coastline Concrete Pty Ltd ('Coastline') and Bayside Mix Pty Ltd ('Bayside') are the two largest suppliers of ready-mix concrete to builders on the Sunshine Coast, together supplying about 85% of the region's concrete. In January 2026 a small new supplier, QuickPour, began selling concrete at about $230 per cubic metre.

At a builders' golf day in February, Coastline's managing director, Grant, told Bayside's sales manager, Mia: "This price war is killing us. If we both go to $265 a cubic metre from 1 March, the builders have nowhere else to go." Mia replied, "Makes sense — I'll run it past my boss." Grant added: "And Hinterland Homes keeps buying from QuickPour. If they want to do that, neither of us should supply them at all." Mia nodded and they shook hands.

On 1 March both Coastline and Bayside raised their price to exactly $265. The following week both told Hinterland Homes, a large local builder, that they would no longer supply it while it used QuickPour. Hinterland had to delay three projects and paid $40,000 extra to truck concrete in from Brisbane.

Bayside says it "just matched Coastline's price, as everyone in the industry does", and that Mia never agreed to anything. Advise the ACCC and Hinterland Homes whether Coastline and Bayside have contravened Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), and what remedies and penalties are available.

Whether Coastline and Bayside have contravened Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) depends upon two issues: first, whether Coastline and Bayside engaged in cartel conduct by fixing the price of concrete; and second, whether they engaged in a primary boycott of Hinterland Homes ('Hinterland').

Issue 1: Cartel conduct (price fixing)

Issue. Have Coastline and Bayside engaged in cartel conduct by fixing the price of ready-mix concrete, in contravention of the CCA?

Law. Australia's competition laws are primarily contained in Part IV of the CCA, which is enforced by the ACCC. Some conduct is prohibited outright (per se), namely cartel conduct, misuse of market power and resale price maintenance; other conduct is prohibited only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market, being "the area of close competition between firms … the field of rivalry between them": Re Queensland Co-operative Milling Association Ltd (1976).

A cartel provision is defined in s 45AD as a provision of a contract, arrangement or understanding between businesses in competition with each other which fixes prices, restricts output, rigs bids or allocates customers or territories: TPC v TNT (1995); ACCC v Visy (2007). Cartel conduct is per se (ss 45AJ, 45AK) and a criminal offence (ss 45AF, 45AG). In CDPP v Nippon Kabushiki Kaisha [2017], NYK was fined $25 million for provisions on freight rates, bid rigging and customer allocation. Price fixing requires (1) a contract, arrangement or understanding with a competitor, (2) containing a provision with the purpose or likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices: ACCC v Alice (1997). An informal understanding suffices; however, parallel conduct such as price leadership is not necessarily collusion: TPC v Email (1980).

Application. First, Coastline and Bayside both supply ready-mix concrete to builders on the Sunshine Coast, so they are competitors in the same field of rivalry (Re QCMA). Second, although nothing was written down, Mia's reply "Makes sense", her nod and the handshake show a meeting of minds, which is at least an arrangement or understanding. Third, the understanding that both would charge $265 per cubic metre from 1 March has the purpose, and in fact the effect, of fixing prices; Grant's remark that "the builders have nowhere else to go" confirms that purpose.

On the other hand, Bayside may argue that, as in TPC v Email, it merely followed Coastline's price, and that Mia only said she would "run it past" her boss. However, unlike mere price leadership, the identical rise to exactly $265 on the very date discussed came straight after a direct conversation, so it is evidence of the understanding rather than an innocent explanation. As in CDPP v Nippon Kabushiki Kaisha, where competitors fixed freight rates, here Coastline and Bayside fixed the price of concrete. Because cartel conduct is per se, it is irrelevant whether competition was actually lessened, and no ACCC authorisation was sought.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Coastline and Bayside engaged in cartel conduct by fixing prices in contravention of ss 45AJ and 45AK. Therefore, the ACCC would be entitled to seek a pecuniary penalty against each company of up to the greatest of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained or 30% of annual turnover (s 76), and an injunction (s 80); both companies may also be prosecuted under ss 45AF and 45AG, as NYK was.

Issue 2: Primary boycott

Issue. Have Coastline and Bayside engaged in a primary boycott of Hinterland, in contravention of the CCA?

Law. A boycott is an action by a group that prevents, or is intended to prevent, another from buying or selling products in a market (ss 45D, 45DA, 45DB, 45DD). A primary boycott occurs where two or more businesses agree that they will collectively refuse to deal with a particular target competitor, supplier or customer: s 45; TPC v JW Bryant (1978). In ACCC v Australian Medical Association (WA) Inc (2001), the AMA's threat that independent doctors would collectively withdraw their services was held to be a primary boycott (and price fixing), with $285,000 in penalties and costs.

Application. First, Grant proposed that "neither of us should supply" Hinterland, and Mia nodded and shook hands, which is an understanding between two competitors. Second, Coastline and Bayside themselves then refused to supply Hinterland, their customer, so the boycotters directly refused to deal with the target; this is a primary, not a secondary, boycott. Bayside may argue it decided independently; however, both companies notified Hinterland in the same week, using the same condition about QuickPour, which strongly suggests a collective decision. Similar to AMA (WA), where doctors collectively threatened to withdraw services, here the two suppliers of 85% of the region's concrete collectively withdrew supply. Its purpose was to keep QuickPour, a new competitor, out of the market. Even if a substantial lessening of competition had to be shown, it would be, given their combined 85% share.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Coastline and Bayside engaged in a primary boycott of Hinterland in contravention of s 45. Therefore, the ACCC would be entitled to seek pecuniary penalties (s 76), and Hinterland would be entitled to damages of $40,000 for its extra costs (s 82) or a compensation order (s 79B), together with an injunction requiring Coastline and Bayside to resume supply (s 80).

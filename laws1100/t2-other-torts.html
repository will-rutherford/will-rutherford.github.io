1 · Learn it in 20 minutes

1.1 The big picture

  • A tort is a civil wrong that lets the aggrieved person sue the wrongdoer for damages, which compensate for personal injury, property damage or economic loss that actually happened (or that the law presumes happened). The main aim of tort law is compensation, not punishment (that’s criminal law). Unlike contract, tort needs no promise: the law imposes these duties on everyone.
  • Two types of torts (W4 slide 6):
    • Intentional torts (e.g. defamation, trespass): the plaintiff must prove the defendant intended the act and that the damage was the direct result of it.
    • Unintentional torts, i.e. negligence (T1): doing something a reasonable person wouldn’t do, or failing to do something they would.
  • The torts on this page: Trespass (to land, to goods, to the person: battery, assault, false imprisonment), Nuisance (private; public is not assessed this semester), Defamation, Deceit, Passing off. Then Defences and Remedies.

1.2 Trespass, the common skeleton

Trespass happens when the defendant interferes with the person or property of the plaintiff. Every form of trespass is “actionable per se”: the plaintiff does not have to prove actual damage. The slides note that all three forms use the same four elements:

  1. Interference (with land / goods / the body, depending on the form);
  2. The interference is direct (a direct result of the defendant’s act);
  3. It is intentional or negligent (unintended, but still a voluntary act);
  4. There is no consent or lawful justification.

Learn the four elements once and you have the Law structure for land, goods, battery, assault and false imprisonment. Only element 1 changes.

1.3 Trespass to land

The defendant unlawfully enters, remains on, or puts something on the plaintiff’s land. Elements: (1) interference with another person’s exclusive possession of land; (2) direct; (3) intentional or negligent; (4) no consent or lawful justification.

Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco [1957] 2 QB 334: Imperial owned a building and leased part of it to Kelsen (a tobacconist shop with a flat). Imperial put up a sign that stuck four inches into Kelsen’s airspace. Held: trespass to airspace, and an injunction was granted. Use it when: anything overhangs or encroaches (signs, awnings, ducts, cranes, stacked goods), even a tiny amount, and it’s the case to cite when the remedy is an injunction.

1.4 Trespass to goods

The defendant interferes with goods in the plaintiff’s possession, for example by taking or using them without permission. Elements: (1) interference with another’s possession of goods; (2) direct; (3) intentional or negligent; (4) no consent or lawful justification.

  • The plaintiff doesn’t have to be the owner. Rightful possession at the time is enough (e.g. a hirer or borrower).
  • No damage has to be proved.
  • No case is given on the slide. Argue from the elements. (Typical facts: a guard takes and searches a customer’s bag; a neighbour moves or uses someone’s equipment.)

1.5 Trespass to the person: Battery (actual physical contact)

Elements: (1) physical interference with a person’s body; (2) direct contact; (3) intentional or negligent; (4) no consent or lawful justification.

  • Battiato v Lagana [1992], Moynihan J at 235: “The direct intentional imposition of any unwanted physical contact on another person constitutes the tort of Battery. There is NO requirement to prove that the contact caused… any physical harm.”
  • Even the smallest touch can be a battery. BUT In re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation) [1990] 2 AC 1, Lord Goff at 72–73: some contact is unavoidable in daily life. This was once explained as implied consent; it’s now a general exception for contact “generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of everyday life.”
  • Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd [2001]: Rixon, a banned patron, played roulette. An employee confronted him and held him until police arrived, putting a hand on his shoulder to get his attention. Held: not battery. The touch was to get his attention and was “generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life”, so Star City was not liable. Use it to distinguish a light tap from a grab, push or drag.

1.6 Trespass to the person: Assault (the threat of contact)

Elements: (1) the defendant causes another to have a reasonable apprehension of direct, imminent and harmful or offensive physical contact; (2) direct; (3) intentional or negligent; (4) no consent or lawful justification.

  • No contact is needed. Assault and battery often happen together.
  • Brady v Schatzel [1911] at 208: it doesn’t matter whether the person was actually put in fear. Otherwise liability would depend on whether the victim was “a courageous or timid person”. So “I wasn’t scared” is no answer.
  • Rozsa v Samuels [1969]: a conditional threat is still an assault (slide example: Johnny threatens to punch Simon unless he leaves). “Come with me or I’ll put you on the ground” counts.
  • Stephens v Myers (1830): the slide gives only the name. (The classic facts: at a meeting the defendant advanced on the plaintiff with a clenched fist, intending to strike, and was stopped just before reaching him. Held: assault, because the threat was imminent.) Use it when: someone lunges, raises a fist or advances, even if they never make contact.

1.7 Trespass to the person: False imprisonment (no reasonable means of escape)

Elements: (1) the defendant causes another person to be totally restrained, meaning there is no reasonable avenue of escape; (2) direct; (3) intentional or negligent; (4) no consent or lawful justification.

  • Symes v Mahon [1922] SASR 447: a police officer told the plaintiff he had a warrant for his arrest and that he must come to Adelaide. He went. He was the wrong person. Held: false imprisonment. Use it to show that no force, lock or touch is needed: submitting to an assertion of authority is enough.
  • Bird v Jones (1845): the plaintiff was stopped from using one side of a bridge but could go another way. Held: merely “obstructed”, not imprisoned. A means of escape only defeats the claim if it is reasonable, without risk of injury or serious inconvenience. Use it when the plaintiff could just go another way.
  • Burton v Davies (1953): a truck driver picked up a hitch-hiker and groped her, then refused to let her out. Her only way out was to jump from the moving truck. Held: false imprisonment, because an escape that risks injury is not a reasonable means of escape. Use it when the only way out is dangerous (a window, a moving vehicle).

1.8 Defences to trespass (W4 slide 19)

The slides do not cover a shopkeeper’s or citizen’s statutory power of arrest. If a shop says it was “lawfully justified” in detaining a shoplifter, argue it through the slide defences (defence of property: reasonably necessary and proportionate) and say you are assuming no further statutory power applies. Don’t invent sections.

1.9 Nuisance

Nuisance is an act by the defendant that indirectly interferes with the plaintiff’s use and enjoyment of land. There are two forms: private and public. Public nuisance “will not be assessed this Semester” (slide 22), so only mention that it exists.

Trespass vs nuisance: trespass to land = direct interference with land in the plaintiff’s possession (something physically enters or sits on it). Nuisance = indirect interference with use and enjoyment (noise, smell, vibration, light, smoke drifting over).

Private nuisance: five elements.

  1. Interference with another’s use and enjoyment of private land, as an indirect result of the defendant’s action;
  2. The plaintiff has an interest in that land (e.g. owner or tenant);
  3. The plaintiff suffers actual harm or damage (note: unlike trespass, nuisance is not actionable per se);
  4. The interference is intentional or reckless;
  5. The interference is sustained and unreasonable.

Unreasonableness balancing test. The court balances the right to undisturbed enjoyment of property against the right to carry on activities, looking at: severity, duration, location, sensitivity (of the plaintiff), deliberateness or maliciousness, and whether any precautions were taken to minimise the interference. Think noise, vibrations, sounds, lighting, smells.

No nuisance case is named in the course slides. Apply the six factors carefully instead, since that’s where the marks are.

1.10 Defamation

A person commits defamation if they publish to a third party, in spoken or written form, a statement about another person that would damage their reputation. The plaintiff must establish three things:

  1. The statement was defamatory. It makes ordinary people think less of the plaintiff, causes people to shun or ridicule them, or causes them to be excluded from society.
    • Radio 2UE Sydney Pty Ltd v Chesterton [2009] (the general test): reputation is injured “when the esteem in which that person is held by the community is diminished in some respect”, i.e. whether the imputation is likely to cause people to think less of the plaintiff.
    • Mirror Newspapers Ltd v World Hosts Pty Ltd (1979): a newspaper implied that a restaurant owner had gone bankrupt (it was actually the manager). The owner sued because the article suggested he was in financial trouble, and the High Court upheld the claim. Use it for statements damaging a business’s financial or trading reputation.
    • Other slide examples: Mirror Newspapers v Jools (impression that a surgeon’s mistake caused a patient’s death); Rogers v Nationwide News (patient blinded by surgeon’s negligence); Andrews v John Fairfax (building leaked, implying the architect was incompetent). These show that implying professional incompetence is defamatory.
  2. The statement identified the plaintiff. They don’t have to be named, as long as the statement can reasonably be identified as referring to them. An entire class can’t sue, but a class with limited membership may be able to.
  3. The statement was published, i.e. communicated to someone other than the plaintiff. Text, video, images, spoken words, or any combination will do. Failing to stop or remove defamatory material can by itself give rise to liability.

Defences (slide 28): Justification (substantial truth); Absolute privilege (statements in parliamentary or judicial proceedings, Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 26); Fair reporting (fair report of proceedings of public concern, e.g. parliamentary committees, commissions of inquiry, law reform bodies); Honest opinion / fair comment (an honestly held opinion on a matter of public interest that is substantially true, Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 31(1)–(6)).

Remedies: damages, including aggravated damages (the slide’s example is defamation) for humiliation and distress, and an injunction (defamation is listed as an ongoing tort suited to one).

1.11 Deceit (fraudulent misrepresentation)

A false statement made during contractual negotiations to induce the other party is a misrepresentation. If it is fraudulent, it is the tort of deceit. Elements:

  1. A statement of fact made to another person knowing it is false;
  2. Made with the intention that it be relied upon;
  3. The other person relies on it;
  4. The other person suffers harm as a result.

Bisset v Wilkinson (1927): the seller made a statement about how many sheep a farm could hold. Held: not liable. It was an opinion honestly and reasonably held, not a statement of fact known to be false. Use it to distinguish a known lie about a verifiable fact (deceit) from an honest estimate or opinion (not deceit).

Links: the W11 and W12 agency slides note that an agent may also be liable in deceit or negligence, as well as under s 18 ACL. Misrepresentation as a vitiating factor in contract is in T4.

1.12 Passing off (see T5 IP)

The defendant misrepresents themselves or their product as having some kind of connection with the plaintiff (e.g. copying another’s trade mark). Elements (W4 slide 30 = W8 slide 48):

  1. A misrepresentation (express or implied) that their goods or services are connected with another person or have that person’s endorsement or approval;
  2. Made in the course of a trade;
  3. Intended to deceive potential purchasers.

Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989): the slide gives only the name. (The facts: a shoe advertisement parodied the famous knife scene from Paul Hogan’s Crocodile Dundee, suggesting Hogan was associated with or endorsed the shoes. Held: passing off.) Use it for any implied endorsement or association with a known person or brand.

Overlap: passing off overlaps with s 18 ACL (misleading or deceptive conduct), and competitors often sue each other under s 18 (Gillette v Energiser (2002), W9). For an unregistered trade mark, the owner can sue in passing off or under s 18 (W8 slide 49). If the question says “under Tort Law”, run passing off and just flag s 18 in one sentence.

1.13 Remedies (W4 slides 31–33)

  • Damages are the typical remedy. The aim is to put the injured person, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in had the tort not been committed. Punishment generally has no place.
  • Ordinary damages: assessed “once and for all” (the plaintiff can’t come back for more), so future losses must be estimated.
  • Nominal damages: a legal right was breached but there is no compensable damage (slide example: trespass to land).
  • Aggravated damages: compensate for humiliation or emotional distress (slide example: defamation; also argue it for a humiliating public detention).
  • Exemplary damages: where the defendant deliberately and maliciously disregarded the plaintiff’s rights, more than compensation can be awarded.
  • Injunction: a court order to do or stop doing something. Appropriate where a tort is ongoing, e.g. nuisance, trespass, defamation. Non-compliance brings civil or criminal penalties. An interim injunction restrains activity temporarily until the final decision.
  • Vicarious liability (W3 slide 57): an employer may be liable for its employees’ acts. Ask: did the act occur as part of their authorised work? (Century v Northern Ireland (1942)) and did it occur within the course of employment? (Deatons v Flew (1949)). Use this to make the business (the defendant with money) liable for its guard, manager or staff.

LAW · general trespass paragraph (write once, before the first trespass tort)
A tort is a civil wrong that allows the aggrieved person to sue the wrongdoer for the recovery of damages. Trespass is an intentional tort committed when the defendant interferes with the person or property of the plaintiff, and takes three common forms: trespass to land, trespass to goods and trespass to the person. Trespass is “actionable per se”, meaning the plaintiff is not required to prove that actual damage occurred. Each form of trespass requires (1) an interference, (2) that is direct, (3) that is intentional or negligent, and (4) for which there is no consent or lawful justification.

3.1 Trespass to land

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of trespass to land?
LAW
A person commits the tort of trespass to land if they unlawfully enter, remain on, or put something on the plaintiff’s land. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant interfered with their exclusive possession of land; (2) the interference was a direct result of the defendant’s actions; (3) the interference was intentional or, if not intended, negligent (a voluntary act); and (4) there was no consent or lawful justification. Land includes the airspace above it. In Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco [1957] 2 QB 334, Imperial Tobacco erected a sign that protruded only four inches into the airspace of premises leased to Kelsen, and an injunction was granted to prevent the trespass. Trespass is actionable per se, so no damage need be proved.
APPLICATION · mirror the 4 elements
(1) Exclusive possession: [P] has exclusive possession of [the land] as [owner / tenant under lease]. [D] interfered with it by [entering / parking / stacking goods / installing a sign that overhangs by X cm].
Case comparison: Similar to Kelsen, where a sign protruding just four inches into the plaintiff’s airspace was a trespass, here [D]’s [sign/duct] projects [X cm] over [P]’s [courtyard]. The small size of the intrusion is irrelevant.
(2) Direct: The interference is a direct result of [D]’s own act of […], rather than something that drifted onto the land (which would be nuisance).
(3) Intentional/negligent: [D] deliberately [installed / parked / entered]. Even if [D] believed it was their own land, the act was voluntary and at least negligent.
(4) No consent/justification: [P] never consented and [objected on DATE]. Nothing suggests necessity, so no defence applies.
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] has committed trespass to [P]’s land and [P] is entitled to an injunction requiring removal, as in Kelsen, together with damages (nominal damages if no loss is proven).

3.2 Trespass to goods

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of trespass to goods?
LAW
Trespass to goods is an interference by the defendant with goods in the possession of the plaintiff, such as taking or using goods without permission. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant interfered with their possession of goods; (2) the interference was direct; (3) it was intentional, or negligent and unintentional; and (4) there was no consent or lawful justification. The plaintiff does not have to be the owner of the goods, as long as they had rightful possession at the time, and does not have to prove any damage.
APPLICATION
[P] was in rightful possession of [the bag / equipment] [(even though it was hired/borrowed, ownership is not required)]. [D] directly interfered by [taking it / emptying it / using it]. This was a deliberate act. [P] [did not consent / refused]. Against this, [D] may argue [P] impliedly consented by [handing it over], but consent must be voluntary and [P] handed it over only because [D] [demanded it while detaining her].
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] committed trespass to [P]’s goods, although because no damage occurred, damages are likely to be nominal.

3.3 Battery

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of battery?
LAW
Battery is a form of trespass to the person involving the actual application of physical force. A person commits battery if: (1) they cause some physical interference with another person’s body; (2) the act is direct; (3) the act is intentional or negligent; and (4) there is no consent or lawful justification. In Battiato v Lagana [1992], Moynihan J held that “the direct intentional imposition of any unwanted physical contact on another person constitutes the tort of Battery”, with no requirement that the contact caused physical harm. However, contact that is “generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of everyday life” is excepted (In re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation) [1990] 2 AC 1, Lord Goff). In Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd [2001], a casino employee who placed his hand on a banned patron’s shoulder to get his attention was held not to have committed battery, because the contact was generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life.
APPLICATION
(1) Physical interference: [D] [grabbed P’s wrist / pushed / dragged / searched P’s pockets]. Following Battiato v Lagana, it does not matter whether this caused injury, although here [P] also suffered [bruising].
For D (Rixon): [D] will argue that, similar to Rixon, the contact was merely to get [P]’s attention and was generally acceptable in everyday life.
Against D (distinguish): However, Rixon is distinguishable. There the employee merely placed a hand on the patron’s shoulder, whereas here [D] [gripped / pulled / dragged] [P] [X metres], which goes well beyond the contact accepted in the ordinary conduct of daily life.
(2)–(3) Direct and intentional: The contact came directly from [D]’s own hands and was deliberate.
(4) No consent/justification: [P] did not consent, and in fact [pulled away / objected]. [D] may claim defence of property (protecting stock from theft), but that defence requires the force to be reasonably necessary and proportionate to an imminent threat. [Explain why it was not: P was not resisting / the alarm was a false trigger / nothing was found].
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D]’s [grab] constitutes battery against [P], as it goes beyond the acceptable contact in Rixon.

3.4 Assault

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of assault?
LAW
Assault is the threat of the actual application of physical force. A person commits assault if: (1) they cause another person to develop a reasonable apprehension of direct, imminent and harmful or offensive physical contact; (2) the act is direct; (3) the act is intentional or negligent; and (4) there is no consent or lawful justification. No physical contact needs to occur. In Brady v Schatzel [1911] it was held that it is not material whether the person was actually put in fear, as otherwise liability would depend on whether the victim was a courageous or timid person. A threat made conditional on the plaintiff complying with a demand is still an assault (Rozsa v Samuels [1969]). In Stephens v Myers (1830), a defendant who advanced on the plaintiff with a clenched fist, but was stopped before reaching him, committed assault.
APPLICATION
(1) Reasonable apprehension of imminent contact: [D] [raised a fist / stepped towards P / said “…”]. A reasonable person in [P]’s position would apprehend imminent contact, because [D was within arm’s reach / larger / already holding P].
Case comparison: Similar to Stephens v Myers, where advancing with a clenched fist was enough, [D]’s [gesture] threatened imminent contact even though [it was not carried out / it came before the grab].
Conditional threat: The fact that [D]’s threat was conditional (“[come with me or I’ll…]”) does not prevent it being an assault (Rozsa v Samuels).
“Not scared” counter-argument: [D] may argue [P] was [not frightened / laughed]. However, under Brady v Schatzel whether [P] was actually afraid is immaterial; the test is reasonable apprehension.
(2)–(4): The threat was direct, deliberate and made without consent or lawful justification, as [P] posed no threat to [D] (so self-defence cannot apply).
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] committed assault against [P].

3.5 False imprisonment

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of false imprisonment?
LAW
False imprisonment is the complete deprivation of a person’s freedom of movement. A person commits false imprisonment if: (1) they cause another person to be totally restrained, meaning there is no reasonable avenue of escape; (2) the act is direct; (3) the act is intentional or negligent; and (4) there is no consent or lawful justification. Physical force is not required. In Symes v Mahon [1922] SASR 447, a police officer told the plaintiff he had a warrant and he had to accompany him to Adelaide; the plaintiff complied, was the wrong person, and successfully sued for false imprisonment. Restraint must be total: in Bird v Jones (1845), a person denied passage on one side of a bridge was merely “obstructed”, because a means of escape existed. That means of escape must be reasonable, without risk of injury or serious inconvenience. In Burton v Davies (1953), a hitch-hiker who was refused permission to leave a truck, and whose only escape was to jump from the moving vehicle, was falsely imprisoned.
APPLICATION
(1) Total restraint: [P] was [locked in / told she could not leave / escorted] for [duration].
No force needed (Symes): Even if [D] argues that [P] “chose” to follow, similar to Symes v Mahon, [P] only complied because [D] asserted authority (“[you must come with me]”). Submission to that assertion is restraint, not consent.
Escape route? (Bird v Jones vs Burton v Davies): [D] may argue [P] could have left by [the window / back door], so that, as in Bird v Jones, she was merely obstructed. However, unlike the bridge in Bird v Jones, where another route was freely available, the only exit here was [a window X m above the ground]. Like jumping from the moving truck in Burton v Davies, it carried a real risk of injury and was therefore not a reasonable means of escape.
(2)–(3): The restraint resulted directly from [D]’s deliberate act of [locking the door].
(4) Justification: [D] will argue it was justified in protecting its goods. Even if a brief detention to check [the alarm] was proportionate, any justification ended once [the search showed nothing], so the remaining [X minutes] was unjustified. [Note: the course materials do not set out statutory arrest powers; I assume none applies beyond these defences.]
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] falsely imprisoned [P] for [at least the X minutes after …].

3.6 Private nuisance

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of private nuisance?
LAW
Nuisance is an act by the defendant that indirectly interferes with the plaintiff’s use and enjoyment of land, and may be private or public. Unlike trespass, which is direct interference with land, nuisance is indirect interference with use and enjoyment. A person commits private nuisance if: (1) they interfere with another person’s use and enjoyment of private land as an indirect result of their action; (2) the other person has an interest in that land, such as an owner or tenant; (3) the other person suffers actual harm or damage; (4) the interference is intentional or reckless; and (5) the interference is sustained and unreasonable. In deciding unreasonableness, the court balances the right to undisturbed enjoyment of property against the right to undertake activities, considering the severity, duration, location, sensitivity, deliberateness or maliciousness of the interference and whether precautions were taken to minimise it.
APPLICATION · use the 6 factors as mini-headings
(1) Indirect interference: The [noise/smell/vibration] travels from [D]’s premises onto [P]’s land. It is not a physical entry, so it is nuisance rather than trespass, and it interferes with [P]’s use of the land for [business purpose].
(2) Interest in land: [P] is the [owner / tenant under a X-year lease], so has standing. [A customer/guest would NOT have standing.]
(3) Actual harm: [P] has suffered [$X lost bookings / complaints / refunds].
(4) Intentional or reckless: [D] knew of the interference after [complaints on DATES] and continued, which is at least reckless.
(5) Sustained and unreasonable: Severity: […]. Duration: [every night since…]. Location: for [D], the area is [a mixed-use / entertainment precinct], where some noise is expected; against, […]. Sensitivity: [D] may argue [P]’s business is especially sensitive; however […]. Deliberateness/malice: [D] [ignored complaints / turned the exhaust towards P]. Precautions: [D] refused [$X insulation]. On balance the interference is unreasonable.
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D]’s [noise/smell] is a private nuisance, and because it is ongoing [P] should seek an injunction as well as damages for [losses].

3.7 Defamation

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of defamation?
LAW
A person commits the tort of defamation if they publish to a third party, in spoken or written form, a statement about another person that would damage their reputation. The plaintiff must establish that: (1) the statement was defamatory; (2) the statement identified them; and (3) the statement was published to a third party. A statement is defamatory if it makes ordinary people think less of the plaintiff, or causes them to be shunned, ridiculed or excluded from society. The general test in Radio 2UE Sydney Pty Ltd v Chesterton [2009] is whether the esteem in which the person is held by the community is diminished, i.e. whether the imputation is likely to cause people to think less of the plaintiff. In Mirror Newspapers Ltd v World Hosts Pty Ltd (1979), a newspaper implied that a restaurant owner had gone bankrupt, when it was the manager who had; the High Court upheld the owner’s claim because the article indicated he was in financial trouble. Imputations of professional incompetence are likewise defamatory (Mirror Newspapers v Jools; Andrews v John Fairfax). The plaintiff need not be named, provided the statement can reasonably be identified as referring to them. Publication means communication to someone other than the plaintiff, in any form, and failure to remove defamatory material may itself give rise to liability. Defences include justification (substantial truth), absolute privilege (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 26), fair reporting, and honest opinion on a matter of public interest (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 31).
APPLICATION
(1) Defamatory: The statement “[quote]” conveys that [P] [is unhygienic / dishonest / insolvent / incompetent]. Applying Chesterton, an ordinary reader would think less of [P]. Similar to World Hosts, where an implication of financial trouble damaged a restaurant owner, here […] damages [P]’s trading reputation.
(2) Identification: Although [P] is not named, the reference to “[the new café on X St with the green awning]” means readers can reasonably identify [P]. [It is not a large class, since only one business fits.]
(3) Publication: The [review/post] was posted on [platform] and read by [X people / customers]. [D]’s refusal to remove it after [P]’s request may itself give rise to liability.
Defences: Justification fails because the statement is not substantially true ([evidence]). Honest opinion fails because [it is a statement of fact, not opinion / not honestly held / not substantially true]. Absolute privilege and fair reporting are irrelevant, as the statement was not made in parliamentary or judicial proceedings or in a report of them.
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] has defamed [P], and no defence is available.

3.8 Deceit

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of deceit?
LAW
A false statement made during contractual negotiations to induce the other party to contract is a misrepresentation; if it is fraudulent, the maker commits the tort of deceit. A person commits deceit if: (1) they make a statement of fact to another person knowing it is false; (2) they intend that it be relied upon; (3) the other person relies on it; and (4) the other person suffers harm as a result. A statement of opinion honestly and reasonably held is not deceit: in Bisset v Wilkinson (1927), a statement about the number of sheep a farm could hold was an honestly held opinion, and the maker was not liable.
APPLICATION
(1) Statement of fact known to be false: [D] stated “[…]”. Unlike the estimate in Bisset v Wilkinson, this is a verifiable fact ([serviced last month / never faulted / certified]), and [D] knew it was false because [evidence: invoice, prior repair, email].
(2) Intended reliance: It was said [during negotiations, in answer to P’s direct question] to persuade [P] to [buy / sign].
(3) Reliance: [P] relied on it, [paying $X without an inspection / signing the next day].
(4) Harm: [P] suffered [$X repair, $Y lost profits] as a result.
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] committed the tort of deceit and is liable for [P]’s resulting losses.

3.9 Passing off

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of passing off?
LAW
A defendant commits the tort of passing off if they misrepresent themselves or their product as having some kind of connection with the plaintiff. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant made a misrepresentation, expressly or by implication, that their goods or services were connected with the plaintiff or had the plaintiff’s endorsement or approval; (2) the misrepresentation was made in the course of a trade; and (3) the misrepresentation was intended to deceive potential purchasers. In Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989), an advertisement for shoes that parodied a scene from Paul Hogan’s film Crocodile Dundee was held to be passing off, because it implied Hogan’s association with the product. There is an overlap between passing off and s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law (misleading or deceptive conduct).
APPLICATION
(1) Misrepresentation of connection: By [using the name “…” / similar green logo / “as served at…”], [D] impliedly represented that its product is connected with or approved by [P]. Similar to Pacific Dunlop v Hogan, where an implied association with Hogan was enough, no express claim of endorsement is needed.
(2) In the course of trade: The [product] was sold from [D]’s [shop/website] for profit.
(3) Intended to deceive: The intention can be inferred from [the timing after P went viral / copying colours / D’s own words]. [Evidence of actual confusion: customers asking P about it.]
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] has committed passing off. [P] may also have a claim under s 18 ACL, but that falls outside Tort Law.

3.10 Vicarious liability (when an employee did it)

LAW
An employer may be liable for the acts or omissions of its employees. The court asks whether the act occurred as part of the employee’s authorised work (Century v Northern Ireland (1942)) and whether it occurred within the course of employment (Deatons v Flew (1949)).
APPLICATION
[Employee] was employed by [Business] as [a security guard], and [stopping and questioning suspected shoplifters] is exactly the work they were authorised to do. Although the manner of doing it ([grabbing, locking the door]) was wrongful, it was an unauthorised way of doing authorised work, done during their shift and for the employer’s benefit, rather than a purely personal act outside the course of employment.
CONCLUSION
Therefore [Business] is likely to be vicariously liable for [Employee]’s torts, and [P] should sue [Business].

3.11 Remedies paragraph (always finish with this)

LAW
The typical remedy in tort is an award of damages, the objective being to restore the plaintiff, so far as money can, to the position they would have been in had the tort not been committed. Ordinary damages are assessed once and for all, so future losses must be estimated. Nominal damages are awarded where a legal right is breached but no compensable damage is suffered, as in trespass to land. Aggravated damages compensate for humiliation or emotional distress, as in defamation. Exemplary damages may be awarded where the defendant deliberately and maliciously disregarded the plaintiff’s rights. An injunction, a court order requiring a person to do or refrain from doing certain acts, is appropriate where the tort is ongoing, such as nuisance, trespass or defamation, and an interim injunction may restrain the activity until the court makes a final decision.
APPLICATION
[P] should claim ordinary damages for [medical costs $X, lost wages $Y, lost profits $Z]; aggravated damages for [the humiliation of being detained in front of customers / distress]; and [exemplary damages because D acted deliberately and maliciously by…]. As the [nuisance / defamatory post / encroachment] is ongoing, [P] should seek an interim injunction now and a final injunction [requiring removal / restraining the noise after 10pm].

Practice Question A · Bargain Barn and the false alarm (trespass to the person)

Bargain Barn Pty Ltd operates a discount department store in Chermside, Brisbane. It employs Trent as an in-store security guard, and his duties include monitoring the exit alarm and questioning customers suspected of shoplifting.

On a busy Saturday, Mei, a 34-year-old nurse, walks out through the exit gates carrying her handbag and a jacket she had bought at another shopping centre that morning. The alarm sounds. Trent walks up behind her and taps her on the shoulder, saying “Excuse me, madam.” Mei turns and says, “Sorry, I’m late for my shift,” and keeps walking. Trent steps in front of her, raises his clenched fist to chest height and says loudly, “Come back inside quietly or I’ll put you on the floor.” Mei later says she was “more embarrassed than frightened.” Trent then grabs Mei’s wrist and pulls her about 20 metres through the store, in front of dozens of shoppers, into a small back office. He takes her handbag and empties it onto the desk. Nothing from Bargain Barn is found. It is later discovered that the jacket still had another retailer’s security tag on it.

Trent then tells Mei: “You stay here until my manager gets back from lunch. You’re not going anywhere.” He locks the office door from the outside. The only other exit is a small window that opens onto the loading dock three metres below. Mei is held for 90 minutes before the manager returns and lets her go.

Mei has bruising to her wrist (her GP visit cost $120), missed her shift ($420 in lost wages), and says she has been humiliated, as several of her patients’ families saw her being dragged through the store.

Advise Mei whether she can bring an action under Tort Law against Bargain Barn. 25 marks

Answer plan (5 lines)
  1. Opener: battery, assault, false imprisonment (+ trespass to goods briefly) → defences → vicarious liability → remedies.
  2. Battery: tap = Rixon (not battery) vs wrist-grab and drag = battery (Battiato); defence of property not proportionate.
  3. Assault: fist + conditional threat = Stephens v Myers, Rozsa; “not frightened” = Brady v Schatzel.
  4. False imprisonment: locked office; window = Burton v Davies not Bird v Jones; Symes; justification ends after search.
  5. Vicarious liability (Century, Deatons); remedies: ordinary ($120 + $420), aggravated (humiliation), possibly exemplary.

Whether Mei can recover against Bargain Barn under Tort Law depends upon whether its security guard, Trent, committed the torts of battery, assault and false imprisonment (and, briefly, trespass to goods), whether any defence applies, and whether Bargain Barn is vicariously liable for Trent’s conduct.

Margin note: the opener lists every issue in the order you’ll deal with them. Markers like seeing the whole map up front.

Trespass to the person: general principles

A tort is a civil wrong that allows the aggrieved person to sue the wrongdoer for damages. Trespass to the person is an intentional tort, and it takes three forms: battery, assault and false imprisonment. Trespass is “actionable per se”, so Mei does not need to prove actual damage. Each form requires an interference that is direct, intentional or negligent, and without consent or lawful justification.

Issue 1: Battery

Issue: Can Mei bring an action against Bargain Barn for the tort of battery?

Law: A person commits battery if (1) they cause some physical interference with another person’s body, (2) the act is direct, (3) the act is intentional or negligent, and (4) there is no consent or lawful justification. In Battiato v Lagana [1992], Moynihan J stated that “the direct intentional imposition of any unwanted physical contact on another person constitutes the tort of Battery”, and that there is no requirement to prove physical harm. However, physical contact that is “generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of everyday life” is excepted (In re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation) [1990] 2 AC 1, Lord Goff). In Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd [2001], a banned patron was confronted by a casino employee, who placed his hand on Rixon’s shoulder to get his attention and detained him until police arrived. The court held that the contact was generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life, and Star City was not liable in battery. Defences to trespass include consent and defence of property, which requires that the trespass was reasonably necessary to protect the defendant’s goods from imminent harm by the plaintiff and was proportionate to the threat.

Application:

The shoulder tap. Trent’s initial tap on Mei’s shoulder while saying “Excuse me, madam” is almost identical to the contact in Rixon. It was made only to get Mei’s attention after the alarm sounded and is the kind of contact generally acceptable in everyday life. The tap alone is therefore unlikely to be a battery.

The wrist grab and drag. Trent’s later conduct is quite different. He physically interfered with Mei’s body by grabbing her wrist and pulling her about 20 metres through the store. Following Battiato v Lagana, this unwanted contact is a battery whether or not it caused harm, although here it also caused bruising. Bargain Barn may argue that, as in Rixon, the contact was a necessary part of detaining a suspect. However, Rixon is distinguishable: there the employee merely rested a hand on the patron’s shoulder, while Trent forcibly gripped and dragged Mei. That goes well beyond contact generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life. The contact was direct (Trent’s own hand) and plainly intentional.

Consent and lawful justification. Mei did not consent; she had already said she was leaving for her shift. Bargain Barn may argue defence of property, since the alarm gave Trent grounds to suspect theft. However, that defence requires the force to be reasonably necessary and proportionate to an imminent threat. Mei was not running, resisting or acting aggressively, and Trent could have asked her to show her bag or simply checked the jacket’s tag at the gate. Dragging her 20 metres was disproportionate. The facts don’t mention any statutory power of arrest, and the course materials don’t address one, so I assume no further justification is available.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Trent’s shoulder tap was not a battery, but his grabbing and dragging of Mei was.

Margin note: arguing “for” with Rixon and then distinguishing it is exactly the case-comparison move the W7 exemplar rewards.

Issue 2: Assault

Issue: Can Mei bring an action against Bargain Barn for the tort of assault?

Law: A person commits assault if (1) they cause another person to develop a reasonable apprehension of direct, imminent and harmful or offensive physical contact, (2) the act is direct, (3) the act is intentional or negligent, and (4) there is no consent or lawful justification. No physical contact needs to occur. In Brady v Schatzel [1911] it was held that it is not material that the person assaulted was actually put in fear, as otherwise the question would depend on whether the victim was a courageous or timid person. If the defendant makes carrying out a threat conditional on the plaintiff complying with a demand, the tort is still committed (Rozsa v Samuels [1969]). In Stephens v Myers (1830), a defendant who advanced towards the plaintiff with a clenched fist, intending to strike, but was stopped before reaching him, was liable in assault.

Application: Trent stepped in front of Mei, raised his clenched fist to chest height and said loudly, “Come back inside quietly or I’ll put you on the floor.” Similar to Stephens v Myers, where advancing with a clenched fist was enough, Trent was face-to-face with Mei and capable of carrying out the threat immediately. A reasonable person in Mei’s position would apprehend imminent harmful contact. Bargain Barn may argue the threat was conditional, since Trent would only act if Mei refused to come back. However, under Rozsa v Samuels, a threat conditional on the plaintiff complying with a demand is still an assault. Bargain Barn may also rely on Mei’s comment that she was “more embarrassed than frightened.” Under Brady v Schatzel this is immaterial: the test is whether the apprehension of contact was reasonable, not whether Mei was actually afraid. The threat was direct and intentional, and Mei posed no physical threat to Trent, so self-defence cannot apply.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Trent committed assault against Mei.

Issue 3: False imprisonment

Issue: Can Mei bring an action against Bargain Barn for the tort of false imprisonment?

Law: A person commits false imprisonment if (1) they cause another person to be totally restrained, meaning there is no reasonable avenue of escape, (2) the act is direct, (3) the act is intentional or negligent, and (4) there is no consent or lawful justification. Physical force is not necessary. In Symes v Mahon [1922] SASR 447, a police officer told the plaintiff he had a warrant for his arrest and he had to accompany him to Adelaide. The plaintiff did so, was found not to be the right person, and successfully sued for false imprisonment. The restraint must be total: in Bird v Jones (1845), a person denied passage on one side of a bridge was held to be merely “obstructed”, because the means of escape must be reasonable, without risk of injury or serious inconvenience. In Burton v Davies (1953), a hitch-hiker who was groped and refused permission to leave a truck, and whose only means of escape was to jump from the moving vehicle, was falsely imprisoned, because that escape carried a risk of injury.

Application:

Total restraint. Mei was dragged into the office, told “You’re not going anywhere,” and locked in for 90 minutes. Even before the door was locked, Trent’s assertion that she must stay is, as in Symes v Mahon, a restraint, because Mei submitted to an assertion of authority and not by her own free choice.

Means of escape. Bargain Barn may argue that, as in Bird v Jones, Mei was not totally restrained because she could have left through the window. However, Bird v Jones is distinguishable: the plaintiff there could simply take another route across the bridge without danger. Here the only alternative exit was a small window three metres above a loading dock. Like jumping from the moving truck in Burton v Davies, climbing out carried a clear risk of injury, so it was not a reasonable means of escape. Mei was therefore totally restrained.

Direct and intentional. Trent personally locked the door and told Mei she must stay, so the restraint was direct and deliberate.

Consent and lawful justification. Mei did not consent. Bargain Barn may argue that a short detention to investigate the alarm was justified to protect its goods. Even if a brief stop at the exit could be justified as proportionate, any justification ended once Trent emptied Mei’s handbag and found nothing from Bargain Barn. Locking her in for a further 90 minutes only because the manager was at lunch was not reasonably necessary to protect any goods and was disproportionate.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Mei was falsely imprisoned, at least for the 90 minutes after nothing was found in her handbag.

Issue 4: Trespass to goods (brief)

Issue: Can Mei bring an action against Bargain Barn for trespass to goods?

Law: Trespass to goods is a direct, intentional or negligent interference with goods in the plaintiff’s possession, such as taking or using them without permission, without consent or lawful justification. No damage need be proved.

Application: Trent took Mei’s handbag and emptied it onto the desk without her consent while she was being held, which is a direct and deliberate interference with goods in her possession.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, this is a trespass to goods, although because no damage was caused, only nominal damages are likely.

Issue 5: Is Bargain Barn vicariously liable?

Law: An employer may be liable for the acts or omissions of its employees, considering whether the act occurred as part of the employee’s authorised work (Century v Northern Ireland (1942)) and within the course of employment (Deatons v Flew (1949)).

Application: Trent is employed by Bargain Barn as a security guard, and monitoring the exit alarm and questioning suspected shoplifters is exactly the work he is authorised to do. Although he carried out that work in a wrongful way, he did so during his shift, in the store and to protect Bargain Barn’s stock. It was not a purely personal act unconnected with his job.

Conclusion: Bargain Barn is likely to be vicariously liable for Trent’s torts.

Remedies

The typical remedy in tort is damages, which aim to restore Mei, so far as money can, to the position she would have been in had the torts not been committed. Ordinary damages are assessed once and for all. Mei should claim ordinary damages for her GP visit ($120) and lost wages ($420). She should also claim aggravated damages, which compensate for humiliation and emotional distress: she was dragged through a busy store in front of dozens of shoppers, including families of her patients. Because Trent continued to hold Mei for 90 minutes after finding nothing, she may argue for exemplary damages on the basis that her rights were deliberately disregarded, although this is less certain, as the facts suggest carelessness rather than malice. Nominal damages are available for the trespass to her handbag.

Overall conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Mei can recover damages from Bargain Barn for battery, assault and false imprisonment (and nominally for trespass to goods), including aggravated damages for her humiliation.

Margin note: if the question had said Mei broke her wrist when Trent tackled her, you would add negligence as a separate Issue using T1, or simply bolt Battery A, Assault B and FI C onto a negligence answer.

Practice Question B · The brewery next door (private nuisance + trespass to land)

Riverside Retreat Pty Ltd owns and operates a 20-room boutique hotel on Mill Street, Toowong, which it has owned since 2015. The street is zoned mixed-use: there are cafés, offices and apartments, and Riverside Retreat promotes itself as a “quiet riverside escape.”

In February 2026, Hopworks Brewing Pty Ltd leased the old warehouse next door and opened a craft brewery and bar. Since March, Hopworks has hosted live bands on its outdoor deck every Thursday to Sunday until 1:00 am, with the bass clearly audible and the windows vibrating in guest rooms on that side of the hotel. Hopworks also vents its brewing exhaust through a fan pointed directly towards the hotel, and guests regularly complain of a strong, sour “boiled grain” smell in the courtyard during the day.

Riverside Retreat’s manager, Grace, complained in writing to Hopworks’ owner, Marco, in March, April and June. Marco replied: “This is a precinct, not a library. Your guests can buy earplugs.” An acoustic engineer quoted $6,000 for sound-insulating panels on the deck. Marco refused to pay for them.

In May, Hopworks installed a large illuminated sign on its wall which projects 40 centimetres over Riverside Retreat’s courtyard. Hopworks’ delivery drivers also routinely park their trucks on Riverside Retreat’s private driveway while unloading kegs, despite a “Private Property — Hotel Guests Only” sign.

Riverside Retreat has received 37 written complaints and refunded or lost bookings worth $38,500, and its online rating has dropped from 4.8 to 3.9 stars.

Advise Riverside Retreat of any actions it may have against Hopworks under Tort Law, and the remedies available. 25 marks

Answer plan (5 lines)
  1. Opener: private nuisance (noise; smell), trespass to land (sign; trucks), remedies. Public nuisance is not assessed, so leave it out.
  2. Nuisance: 5 elements; then 6 factors (severity, duration, location (both sides), sensitivity (both sides), deliberateness, precautions).
  3. Trespass to land: sign = Kelsen (40cm > 4 inches); trucks = direct entry; no consent (sign + complaints).
  4. Defences: none fit (no consent, no necessity).
  5. Remedies: damages $38,500 (ordinary), nominal for trespass if no loss, interim + final injunctions (ongoing torts).

Whether Riverside Retreat can take action against Hopworks under Tort Law depends upon two issues: whether Hopworks’ noise and exhaust odour amount to a private nuisance, and whether its overhanging sign and the parking of its trucks amount to trespass to land. If so, the remaining question is what remedies are available.

Issue 1: Private nuisance

Issue: Can Riverside Retreat bring an action against Hopworks for the tort of private nuisance?

Law: Nuisance is an act by the defendant that indirectly interferes with the plaintiff’s use and enjoyment of private or public land. It differs from trespass, which is a direct interference with the land itself. (Public nuisance is not relevant here.) A person commits private nuisance if: (1) they interfere with another person’s use and enjoyment of private land as an indirect result of their actions; (2) the other person has an interest in that land, such as an owner or tenant; (3) the other person suffers actual harm or damage; (4) the interference is intentional or reckless; and (5) the interference is sustained and unreasonable. Because there must be a balance between the right to undisturbed enjoyment of property and the right to undertake activities, the court considers the severity, duration, location, sensitivity, and deliberateness or maliciousness of the interference, and whether any precautions were taken to minimise it.

Margin note: no nuisance case is named in the course slides, so the marks come from working through the six balancing factors with the facts, on both sides.

Application:

(1) Indirect interference with use and enjoyment. The band noise, the vibrations and the brewing odour all start on Hopworks’ premises and travel onto Riverside Retreat’s land. Nothing physically enters the land, so this is indirect interference and properly nuisance rather than trespass. It interferes with Riverside Retreat’s use of its land as a hotel: guests can’t sleep in the rooms on that side, and the courtyard is unpleasant during the day.

(2) Interest in land. Riverside Retreat has owned the hotel since 2015, so it has a clear interest in the land. Note that individual guests have no interest in the land and could not sue in nuisance themselves.

(3) Actual harm. Unlike trespass, nuisance requires actual harm. Riverside Retreat has suffered $38,500 in refunded and lost bookings, 37 written complaints and a fall in its online rating from 4.8 to 3.9.

(4) Intentional or reckless. Hopworks deliberately schedules late bands and deliberately points its exhaust fan towards the hotel. In any case, after Grace’s written complaints in March, April and June, Hopworks knew of the interference and continued, which is at least reckless.

(5) Sustained and unreasonable.

  • Severity: the bass is “clearly audible” inside guest rooms and makes the windows vibrate, and the smell is described as strong. These are serious interferences, not trivial annoyances.
  • Duration: the noise occurs four nights every week until 1:00 am and has continued since March; the odour occurs regularly during the day. The interference is sustained, not a one-off event.
  • Location: Hopworks will argue, as Marco said, that Mill Street is a mixed-use “precinct” with cafés and bars, where residents and businesses must accept some noise. This is Hopworks’ strongest argument. However, the precinct also contains apartments and a hotel that has operated since 2015. Even in a mixed-use area, amplified bass until 1:00 am four nights a week goes beyond the ordinary noise expected there.
  • Sensitivity: Hopworks may argue that a hotel marketed as a “quiet riverside escape” is unusually sensitive to noise. However, needing to sleep at night is not an abnormal sensitivity: any residential neighbour would be affected in the same way. The odour also affects the courtyard during the day, which isn’t a matter of special sensitivity.
  • Deliberateness or maliciousness: Marco’s dismissive response (“Your guests can buy earplugs”) and the decision to keep the exhaust fan pointed at the hotel show a deliberate disregard for Riverside Retreat’s interests.
  • Precautions: Hopworks took no precautions at all. It refused a $6,000 insulation solution, which is modest compared with the $38,500 loss caused, and did nothing to redirect its exhaust.

Balancing these factors, although Hopworks has a legitimate right to run a brewery in a mixed-use area, the severity, duration, deliberateness and complete absence of precautions outweigh the location argument. The interference is therefore sustained and unreasonable.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Hopworks’ late-night noise and exhaust odour constitute a private nuisance against Riverside Retreat.

Issue 2: Trespass to land

Issue: Can Riverside Retreat bring an action against Hopworks for the tort of trespass to land?

Law: Trespass to land occurs where the defendant unlawfully enters, remains on, or puts something on the plaintiff’s land. A person commits trespass to land if: (1) they interfere with another person’s exclusive possession of land; (2) the interference is a direct result of the defendant’s actions; (3) the interference is intentional or, though not intended, negligent (a voluntary act); and (4) there is no consent or lawful justification. Trespass is actionable per se, so no damage needs to be proved. In Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco [1957] 2 QB 334, Imperial Tobacco erected a sign that protruded four inches into the airspace above premises it had leased to Kelsen. The court held this was a trespass and granted an injunction to prevent it. Defences include consent and necessity, i.e. that the trespass was necessary to protect life, land or goods from imminent and real harm.

Application:

(a) The illuminated sign. Riverside Retreat, as owner, has exclusive possession of its courtyard and the airspace above it. Hopworks’ sign projects 40 centimetres over the courtyard. Similar to Kelsen, where a protrusion of only four inches was a trespass, this is an interference with Riverside Retreat’s airspace, and at 40 centimetres it is a much larger intrusion than in Kelsen. The interference is direct, because Hopworks itself installed the sign, and intentional, because it chose the sign’s size and position. Even if Hopworks did not realise the sign crossed the boundary, installing it was a voluntary act, so it was at least negligent.

(b) The delivery trucks. Hopworks’ drivers physically enter and park on Riverside Retreat’s private driveway while unloading. This is a direct entry onto the land, and it is deliberate. Riverside Retreat may need to show the drivers are Hopworks’ employees, or are acting on its instructions, for Hopworks to be responsible; the facts call them “Hopworks’ delivery drivers”, so I assume they are employees acting in the course of their employment (Deatons v Flew (1949)).

(c) Consent or lawful justification. Riverside Retreat never consented. The “Private Property — Hotel Guests Only” sign expressly denies permission, and Grace’s written complaints show clear objection. Necessity does not apply: parking on the driveway for convenience is not needed to protect life, land or goods from imminent and real harm.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, both the overhanging sign and the parking of delivery trucks constitute trespass to Riverside Retreat’s land.

Remedies

The typical remedy in tort is an award of damages, the objective being to restore Riverside Retreat, so far as money can, to the position it would have been in had the torts not been committed.

  • Ordinary damages (nuisance): Riverside Retreat should claim the $38,500 in refunded and lost bookings. Because damages are assessed once and for all, it should also ask the court to estimate future lost bookings flowing from its reduced online rating.
  • Nominal damages (trespass): the sign and the truck parking may not have caused measurable financial loss. Because trespass is actionable per se, Riverside Retreat is still entitled to at least nominal damages for the breach of its rights.
  • Exemplary damages: Riverside Retreat could argue that Marco deliberately disregarded its rights by refusing modest precautions and mocking its complaints. However, punishment is generally not the purpose of tort damages, so this is less likely to succeed.
  • Injunctions: the most important remedy here, because every tort in this question is ongoing. The slides note an injunction is appropriate where a defendant commits a tort such as nuisance or trespass on an ongoing basis. Riverside Retreat should seek an interim injunction immediately to restrain the amplified music after a reasonable hour and redirect the exhaust until trial, and then a final injunction (i) restraining the nuisance, (ii) requiring removal or repositioning of the sign, as was granted in Kelsen, and (iii) restraining Hopworks’ vehicles from entering the driveway. Failure to comply would expose Hopworks to civil or criminal penalties.

Overall conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Riverside Retreat can succeed against Hopworks in private nuisance and trespass to land, recovering its $38,500 loss (plus nominal damages for the trespass) and obtaining injunctions to stop the noise and odour, remove the sign and keep the trucks off its driveway.

Practice Question C · The coffee war (defamation + deceit + passing off)

Priya owns Bean There Café, which opened in January 2026 on Main Street, Paddington, in a shopfront with a distinctive green awning. Two doors down is Coastline Coffee Roasters, owned by Dan, which had been the only café on the block for ten years.

In February, Priya was looking for a second espresso machine. Dan offered to sell her his spare machine for $14,000. When Priya asked whether it had any problems, Dan said, “It was fully serviced last month and has never had a single fault.” In fact, Dan knew the boiler had cracked in December and had been patched with sealant, and it had not been serviced for two years. Relying on Dan’s statement, Priya paid $14,000 without getting it inspected. In April the boiler failed: repairs cost $5,200 and Priya had to close for four days, losing $6,000 in profits.

In March, a TikTok video of Bean There’s pistachio latte went viral. Business boomed. Dan then created a Google account under the name “Local Mum Kate” and posted this review of Bean There: “Got food poisoning after a latte at the new café on Main St with the green awning. Staff told me the owner buys out-of-date milk to save money. Disgusting — avoid!” Dan had never been a customer, and a council health inspection in March gave Bean There a perfect score. The review was read more than 4,000 times and shared in a local Facebook group, and Bean There’s weekly takings dropped by 30%. When Priya’s lawyer asked Google and “Local Mum Kate” to remove it, Dan refused to delete it.

In May, Dan began selling bags of coffee beans called “The Bean There Blend” in green packaging similar to Bean There’s awning colour, with the words “The pistachio latte blend Main St is talking about.” Several customers have since asked Priya whether she now sells her beans through Coastline.

Advise Priya of the actions she may bring against Dan under Tort Law. 25 marks

Answer plan (5 lines)
  1. Opener: deceit (machine), defamation (review), passing off (blend), then remedies.
  2. Deceit: known false fact (serviced, no faults), distinguish Bisset; intended reliance; reliance (no inspection); harm $5,200 + $6,000.
  3. Defamation: defamatory (Chesterton, World Hosts), identified (green awning), published (4,000 + shares; refusal to remove); defences fail (justification, honest opinion).
  4. Passing off: implied connection (Pacific Dunlop v Hogan), trade, intent to deceive (timing, colour, actual confusion); flag s 18 ACL.
  5. Remedies: ordinary, aggravated (defamation), exemplary (malicious fake review), injunctions (remove review, stop the blend).

Priya has three potential actions against Dan under Tort Law: the tort of deceit in relation to the espresso machine, the tort of defamation in relation to the Google review, and the tort of passing off in relation to “The Bean There Blend.” Each is considered in turn, followed by remedies.

Issue 1: Deceit

Issue: Can Priya bring an action against Dan for the tort of deceit?

Law: If a person makes a false statement during contractual negotiations to induce the other party to contract, they make a misrepresentation; if the misrepresentation is fraudulent, they commit the tort of deceit. A person commits deceit if: (1) they make a statement of fact to another person knowing that it is false; (2) they make the statement intending that it be relied upon; (3) the other person relies on the statement; and (4) the other person suffers harm as a result. In Bisset v Wilkinson (1927), a seller’s statement about the number of sheep a farm could hold was held to be an opinion honestly and reasonably held, not a statement of fact, and the seller was not liable.

Application:

(1) Statement of fact known to be false. Dan said the machine “was fully serviced last month and has never had a single fault.” Unlike the estimate of the farm’s carrying capacity in Bisset v Wilkinson, which the seller couldn’t know for certain, Dan’s statement concerned past, verifiable facts within his own knowledge: whether the machine was serviced and whether it had faulted. It was false on both counts. The boiler had cracked in December and the machine had not been serviced for two years. Dan knew this, because he arranged the sealant patch himself. It cannot be characterised as an honestly held opinion.

(2) Intention that it be relied on. The statement was made during negotiations for the sale, in direct answer to Priya’s question about problems with the machine. It can be inferred that Dan intended Priya to rely on it in deciding to buy.

(3) Reliance. Priya relied on the statement, paying $14,000 without having the machine inspected. Dan may argue she should have had it inspected, but the facts show she didn’t because of his assurance.

(4) Harm. As a result, Priya suffered $5,200 in repair costs and $6,000 in lost profits from the four-day closure.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Dan committed the tort of deceit and is liable for Priya’s resulting losses.

Issue 2: Defamation

Issue: Can Priya bring an action against Dan for the tort of defamation?

Law: A person commits the tort of defamation if they publish to a third party, in spoken or written form, a statement about another person that would damage their reputation. The plaintiff must establish that: (1) the statement was defamatory; (2) the statement identified them; and (3) the statement was published to a third party. A statement is defamatory if it makes ordinary people think less of the plaintiff, or causes them to be shunned, ridiculed or excluded from society. The general test in Radio 2UE Sydney Pty Ltd v Chesterton [2009] is whether “the esteem in which that person is held by the community is diminished in some respect”. In Mirror Newspapers Ltd v World Hosts Pty Ltd (1979), a newspaper implied that a restaurant owner had gone bankrupt, when in fact it was the restaurant’s manager; the High Court upheld the owner’s claim because the article indicated he was in financial trouble. The plaintiff need not be named, as long as the statement can reasonably be identified as referring to them. Publication means communication to someone other than the plaintiff in any form, and failure to remove defamatory material may by itself give rise to liability. Defences include justification (substantial truth); absolute privilege for statements in parliamentary or judicial proceedings (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 26); fair reporting of proceedings of public concern; and honest opinion on a matter of public interest that is substantially true (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 31(1)–(6)).

Application:

(1) Defamatory. The review conveys that Bean There’s coffee causes food poisoning and that Priya deliberately buys out-of-date milk to save money, i.e. that she is unhygienic and dishonest towards customers. Applying Chesterton, these imputations would plainly diminish the esteem in which ordinary members of the community hold Priya and her café, and would cause people to shun it. Similar to World Hosts, where an implication that a restaurant owner was in financial trouble was defamatory, here the imputation goes directly to Priya’s fitness to run a food business. The 30% drop in takings confirms that customers think less of her.

(2) Identification. Priya and Bean There are not named. However, the review refers to “the new café on Main St with the green awning” and to “the owner.” Bean There is the only new café on that part of Main Street with a green awning, and Priya is its owner, so ordinary readers, especially locals, would reasonably identify her. This is not a large class of persons: only one business fits the description.

(3) Publication. The review was posted publicly on Google, read more than 4,000 times and shared in a local Facebook group, so it was clearly communicated to third parties. Further, Dan’s refusal to delete the review after the lawyer’s request may by itself give rise to liability.

Defences. Justification fails, because the statements are not substantially true: Dan was never a customer, and the council inspection gave Bean There a perfect score. Honest opinion fails for three reasons: the review states facts (food poisoning, out-of-date milk) rather than opinion; it is not honestly held, as Dan invented it under a false name; and it is not substantially true. Absolute privilege and fair reporting are irrelevant, as the review was not made in parliamentary or judicial proceedings or in a report of proceedings of public concern.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Dan has defamed Priya and no defence is available.

Margin note: name each defence and dismiss it with a fact. That shows the marker you know the full list without wasting time.

Issue 3: Passing off

Issue: Can Priya bring an action against Dan for the tort of passing off?

Law: A defendant commits the tort of passing off if they misrepresent themselves or their product as having some kind of connection with the plaintiff. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant made a misrepresentation, expressly or by implication, that their goods were connected with the plaintiff or had the plaintiff’s endorsement or approval; (2) the misrepresentation was made in the course of a trade; and (3) the misrepresentation was intended to deceive potential purchasers. In Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989), a shoe advertisement parodying a well-known scene from Paul Hogan’s film Crocodile Dundee was held to be passing off, because it implied that Hogan was associated with the product. Passing off overlaps with liability under s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law for misleading or deceptive conduct.

Application:

(1) Misrepresentation of connection. By naming his product “The Bean There Blend”, packaging it in green similar to Bean There’s awning, and describing it as “the pistachio latte blend Main St is talking about” (a direct reference to Bean There’s viral latte), Dan impliedly represents that his beans are the same beans Bean There uses, or that Bean There has approved or supplied them. Similar to Pacific Dunlop v Hogan, where there was no express claim of endorsement but the association was clearly implied, the overall impression here is of a connection with Priya’s café. Dan may argue that he never expressly said Bean There supplied or endorsed the beans; however, a misrepresentation may be made by implication.

(2) In the course of trade. Dan sells the beans commercially through Coastline Coffee Roasters, so the misrepresentation is made in the course of trade.

(3) Intention to deceive. Dan’s intention can be inferred from the timing (launched shortly after Bean There went viral), the deliberate use of Priya’s business name and colour, and the reference to the pistachio latte. Dan’s earlier conduct towards Priya, including the fake review, also suggests a deliberate strategy to take her goodwill. The fact that several customers have asked Priya whether she now sells her beans through Coastline shows that potential purchasers are in fact being deceived.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Dan has committed the tort of passing off. Priya may also have a claim under s 18 ACL, but as the question is limited to Tort Law, that is not considered further.

Remedies

The typical remedy in tort is damages, the objective being to restore Priya, so far as money can, to the position she would have been in had the torts not been committed. Damages are assessed once and for all.

  • Deceit: ordinary damages of $5,200 (repairs) and $6,000 (lost profits during closure).
  • Defamation: ordinary damages for the lost takings caused by the 30% drop, with future losses estimated; aggravated damages, which the slides identify as appropriate for defamation, to compensate for Priya’s humiliation and distress; and arguably exemplary damages, as Dan deliberately and maliciously disregarded Priya’s rights by inventing a fake identity, fabricating a food-poisoning claim and refusing to remove it.
  • Passing off: damages for any sales diverted from Bean There.
  • Injunctions: because the defamation and passing off are ongoing, Priya should seek an interim injunction immediately requiring Dan to remove the review and to stop selling “The Bean There Blend”, followed by final injunctions. Failure to comply would expose Dan to civil or criminal penalties.

Overall conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Priya can succeed against Dan in deceit, defamation and passing off, recovering damages (including aggravated and possibly exemplary damages for the defamation) and obtaining injunctions to remove the review and stop the copycat blend.

Copy-ready Law paragraphs (short form)

Trespass (general): Trespass is an intentional tort committed when D interferes with the person or property of P: to land, goods or the person. It is actionable per se, so P need not prove damage. It requires an interference that is direct, intentional or negligent, and without consent or lawful justification.
Land: D must interfere with P’s exclusive possession of land by entering, remaining on or putting something on it, including airspace. In Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco a sign protruding 4 inches into P’s airspace was a trespass, and an injunction was granted.
Goods: Interference with goods in P’s possession, e.g. taking or using them without permission. P need only have rightful possession, not ownership, and need not prove damage.
Battery: Physical interference with P’s body. “The direct intentional imposition of any unwanted physical contact… constitutes… Battery”; no harm is required (Battiato v Lagana). Contact “generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of everyday life” is excepted (In re F). In Rixon v Star City a hand on a banned patron’s shoulder to get his attention was not battery.
Assault: Causing a reasonable apprehension of direct, imminent, harmful or offensive contact. No contact is needed. Actual fear is immaterial, otherwise it would depend on whether P was courageous or timid (Brady v Schatzel). A conditional threat still counts (Rozsa v Samuels). Advancing with a clenched fist was assault in Stephens v Myers.
False imprisonment: Total restraint with no reasonable avenue of escape. In Symes v Mahon P accompanied police on a mistaken warrant and was falsely imprisoned (no force needed). In Bird v Jones P blocked from one side of a bridge was merely obstructed: escape must be reasonable, without risk of injury or serious inconvenience. In Burton v Davies the only escape was jumping from a moving truck, so P was imprisoned.
Private nuisance: D’s act indirectly interferes with P’s use and enjoyment of private land (unlike trespass, which is direct). P needs an interest in the land, actual harm, intentional or reckless interference, and interference that is sustained and unreasonable. The court balances undisturbed enjoyment against D’s right to undertake activities, considering severity, duration, location, sensitivity, deliberateness or malice, and precautions.
Defamation: Publishing to a third party a statement about P that would damage their reputation. P must prove it was (1) defamatory: esteem diminished (Radio 2UE v Chesterton); in World Hosts an implication that a restaurant owner was bankrupt was enough; (2) identified P (need not be named); (3) published (any form; failure to remove can create liability). Defences: justification, absolute privilege (s 26), fair reporting, honest opinion (s 31) (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld)).
Deceit: A fraudulent misrepresentation. D makes a statement of fact knowing it is false, intending reliance; P relies on it and suffers harm. An opinion honestly and reasonably held is not deceit (Bisset v Wilkinson: the sheep-capacity statement was not deceit).
Passing off: D misrepresents (expressly or impliedly) a connection with or endorsement by P, in the course of trade, intending to deceive purchasers (Pacific Dunlop v Hogan: an implied Crocodile Dundee association was enough). Overlaps s 18 ACL and unregistered trade marks (T5).

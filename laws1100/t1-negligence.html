① Learn it in 20 minutes

What negligence is

A tort is a civil wrong ("tort" means "wrong"). It lets the injured person sue the wrongdoer for damages, which is money to compensate for personal injury, property damage or economic loss. A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another person. Most harm in society comes from carelessness rather than intent, so negligence is by far the most common tort.

A negligence claim has 3 stages, and then two more things to check:

  1. Duty of care. Did the defendant (D) owe the plaintiff (P) a duty of care?
  2. Breach. Did D fall below the standard of the reasonable person? Use s 9 of the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) (CLA).
  3. Causation. Did the breach actually cause P's harm, and is that harm not too remote? Use s 11 CLA. The lecturer calls this stage "by far the most challenging".

Then: Defences? (contributory negligence, voluntary assumption of risk/obvious risk) and Remedies? (damages).

Each state has its own civil liability statute (NSW CLA 2002, Vic Wrongs Act 1958, etc.). Your exam facts are set in Qld, so cite the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld).

Stage 1: Duty of care

Starting point: Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562. Mrs Donoghue drank a ginger beer that her friend had bought at a café. A decomposed snail floated out of the opaque bottle, and she suffered nervous shock and gastro-enteritis. She had no contract with the manufacturer. Held (majority: Lords Atkin, Thankerton and Macmillan) that the manufacturer owed her, as the ultimate consumer, a duty of care. Lord Atkin stated the neighbour principle: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour". Your neighbours are "persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation". The question it poses is whether it was reasonably foreseeable that D's conduct could harm someone in P's position.

A. Established categories (the usual exam route)

If the relationship is a recognised one, duty is straightforward. Examples: motorist → other road users; doctor → patient; accountant → client; banker → client; manufacturer → consumer (Donoghue v Stevenson); occupier → people coming onto the premises (Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987)); employer → employees. An occupier can even owe a duty to people who are on the premises without permission (Hackshaw v Shaw (1984)).

Even when the duty is established, P still has to prove breach and causation.

B. Public authorities (councils, government bodies)

Public authorities often control parks, roads, beaches and waterways, so they act like occupiers. It is questionable whether they automatically owe a duty. The key question is whether there is a hidden risk that required a warning or prevention, or whether the risk was so obvious to a reasonable person that no duty exists.

  • Nagle v Rottnest Island Authority (1993): a submerged rock at a designated swimming area with a "Swimming area" sign, which encouraged people to swim there. This was a hidden risk, and the authority owed a duty to warn.
  • Swain v Waverley (2005): a submerged sandbar at a patrolled surf beach, between the flags. A surfer dived into the waves. This was a hidden risk, so there was a duty to warn.
  • Romeo v Conservation Commission (1998): an unfenced cliff top in a public space. P argued there should have been a "don't go near the edge / loose rocks" sign. The risk was obvious, so the authority was not liable.
  • Vairy v Wyong Shire Council (2005): P dived from a natural rock platform at an unpatrolled beach with no signs. The risk was obvious, so the council was not liable.

The slides set Romeo and Vairy up as questions ("Was there a hidden risk?"). The outcomes given here (no liability because the risks were obvious) are the actual decisions and fit the slide's contrast with Nagle and Swain.

C. No established category (novel duty)

Use 2 tests. (1) Reasonable foreseeability of harm to someone in P's position (the Donoghue neighbour principle). (2) Are the salient features consistent with a duty? Salient features: the relationship between the parties, control, relative knowledge, experience, vulnerability and reliance, and personal responsibility. Weigh all the relevant ones.

Feedback (Tut Sub 2): students "seemed particularly confused about novel duties of care … an area that is not relevant to this question". Only use salient features if there is NO established category.

D. Negligent misstatement (a sub-type of duty, for advice cases)

A person giving advice may owe a duty if (1) the advice is of a business or serious nature; (2) they know or should know the other person intends to rely on it; and (3) it is reasonable for that person to rely on it. The adviser does not have to be a professional. If D holds themselves out as having special skill, reasonableness is easier to satisfy.

  • Hedley Byrne v Heller (1964): a bank gave a careless credit reference. There was no liability only because of a disclaimer. In obiter, the House of Lords said a duty was owed because of D's expertise and D's knowledge that the reference would be relied on.
  • Rentokill v Channon (1990): an incorrect pest inspection report was relied on to buy a house, which then had to be demolished. Held: a duty was owed and breached.
  • Third parties: Esanda Finance v Peat Marwick (1997). An adviser generally owes no duty to a third party who relies on the advice. The exception is where the adviser knew or should have known (i) the advice would be passed to the third party, (ii) it was likely to lead them into a particular type of transaction, and (iii) they would likely suffer financial loss if it was wrong.

Stage 2: Breach (s 9 CLA)

A duty existing does not mean it was breached. s 9(1): D breaches their duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if:

  1. the risk was foreseeable (D knew or ought reasonably to have known of it);
  2. the risk was not insignificant; and
  3. in the circumstances a reasonable person in D's position would have taken those precautions.

The reasonable person at common law is someone of normal intelligence, with the perception of the surrounding circumstances and the knowledge of other pertinent matters that the relevant person would normally have.

s 9(2) lists the factors that decide what a reasonable person would have done under s 9(1)(c):

Balls had cleared the ground only very rarely in many years (about 6 times in roughly 30 years). The risk was so small that a reasonable club would not have taken more precautions, so there was no breach.
(b)Likely seriousness of harmParis v Stepney Borough Council (1951)A worker with sight in only one eye was not given goggles for dangerous work (welding steel, per the slides) and lost the sight of his good eye. The lecturer specifically flags Bolton v Stone as the case to analyse at breach. It "can often be distinguished" because exam facts usually involve a known, reported, likely risk with a cheap fix.

Stage 3: Causation (s 11 CLA), "critical"

Creating a risk is not enough. The injury must actually happen, it must flow from the breach, and it must not be remote. Recognised injury types: physical injury, nervous shock/mental/psychiatric injury, economic loss, property damage.

Two tests (s 11(1)), and you must apply BOTH to EACH head of damage:

  1. Factual causation, s 11(1)(a). Was the breach a necessary condition of the harm? Use the "but for" test: but for D's carelessness, would P still have been harmed? If not, D caused the harm (directly or indirectly). This is a question of fact.
  2. Scope of liability, s 11(1)(b) (also called legal causation or "remoteness"). Is it appropriate for D's liability to extend to the harm? The type of harm must have been a reasonably foreseeable consequence. Far-fetched or remote harm is not covered. This is a question of law. Case: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961] (the "Wagon Mound": oil spilt into Sydney Harbour later caught fire and burned a wharf; damage by fire was not a foreseeable type of harm, so D was not liable).

Multiple causes: the material contribution test

"But for" is not appropriate where the harm has several causes. The court instead asks whether D's breach materially contributed to the harm: did it increase the risk of injury, and did that risk eventuate, whether or not other factors also contributed? D's breach does not have to be the sole cause.

  • Chappel v Hart (1998), McHugh J: D is causally liable only where the conduct increased the risk of injury. If it didn't, D did not materially contribute.
  • Cook v ACT Racing Club Inc and the AJC Inc [2001]: an overheated racetrack sauna severely dehydrated a jockey, who then had a heart attack. Dehydration was only one cause, but the sauna significantly increased the risk, so causation was established.

Breaks in the chain and the drug-addiction cases (case analysis is expected)

  • Yates v Jones [1990]: Yates was injured in a car accident caused by Jones. In hospital, a friend suggested she take heroin for the pain, and she became addicted. Held: the addiction was not caused by the accident. It was caused by the friend's actions, which were a break in the chain of causation.
  • Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016): P was hospitalised after a motorcycle accident, and liability was admitted. P resigned from work because of mental health issues and turned to illegal drugs to cope with pain, without reporting his symptoms to doctors or taking their advice. Held: taking the drugs was a deliberate, voluntary, unreasonable act that was not prescribed or recommended treatment, so there was no causation for the drug intake. Damages were awarded for medical costs and psychological treatment because they were reasonably foreseeable, "ordinary and natural consequences". Damages for the resignation were refused because there were other causes (issues at his work unrelated to the accident).

The exam twist is almost always prescribed medication → addiction → rehab. Distinguish both cases: here the drug was prescribed and taken on medical advice, not illegal or self-chosen. So rehab is an "ordinary and natural consequence" and is recoverable. If P kept taking it beyond the prescription (as in 2022 S2), it moves closer to Schneider and recovery is unlikely.

Defences

Even if all 3 elements are proven, D can reduce liability, or sometimes avoid it, through a defence. The course teaches two:

  • Contributory negligence (partial). If P contributed to their own harm, liability is apportioned and damages are reduced by P's share. Ingram v Britten [1994]: P drove a tractor on a farm at speed, but D had not secured a metal frame to the tractor. Manley v Alexander: P drank 12 stubbies and slept in the middle of the road, and D negligently drove over him. This is the course's intoxication case. (TW4 MCQ example: driver 70% / distracted-on-phone pedestrian 30%.)
  • Voluntary assumption of risk (complete). It is very hard to establish. P must have had full knowledge and appreciation of the precise risk that eventuated and freely and willingly accepted it. Agar v Hyde (2000): rugby players broke their necks when a scrum collapsed; they were fully aware of the risks inherent in rugby and had voluntarily assumed them. CLA provisions on obvious risk: ss 13–14 (if the risk was obvious to a reasonable person, P is presumed to have been aware of it unless P proves on the balance of probabilities that they were not); s 15 (no duty to warn of an obvious risk); ss 17–19 (no liability for harm from an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity).

Feedback: "contributory negligence was a relevant defence. Voluntary Assumption of Risk, however, was not relevant. Many students discussed [it]… Be careful… not to include irrelevant legal areas." Raise VAR only where P knew of the specific danger, or the danger was obvious.

Vicarious liability (mentioned in the lecture "for your own time"): an employer may be liable for its employees' acts or omissions if they occurred during authorised work or in the course of employment (Century v Northern Ireland (1942); Deatons v Flew (1949)). This is useful when the careless person is a staff member, e.g. "a Skyline staff member".

Remedies (TW4 slides)

The usual remedy is damages, which are compensatory, not punishment. They aim to put P in the position they would have been in had the tort not been committed, "so far as money can do so".

  • Ordinary (compensatory) damages are assessed "once and for all". P cannot come back later, so future losses must be estimated now (e.g. future loss of earning capacity, future treatment).
  • Nominal damages: a right was breached but there is no compensable loss (e.g. trespass to land; not usually relevant in negligence).
  • Aggravated damages: for humiliation or emotional distress (e.g. defamation).
  • Exemplary damages: where D deliberately and maliciously disregarded P's rights, more than compensation can be awarded. Consider these briefly where D knew of a danger and consciously chose not to act.
  • Injunctions are for ongoing torts (nuisance, trespass, defamation). They are not usually relevant to a one-off negligence injury.

Heads to list in the exam: medical/hospital expenses, rehabilitation/addiction treatment, past lost earnings (show the calculation), future loss of earning capacity (estimated once and for all), and pain and suffering. Then apply the contributory negligence % reduction.

The slides don't list a separate "pain and suffering" head or any CLA caps or thresholds. Mention pain and suffering briefly as part of ordinary compensatory damages. Don't cite caps, because none are in the course materials.

LAW: INTRO
A tort is a civil wrong that allows the injured person to sue the wrongdoer for damages. A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. To succeed, [P] must establish on the balance of probabilities that: (1) [D] owed them a duty of care; (2) [D] breached that duty by failing to meet the standard of the reasonable person; and (3) the breach caused [P]'s loss, which is within the scope of [D]'s liability. 1 · Duty of care

LAW
The onus is on [P] to establish a duty of care. In Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, a manufacturer was held to owe a duty to the ultimate consumer of a ginger beer containing a decomposed snail, even though there was no contract between them. Lord Atkin's 'neighbour principle' requires a person to 'take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour', being persons 'so closely and directly affected' by the act that they ought reasonably to be in contemplation. This led to later decisions recognising established categories of relationship in which a duty of care automatically arises.

Then pick ONE of these depending on the facts:

[Occupier] An occupier of premises owes a duty of care to all persons entering the premises to ensure that the premises are safe: Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987). (Trespasser on the land? Add: a duty may be owed even to persons on the premises without permission: Hackshaw v Shaw (1984).)
[Public authority] Public authorities, such as councils and government bodies, often control public spaces such as parks, roads and waterways and may be occupiers of them. However, it is questionable whether they automatically owe a duty of care. This depends on whether there was a hidden risk that required a warning or prevention, or whether the risk was so obvious to a reasonable person that no duty would exist. [Employer / motorist / doctor / manufacturer] It is an established category that [employers owe a duty of care to their employees / motorists to other road users / doctors to their patients / manufacturers to consumers of their products].

2 · Breach of duty

LAW
The existence of a duty does not automatically mean it was breached. Under s 9(1) CLA, a person breaches their duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if: (a) the risk was foreseeable (that is, a risk they knew or ought reasonably to have known about); (b) the risk was not insignificant; and (c) in the circumstances, a reasonable person in their position would have taken those precautions. At common law, the reasonable person is someone of normal intelligence, credited with the perception of the surrounding circumstances and the knowledge that the relevant person would normally possess.
Under s 9(2) CLA, in deciding whether a reasonable person would have taken precautions, the court considers: (a) the probability that the harm would occur if care were not taken; (b) the likely seriousness of the harm (Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951), where an employer breached its duty by failing to provide goggles to a worker who was already blind in one eye, given how serious harm would be to him); (c) the burden of taking precautions to avoid the risk; and (d) the social utility of the activity creating the risk (Watt v Hertfordshire County Council (1954), where there was no breach because the urgent rescue of a life justified transporting unsecured equipment that injured a fireman).
Case analysis: In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket ball was hit out of the ground and struck a woman standing on the road outside. Balls had very rarely cleared the ground in many years, so the probability of harm was extremely low and the burden of eliminating that risk was high. The court held that a reasonable person would not have taken further precautions, so there was no breach.
APPLICATION: walk s 9(1)(a), (b), (c), then s 9(2)(a)–(d)
s 9(1)(a) Foreseeable: The risk of [patrons falling on unstable flooring] was foreseeable because [D] actually knew of it: [two attendees reported it and a staff member inspected it at 2pm]. Even without actual knowledge, [D] ought reasonably to have known because […].
s 9(1)(b) Not insignificant: The risk was not insignificant because [the flooring visibly shifted when walked on / large crowds would cross it / internal reports called it 'significant'].
s 9(1)(c) Reasonable person: A reasonable [event organiser] in [D]'s position would have [cordoned off the area, placed physical warning signs and repaired the flooring]. Instead, [D] only [posted on Facebook]. To strengthen this argument, the court will consider the s 9(2) factors:
(a) Probability: There was a high probability of harm because [the area was near the main stage, crowded, at night, patrons were looking at the stage and drinking].
(b) Seriousness: The likely seriousness of harm was high because [a fall on shifting flooring in a crowd could cause serious injury such as back or head injuries]. As in Paris v Stepney, the more serious the potential harm, the more a reasonable person would do.
(c) Burden: The burden of taking precautions was low because [barrier tape, a sign or a staff member redirecting patrons costs little and is quick]. [D] may argue [a staff shortage / $X cost / closure] made precautions burdensome, but [the staffing level was within D's control and simpler measures were available].
(d) Social utility: [D]'s activity has [some entertainment/commercial] value, but unlike Watt v Hertfordshire it was not an emergency involving the saving of life, so it does not justify leaving a known hazard in place. (Or, if irrelevant: "The social utility of D's activity is not relevant here.")
Compare Bolton v Stone: This case is distinguishable from Bolton v Stone. There, the risk was remote because balls rarely left the ground and the burden of eliminating it was high. Here, the risk was [known, reported and likely to eventuate] and could have been avoided at [little cost]. A reasonable person would therefore have taken precautions.
For D (balance): [D] may argue it took reasonable steps by [posting a warning]. However, [a Facebook post is unlikely to reach patrons who are already at the venue, especially at night], so it was not what a reasonable person would have done.
CONCLUSION (mini)
Therefore, [D] breached its duty of care to [P] under s 9 CLA.

3 · Causation (apply to EACH head of damage)

LAW
Merely creating a risk is not actionable. The harm must actually occur, must flow from the breach and must not be too remote. Under s 11(1) CLA, a decision that a breach caused harm has two elements: (a) factual causation, meaning the breach was a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm; and (b) scope of liability, meaning it is appropriate for the scope of the liability of the person in breach to extend to the harm so caused.
Factual causation is tested by asking whether, 'but for' [D]'s carelessness, [P] would still have suffered the harm. In Yates v Jones [1990], Yates was injured in a car accident caused by Jones and, while in hospital, a friend suggested she take heroin for the pain. She became addicted, but the addiction was held not to have been caused by the accident. It was caused by the friend's actions, which broke the chain of causation.
[Use if there are 2+ causes] The 'but for' test is not appropriate where harm has several causes. The court will then apply the 'material contribution' test: whether the defendant's conduct increased the risk of injury and that risk eventuated, whether or not other factors also contributed. The breach need not be the sole cause: Chappel v Hart (1998) (McHugh J). In Cook v ACT Racing Club Inc [2001], an overheated sauna dehydrated a jockey who then suffered a heart attack. Although dehydration was only one cause, the sauna significantly increased the risk of injury and causation was established.
Under the scope of liability test, the defendant is not liable for every consequence of their carelessness. The type of harm suffered must have been a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the breach and not too remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]. In Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016), the plaintiff was injured in a motorcycle accident and turned to illegal drugs to cope with his pain without following medical advice. The court held this was a deliberate, voluntary and unreasonable act that was not part of prescribed or recommended treatment, so causation was not established for the drug intake. However, medical and psychological treatment costs were recoverable as the 'ordinary and natural consequences' of the accident. Lost income from his resignation was not recoverable because there were other causes unrelated to the accident.
APPLICATION: one heading per head of damage
Headings to use: (a) The [back injury], medical expenses and lost income · (b) The addiction and rehabilitation costs · (c) [any odd/remote loss, e.g. course fees, resignation, extra surgery]. Under each heading: factual causation → scope of liability → mini-conclusion.
(a) Factual causation: But for [D]'s failure to [repair/warn/cordon off], [P] would not have [stepped onto the unstable section and fallen]. The breach was therefore a necessary condition of the [injury], and the medical expenses and lost income flow directly from it.
(a) Multiple causes: [D] may argue the injury was also caused by [the noise from SoundCo's equipment], so 'but for' is not appropriate. However, applying the material contribution test, [D]'s breach increased the risk of injury and that risk eventuated, because [P] was already losing balance on the unstable flooring when [the noise] occurred. Similar to Cook v ACT Racing Club, where the overheated sauna was only one cause of the heart attack, the breach need not be the sole cause.
(a) Break in the chain? [D] may argue that [the third party's act] broke the chain of causation, as the friend's suggestion did in Yates v Jones. However, unlike Yates, [the third party's act was not an independent, deliberate choice made after the harm. It occurred at the same moment and combined with the existing danger].
(a) Scope of liability: A [serious back injury from falling] is exactly the type of harm that is reasonably foreseeable from [unstable flooring], and medical costs and lost income are the ordinary and natural consequences of such an injury (Schneider v Smith). Unlike the fire damage in Overseas Tankships, the harm is not remote. Both tests are satisfied.
(b) Addiction/rehab, factual: But for the injury, [P] would not have been prescribed [the medication] and would not have become addicted, so the breach is a necessary condition (indirectly) of the rehabilitation cost.
(b) Addiction/rehab, scope: This case is distinguishable from Yates v Jones, where the addiction resulted from heroin suggested by a friend, and from Schneider v Smith, where the plaintiff deliberately took illegal drugs outside medical advice. Here, [P] took [prescribed medication in accordance with medical advice], so there was no deliberate or unreasonable voluntary act breaking the chain. As in Schneider, where medical and psychological treatment were 'ordinary and natural consequences', the rehabilitation cost is a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the injury and is within [D]'s scope of liability. [D] may argue addiction is too remote, but this argument is weak where the drug was prescribed.
(c) Remote/other loss: It is unclear whether [D]'s breach caused [P] to [fail the exam / resign / re-injure], because [P] may have [failed anyway / had other reasons / chose to act against medical advice]. Even if the 'but for' test is satisfied, the loss is probably too remote to be within the scope of liability. Similar to the resignation in Schneider, there were other causes external to the incident.

Whether Oliver can recover damages from Skyline Events ('Skyline') depends on whether he can establish each element of the tort of negligence (duty, breach and causation) and whether any defences reduce Skyline's liability.

Issue

Can Oliver bring an action against Skyline for the tort of negligence?

Law: Negligence generally

A tort is a civil wrong that allows an injured person to sue the wrongdoer for damages. A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. To succeed, Oliver must establish on the balance of probabilities that: (1) Skyline owed him a duty of care; (2) Skyline breached that duty; and (3) the breach caused his loss, which falls within the scope of Skyline's liability. Defences and remedies must then be considered. The common law applies together with the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA').

1. Duty of care

Law

In Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, a manufacturer was held to owe a duty of care to the ultimate consumer of a ginger beer containing a decomposed snail. Lord Atkin's 'neighbour principle' requires reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that one can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure one's 'neighbour', being persons so closely and directly affected that they ought reasonably to be in contemplation. Later cases recognised established categories of duty. Relevantly, an occupier of premises owes a duty of care to all persons entering the premises to ensure that the premises are safe: Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987).

Application

Skyline organised the festival and controlled the site, including the temporary flooring near the main stage. It was therefore the occupier. Oliver entered the site as a festival patron (it is assumed he held a valid ticket). The relationship falls within the established occupier–entrant category in Zaluzna. It was also reasonably foreseeable that carelessness about the safety of the flooring could injure a patron walking near the main stage, so Oliver was Skyline's 'neighbour'. Skyline is a private event organiser, not a public authority, so the hidden-risk analysis applicable to public authorities is not required.

Conclusion

Skyline owed Oliver a duty of care.

2. Breach of duty

Law

A duty of care is not automatically breached. Under s 9(1) CLA, a person breaches their duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if (a) the risk was foreseeable, being one they knew or ought reasonably to have known about; (b) the risk was not insignificant; and (c) in the circumstances a reasonable person in their position would have taken those precautions. Under s 9(2), the court considers (a) the probability of harm, (b) the likely seriousness of the harm, (c) the burden of taking precautions and (d) the social utility of the activity. In Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951) an employer breached its duty by failing to give goggles to a worker blind in one eye, because harm to him would be so serious. In Watt v Hertfordshire County Council (1954) the social utility of rushing to save a life justified the risk of carrying unsecured equipment, so there was no breach.

In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket ball was hit out of the ground and struck a woman on the road. Because balls had very rarely left the ground over many years, the probability of harm was very low and the burden of eliminating the risk was high, so a reasonable person would not have taken further precautions and there was no breach.

Application

Foreseeability (s 9(1)(a)). Skyline actually knew of the risk. By about 2pm, two attendees had reported the unstable flooring and a Skyline staff member inspected it and acknowledged the danger by posting a warning about 'uneven flooring'. The risk was therefore foreseeable.

Not insignificant (s 9(1)(b)). The flooring shifted when walked on and was located near the main stage, the busiest part of a large festival, through which many patrons would pass. The risk was not insignificant.

Reasonable person (s 9(1)(c) and s 9(2)). A reasonable festival organiser would have cordoned off the area, placed physical warning signs and repaired or replaced the section. The s 9(2) factors support this:

  • Probability (a): The probability of harm was high. Large crowds would walk across the area for the rest of the day and into the evening, often in poor light, looking at the stage rather than the ground, and in many cases after drinking alcohol.
  • Seriousness (b): A fall on shifting flooring in a crowd can cause serious injuries such as back and head injuries, as in fact occurred. As in Paris v Stepney, the seriousness of the potential harm demanded more than minimal precautions.
  • Burden (c): The burden was low. Barrier tape, a physical sign or rerouting patrons would have been quick and inexpensive. Skyline may argue that a staff shortage made repairs impossible at the time. However, staffing levels were within Skyline's own control, and even if repairs had to wait, cordoning the area or placing a sign required minimal resources.
  • Social utility (d): A music festival has entertainment and commercial value, but unlike the emergency rescue in Watt v Hertfordshire, nothing urgent justified leaving a known hazard in place.

Skyline will argue that it took reasonable precautions by posting a warning on its official Facebook page. However, patrons already on site, watching performances in the evening, are unlikely to check the festival's Facebook page. The warning also did not identify the exact location and left the hazard in place, so it was not what a reasonable person would have done.

This case is distinguishable from Bolton v Stone. There, the harm was an extremely rare event that the defendant could not practically prevent. Here, the danger was specific, reported, inspected and likely to cause injury, and could have been avoided at little cost.

Margin note: the "Similar to / Unlike Bolton v Stone" sentence is the case analysis the feedback says HD answers do. Always compare the probability AND the burden.

Conclusion

Skyline breached its duty of care to Oliver under s 9 CLA.

3. Causation

Law

Under s 11(1) CLA, a breach causes harm if (a) the breach was a necessary condition of the harm (factual causation) and (b) it is appropriate for the scope of the defendant's liability to extend to the harm (scope of liability). Factual causation is usually tested with the 'but for' test. However, where harm has several causes, the court applies the 'material contribution' test: whether the defendant's conduct increased the risk of injury and that risk eventuated. The breach need not be the sole cause: Chappel v Hart (1998). In Cook v ACT Racing Club Inc [2001], an overheated sauna that dehydrated a jockey was held to have caused his heart attack even though dehydration was only one cause, because it significantly increased the risk of injury. An intervening act may break the chain of causation. In Yates v Jones [1990], a plaintiff injured in a car accident became addicted to heroin suggested by a friend while in hospital, and the addiction was held to have been caused by the friend, not the accident.

Under the scope of liability test, the type of harm must be a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the breach and not too remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]. In Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016), a plaintiff injured in a motorcycle accident deliberately turned to illegal drugs outside medical advice. This was held to be a voluntary, unreasonable act not forming part of prescribed treatment, so causation failed for the drug use. However, medical and psychological treatment costs were recoverable as the 'ordinary and natural consequences' of the accident.

Application

(a) Back injury, medical expenses ($36,000) and lost income.

Factual causation: But for Skyline's failure to repair, cordon off or signpost the flooring, Oliver would not have stepped onto the unstable section, lost his balance and begun to fall. However, the medical evidence shows that his injuries were caused by a combination of the unstable flooring and his sudden reaction to the noise from SoundCo's equipment. Because there are multiple causes, the material contribution test is more appropriate. Skyline's breach plainly increased the risk of injury, and that risk eventuated: Oliver was already unbalanced and falling because of the flooring when the noise occurred. Similar to Cook v ACT Racing Club, where the overheated sauna was only one cause of the heart attack, the flooring need not be the sole cause for Skyline to be liable.

Skyline may argue that SoundCo's malfunction was an intervening act that broke the chain of causation, as the friend's suggestion did in Yates v Jones. This argument is weak. Unlike Yates, where a third party's independent and later choice caused a separate harm (the addiction), here the noise occurred 'at that moment' while Oliver was already falling, and combined with the existing danger to produce a single injury. Further, a loud noise from speakers at a music festival is not unforeseeable, and SoundCo was engaged by Skyline itself. (Oliver may also have a separate claim against SoundCo for its malfunctioning equipment, but this advice is confined to Skyline.)

Scope of liability: A serious back injury is exactly the type of harm that is reasonably foreseeable from a patron falling on unstable flooring. Hospitalisation, medical expenses and loss of income while unable to work are the ordinary and natural consequences of that injury, as recognised in Schneider. Unlike the unforeseeable fire damage in Overseas Tankships, this harm is not remote.

(b) Addiction and rehabilitation program ($5,000).

Factual causation: But for the back injury, Oliver would not have been hospitalised or prescribed strong pain medication, and would not have become addicted. The breach is therefore an indirect but necessary condition of the rehabilitation cost.

Scope of liability: Skyline will rely on Yates v Jones and Schneider v Smith to argue the addiction is too remote. Both cases are distinguishable. In Yates, the addiction was to heroin suggested by a friend, which was an independent act of a third party. In Schneider, the plaintiff deliberately took illegal drugs without reporting his symptoms or following medical advice. By contrast, Oliver took medication that was prescribed to him in hospital and took it 'in accordance with medical advice'. There was no deliberate, voluntary or unreasonable act on his part to break the chain of causation. Consistent with Schneider, where treatment costs were recoverable as ordinary and natural consequences, dependence on prescribed pain relief after a serious injury and the cost of treating it are reasonably foreseeable consequences of Skyline's breach.

Margin note: applying BOTH tests separately to EACH head of damage under headings is what separated HD from Credit in the Tut Sub 2 feedback.

Conclusion

Skyline's breach caused Oliver's back injury, medical expenses, lost income and rehabilitation costs, all of which fall within the scope of its liability.

4. Defences

Law

If a plaintiff contributed in some way to their own injury, liability is apportioned between the parties and damages reduced (contributory negligence): Ingram v Britten [1994], where the plaintiff drove a tractor at speed but the defendant had failed to secure its metal frame; Manley v Alexander, where the plaintiff drank 12 stubbies and slept on the road before being negligently driven over. Voluntary assumption of risk is a complete defence but is very difficult to establish. The plaintiff must have had full knowledge and appreciation of the precise risk and freely accepted it: Agar v Hyde (2000). Under ss 13–15 CLA, a plaintiff is presumed aware of an obvious risk, and there is no duty to warn of one.

Application

Contributory negligence: Skyline will argue that Oliver contributed to his injury. He had consumed several alcoholic drinks and was looking towards the stage rather than at the ground in front of him. Similar to Manley v Alexander, where the plaintiff's intoxication contributed to his injury, Oliver's drinking likely affected his balance and awareness. Like the plaintiff in Ingram v Britten, he failed to take reasonable care for his own safety. On the other hand, the flooring shifted unexpectedly, and patrons at a music festival can be expected to look at the stage. Skyline's failure to address a known hazard therefore remains the primary cause. If Oliver had read the Facebook warning, his responsibility would be greater, but the facts do not say this and it is assumed he had not. On balance, a reduction of around 25% is likely.

Voluntary assumption of risk: This defence is unlikely to succeed. Unlike the rugby players in Agar v Hyde, who knew the inherent risks of their sport, there is no evidence Oliver knew the flooring was unstable. The only warning was online. The instability was not an obvious risk under ss 13–15 CLA, because the flooring only shifted when stepped on and walking across a festival floor is not an obviously risky activity.

Conclusion

Oliver's damages are likely to be reduced for contributory negligence, but Skyline has no complete defence.

5. Remedies

Law

The typical remedy in tort is compensatory damages. These aim to restore the plaintiff, so far as money can, to the position he would have been in had the tort not occurred, and they are assessed once and for all, so future losses must be estimated.

Application

Oliver may claim ordinary damages for:

  • medical expenses: $36,000;
  • rehabilitation program: $5,000;
  • lost income: six months of a $95,000 annual salary = $95,000 ÷ 12 × 6 = $47,500.

These total $88,500. Oliver may also claim an amount for pain and suffering from a serious back injury and extended hospitalisation and, because damages are assessed once and for all, any future medical costs or loss of earning capacity supported by medical evidence (the facts do not indicate any, so this is noted as an assumption). Applying a 25% reduction for contributory negligence, Oliver would recover 75% of $88,500 = $66,375, plus 75% of any general damages awarded.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Oliver can bring an action against Skyline in the tort of negligence and is likely to recover damages for his medical expenses, lost income and rehabilitation costs (approximately $66,375 after a reduction for contributory negligence).

Whether Liam can recover damages from the Gladstone Recreation Authority ('GRA') depends on whether GRA, as a public authority, owed him a duty of care, whether it breached that duty, whether the breach caused each of his losses, and whether any defences apply.

Issue

Can Liam bring an action against GRA for the tort of negligence?

Law: Negligence generally

A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. Liam must establish on the balance of probabilities that GRA (1) owed him a duty of care, (2) breached that duty, and (3) caused his loss through that breach, with the loss falling within the scope of GRA's liability. Defences and remedies must then be considered under the common law and the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA').

1. Duty of care

Law

Under the neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, a person must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that could reasonably foreseeably injure persons closely and directly affected by them. An occupier owes a duty to persons entering its premises to ensure they are safe: Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987). Public authorities often control public spaces such as waterways and may be occupiers. However, it is questionable whether they automatically owe a duty of care. This depends on whether there is a hidden risk requiring a warning or prevention, or whether the risk is so obvious to a reasonable person that no duty exists. In Nagle v Rottnest Island Authority (1993), a submerged rock in an area signposted as a 'Swimming area' was a hidden risk, and because the sign encouraged swimming there a duty to warn was owed. In Swain v Waverley (2005), a submerged sandbar between the flags at a patrolled beach was a hidden risk giving rise to a duty to warn. By contrast, in Romeo v Conservation Commission (1998) (an unfenced cliff top) and Vairy v Wyong Shire Council (2005) (diving from a natural rock platform at an unpatrolled beach) the risks were obvious and no liability arose.

Application

GRA is a public authority that maintains and controls the artificial lake at the Oceanside Leisure Centre, so it is the occupier of the lake. Similar to Nagle, GRA marked out a 'designated swimming zone' and was running a festival that relied on the lake, which actively encouraged people to swim there. Like the submerged rock in Nagle and the sandbar in Swain, the exposed pipe was underwater and invisible, and the strong current it created was a hidden risk that Liam was 'unaware' of.

GRA will argue, relying on Romeo and Vairy, that the risk of swimming into deep water away from the shore is obvious, particularly to a competitive swimmer, and that signs prohibited swimming outside designated areas. However, those cases concerned natural features, such as cliffs and rock platforms, whose dangers anyone could see. Here, the lake is artificial, the hazard was created by GRA's own drainage infrastructure, and the danger that materialised was not deep water itself but a concealed current. A reasonable person would not have known about it.

Conclusion

GRA owed Liam a duty of care to warn of, or prevent, the hidden risk created by the exposed pipe.

2. Breach of duty

Law

Under s 9(1) CLA, a person breaches their duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if (a) the risk was foreseeable, being one they knew or ought reasonably to have known about, (b) it was not insignificant, and (c) a reasonable person in their position would have taken those precautions. Under s 9(2), the court considers the probability of harm, the likely seriousness of the harm (Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951)), the burden of taking precautions, and the social utility of the activity (Watt v Hertfordshire County Council (1954), where the urgency of saving a life justified the risk taken). In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket club was not in breach when a ball struck a person outside the ground, because such hits were extremely rare and the burden of eliminating that risk was high.

Application

Foreseeability (s 9(1)(a)). GRA actually knew of the risk. Its own staff discovered the exposed pipe during routine maintenance, and internal reports identified the danger.

Not insignificant (s 9(1)(b)). GRA's own reports described the current as 'posing a significant risk to swimmers', so the risk was clearly not insignificant.

Reasonable person (s 9(1)(c) and s 9(2)). A reasonable authority would have either closed the lake or posted specific warning signs, which are exactly the precautions its internal reports recommended.

  • Probability (a): The probability of harm was high. A festival would bring large numbers of swimmers into the lake, and it was foreseeable that some, especially strong swimmers, would move into the deeper water where the pipe was located.
  • Seriousness (b): Being pulled under by a strong current can cause drowning, death or, as here, severe lung damage. As in Paris v Stepney, where the seriousness of the consequences required precautions, the gravity of the potential harm here was extreme.
  • Burden (c): GRA will argue that closing the lake during the festival would have been costly and disruptive. However, the alternative precaution, warning signs about the current or roping off the deeper section, was cheap and quick and would not have stopped the festival.
  • Social utility (d): The festival had community and recreational value. However, unlike the emergency rescue in Watt v Hertfordshire, it was not urgent, and its commercial and recreational purposes do not justify exposing swimmers to a known and significant danger.

GRA may point to the general signs prohibiting swimming outside designated areas and the staff warnings not to swim too far out. However, these were generic and did not tell swimmers about the specific danger of the current. This case is clearly distinguishable from Bolton v Stone. There, the risk was remote and costly to eliminate. Here, the risk was known, documented as significant and likely to eventuate during a festival, and simple precautions were recommended but deliberately postponed.

Conclusion

GRA breached its duty of care to Liam under s 9 CLA.

3. Causation

Law

Under s 11(1) CLA, Liam must show (a) factual causation, meaning the breach was a necessary condition of the harm, tested by asking whether 'but for' the breach the harm would have occurred, and (b) that it is appropriate for GRA's scope of liability to extend to the harm, meaning the type of harm was reasonably foreseeable and not too remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]. In Yates v Jones [1990], an addiction to heroin suggested by a friend while the plaintiff was in hospital after a car accident was held to have been caused by the friend, not the accident. In Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016), the plaintiff's deliberate use of illegal drugs, outside medical advice, to cope with pain was not caused by the accident. However, medical and psychological treatment costs were recoverable as 'ordinary and natural consequences', and losses from his resignation were not recoverable because there were other causes unrelated to the accident.

Application

(a) Lung damage and hospital fees ($189,000). Factual causation: But for GRA's failure to close the lake or warn of the current, Liam would not have been pulled under. GRA will argue that Liam ignored staff warnings and signs, so he would have ignored any further warning as well. However, the warnings he received were general ('don't swim too far'). A specific warning of a dangerous current from an exposed pipe, or closure of the lake, would likely have prevented a competitive swimmer from entering that area. Closure would certainly have done so. Scope of liability: Severe lung damage from water inhalation is precisely the type of harm that is foreseeable from a strong underwater current, and the hospital fees are its ordinary and natural consequence. Both tests are satisfied.

(b) Lost earnings and chronic respiratory issues. Factual causation: But for the incident, Liam would not have developed chronic respiratory issues and would have continued to earn around $60,000 a year from swimming. Scope of liability: Loss of income while recovering, and reduced capacity to compete, are foreseeable consequences of serious lung damage to an athlete. GRA may argue, relying on Schneider, where lost income from the plaintiff's resignation was refused because of other causes, that Liam's inability to find 'suitable work' for 12 months reflects other factors, such as the job market or his own choices. No such external causes appear on the facts. It is assumed the 12 months of lost income resulted from his injury, so this loss is likely within scope.

(c) Addiction and rehabilitation ($8,000). Factual causation: But for the injury, Liam would not have been given the prescription medication in hospital or become addicted. Scope of liability: This case is distinguishable from Yates v Jones, where the addictive drug (heroin) was suggested by a friend, and from Schneider, where the plaintiff deliberately chose illegal drugs outside medical advice. Here, the medication was prescribed to Liam in hospital as part of his treatment. Assuming he took it as directed (the facts do not suggest otherwise), there was no voluntary, unreasonable act breaking the chain. Consistent with Schneider, the cost of treating a consequence of medical treatment is an ordinary and natural consequence of the injury and is within GRA's scope of liability.

Conclusion

GRA's breach caused Liam's hospital fees, lost earnings and rehabilitation costs, all of which are within the scope of GRA's liability.

4. Defences

Law

Where the plaintiff contributed to their own injury, liability is apportioned and damages reduced: Ingram v Britten [1994]; Manley v Alexander. Voluntary assumption of risk is a complete defence but is very difficult to establish. The plaintiff must have had full knowledge and appreciation of the precise risk that eventuated and freely accepted it: Agar v Hyde (2000). Under ss 13–14 CLA, a plaintiff is presumed aware of an obvious risk unless they prove otherwise; under s 15 there is no duty to warn of an obvious risk; and under ss 17–19 there is no liability for harm from an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity.

Application

Contributory negligence: This is GRA's strongest argument. Liam ignored signs prohibiting swimming outside designated areas and ignored direct warnings from GRA staff not to swim far from the shore, and he deliberately swam into deeper water. Similar to the plaintiff in Ingram v Britten, whose own careless conduct contributed to his injury despite the defendant's failure, Liam failed to take reasonable care for his own safety. As a competitive swimmer, he should have appreciated the importance of following safety instructions. Against this, he did start in a designated zone and could not have known of the hidden current. A significant reduction, perhaps around 30%, is likely.

Voluntary assumption of risk / obvious risk: GRA may argue that Liam, an experienced swimmer who ignored warnings, voluntarily assumed the risk of deep-water swimming, or that this was an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity under ss 17–19 CLA. However, unlike the rugby players in Agar v Hyde, who knew the precise risks inherent in a scrum, Liam was 'unaware of the exposed pipe'. The precise risk that eventuated, a strong current, was hidden, not obvious, so he cannot be presumed aware of it under ss 13–14. Recreational swimming in a managed lake is also unlikely to be a 'dangerous' recreational activity. This defence is likely to fail.

Conclusion

Liam's damages are likely to be reduced for contributory negligence, but GRA has no complete defence.

5. Remedies

Law

Tort damages are compensatory and aim to restore the plaintiff, so far as money can, to his position had the tort not occurred. Ordinary damages are assessed once and for all, so future losses must be estimated. Exemplary damages may be awarded where the defendant deliberately and maliciously disregarded the plaintiff's rights.

Application

Liam may claim:

  • hospital fees: $189,000;
  • lost income for 12 months after release: 12 months of $60,000 a year = $60,000 (plus income lost during his hospitalisation, which is not quantified in the facts);
  • drug rehabilitation: $8,000.

These total $257,000. Because damages are assessed once and for all, Liam should also claim an estimated amount for future loss of earning capacity, since his chronic respiratory issues affect his ability to train and compete, and an amount for pain and suffering. Applying a 30% reduction for contributory negligence, Liam would recover 70% of $257,000 = $179,900, plus 70% of any future and general damages. Liam may also argue for exemplary damages because GRA consciously chose to put the festival ahead of known safety recommendations. However, as GRA's conduct was not malicious, such an award is unlikely.

Margin note: showing the arithmetic (12 × $60k/12, then the % reduction) is cheap marks. No calculators are allowed, so choose round percentages.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Liam can bring an action against GRA in the tort of negligence and is likely to recover damages for his hospital fees, lost and future earnings and rehabilitation costs, reduced by approximately 30% for his contributory negligence.

⑤ Variants: twists likely to appear

Public authority defendant (council, "Authority", beach, park, lake)

Duty is not automatic. Go straight to hidden vs obvious risk. Hidden risk plus the authority encouraging use (signs saying "Swimming area", flags, patrols, a designated zone) → duty (Nagle, Swain). Natural feature whose danger anyone could see, unpatrolled, no invitation → no duty (Romeo, Vairy). Also link to s 15 CLA (no duty to warn of an obvious risk). Past examples: 2025 S2 (GRA lake), 2021 S2 (Caloundra council beach, sandbar between the flags, which is almost exactly Swain), practice Q (Greenbank Regional Council paving slab, where the "Uneven Surface – Take Care!" sign raises whether the warning was adequate).

Negligent misstatement / professional advice

Use where the harm comes from words (advice, a report, a reference) rather than an act. Duty test: (1) business or serious advice; (2) D knew or should have known P would rely on it; (3) reliance was reasonable, which is easier if D claims expertise (Hedley Byrne, Rentokill v Channon). Point out that a non-professional can still owe the duty, but casual or social advice is unlikely to be "serious". Disclaimer? In Hedley Byrne, a disclaimer defeated liability, so mention one if the facts include it. Third party relying? Esanda v Peat Marwick: generally no duty, unless D knew the advice would be passed on, would lead to a particular transaction, and would likely cause loss. Then still do breach (s 9) and causation (s 11), as the slides insist. The loss is usually economic loss (a recognised injury type). Exam hint: 2025 S1 Q1 (Tyler, a junior shop worker: "I've seen my mum use them… Layla made it clear she was relying on his advice") was ACL-framed, but if it asked under tort, this is the route.

Pure psychiatric / mental harm (e.g. a child who witnesses a parent's injury)

The course only lists "nervous shock / mental / psychiatric injury" as a recognised type of injury (Donoghue herself suffered nervous shock). Child plaintiff

The course materials don't teach a special standard for children. Treat a child as a vulnerable entrant: it raises s 9(2)(a) probability (children are less careful) and makes contributory negligence and obvious-risk arguments weaker against them. Don't invent a rule.

Employer / employee

Duty is an established category (employer → employees). Breach: Paris v Stepney (provide safety gear, especially where the worker is vulnerable) and Watt v Hertfordshire (emergency services, social utility). If the careless person is an employee and P sues the business: vicarious liability. Was the act part of authorised work or in the course of employment? (Century v Northern Ireland; Deatons v Flew). An independent contractor (e.g. SoundCo) is not an employee, so sue the contractor directly or rely on the occupier's own breach.

Occupier (shop, restaurant, venue, festival) incl. trespassers

Zaluzna (supermarket occupier → entrants). If P was somewhere they weren't allowed (a storeroom, after hours, climbing a fence): Hackshaw v Shaw, where a duty can still be owed to a trespasser. Tutorial 3 (Bazza's storeroom chair) is the course's model occupier problem. Duty yes; breach yes (warned 2 months earlier, never repaired); medical costs and 2 days' wages recoverable; course fees for the failed exam too remote; contributory negligence for swinging on the chair; VAR not relevant. In Class Exercise 1, if Vinny had told Bazza about the exam, the exam loss becomes more foreseeable (the scope-of-liability argument strengthens). In Class Exercise 2, if Bazza had warned that the chair was loose, the warning goes to breach (precaution taken?) and makes contributory negligence or VAR more arguable.

Second wrongdoer (surgeon error, third party, plaintiff's own later conduct)

Ask whether the later event was an independent, voluntary act that breaks the chain (Yates) or whether it is a foreseeable consequence / combined cause (material contribution: Chappel, Cook). 2022 S2: an intoxicated surgeon's critical mistakes (a strong argument that Harry is not liable for the extra damage the surgeon caused, which can be pursued against the surgeon), and medication continued for 3 months beyond the prescription (like Schneider, deliberate, against medical advice → addiction therapy not recoverable from Harry). Practice Q (Sophie): she travelled interstate against her doctor's advice, and the extra surgery and 8 weeks' lost income are arguably caused by her own choice, not GRC (cf Schneider: not following medical advice). The original 6 weeks (6 × $5,000 = $30,000) and $20,000 are recoverable.

Sport / dangerous recreational activity

This is where voluntary assumption of risk genuinely matters: Agar v Hyde (P knew the inherent risks of rugby). Use ss 13–14 (obvious risk presumption), s 15 (no duty to warn of obvious risk) and ss 17–19 (dangerous recreational activity). Always ask: was the precise risk that eventuated obvious or known?

Order: Opening line → ISSUE "Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of negligence?" → Law intro (3 elements; CLA 2003 (Qld)) → 1 Duty → 2 Breach (s 9(1)(a)(b)(c) + s 9(2)(a)–(d) + Bolton) → 3 Causation (s 11(1)(a) but-for / material contribution; s 11(1)(b) scope) PER HEAD OF DAMAGE → 4 Defences (CN; VAR only if relevant) → 5 Remedies ($ calc, − CN %) → Conclusion "On the balance of probabilities…"
Law intro. A tort is a civil wrong allowing the injured person to sue for damages. Negligence = carelessly causing harm to another. P must prove on the balance of probabilities: (1) D owed a duty of care; (2) D breached it (reasonable person standard); (3) the breach caused P's loss, within the scope of D's liability. Then defences and remedies. Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA') + common law.
1 DUTY. Donoghue v Stevenson [1932]: snail in ginger beer; manufacturer owed a duty to the consumer despite no contract. Neighbour principle: take reasonable care to avoid acts/omissions you can reasonably foresee would injure persons so closely and directly affected that you ought to have them in contemplation.
Established categories: motorist→road users; doctor→patient; accountant/banker→client; manufacturer→consumer; occupier→entrants (Australian Safeway Stores v Zaluzna (1987)); trespassers (Hackshaw v Shaw (1984)); employer→employee.
Public authority: not automatic. Was there a hidden risk needing a warning/prevention, or was it obvious? Hidden → duty: Nagle v Rottnest (submerged rock, "Swimming area" sign); Swain v Waverley (sandbar between the flags, patrolled). Obvious → none: Romeo v Conservation Commission (cliff top); Vairy v Wyong (natural rock platform, unpatrolled).
No category: (1) reasonable foreseeability; (2) salient features: relationship, control, relative knowledge, experience, vulnerability and reliance, personal responsibility.
Negligent misstatement: (1) business/serious advice; (2) knew/should know P would rely; (3) reliance reasonable (easier if D claims special skill). Hedley Byrne v Heller (careless reference; saved by a disclaimer; duty in obiter); Rentokill v Channon (bad pest report, house demolished: duty + breach). 3rd party: Esanda v Peat Marwick, no duty unless D knew the advice would be passed on, would lead to a transaction, and would cause loss.
2 BREACH, s 9(1) CLA: (a) risk foreseeable (knew/ought to have known); (b) not insignificant; (c) a reasonable person in D's position would have taken precautions. Reasonable person = normal intelligence + the perception/knowledge such a person would have.
s 9(2): (a) probability: Bolton v Stone; (b) seriousness: Paris v Stepney (one-eyed worker, no goggles → breach); (c) burden: Bolton v Stone; (d) social utility: Watt v Hertfordshire (fire rescue, unsecured equipment → no breach).
Bolton v Stone (1951): cricket ball hit out of the ground struck a woman on the road; it happened very rarely in many years; low probability + high burden → no breach. Distinguish: "Unlike Bolton, the risk here was known/reported/likely and cheap to prevent."
3 CAUSATION, s 11(1) CLA (apply BOTH to EACH head of damage):
(a) Factual: breach a necessary condition: "but for" D's carelessness, would P still be harmed? Yates v Jones [1990]: car crash → friend suggested heroin in hospital → addiction caused by the friend, not the crash (break in the chain).
Several causes → material contribution: D's conduct increased the risk and the risk eventuated; need not be the sole cause. Chappel v Hart (1998) (McHugh J); Cook v ACT Racing Club [2001] (overheated sauna → dehydration → heart attack; one cause only, still liable).
(b) Scope of liability: type of harm reasonably foreseeable, not remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961] (oil → fire at a wharf: not foreseeable). Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016): motorcycle crash; illegal drugs taken without medical advice = deliberate voluntary act, not prescribed → no causation for drugs; medical + psych treatment = "ordinary and natural consequences" → recoverable; resignation had other work causes → not recoverable.
Injury types: physical, nervous shock/psychiatric, economic loss, property.
4 DEFENCES. Contributory negligence (partial, apportioned): Ingram v Britten [1994] (tractor at speed; frame unsecured); Manley v Alexander (12 stubbies, slept on the road, run over) = intoxication. Voluntary assumption of risk (complete; very hard): full knowledge + appreciation of the precise risk, freely accepted: Agar v Hyde (2000) (rugby scrum). CLA ss 13–14 obvious risk → presumed aware unless P proves otherwise; s 15 no duty to warn of obvious risk; ss 17–19 dangerous recreational activity. Only if relevant!
Vicarious liability: employer liable for employee acts in the course of authorised work/employment: Century v Northern Ireland (1942); Deatons v Flew (1949).
5 REMEDIES (TW4). Damages = compensation, not punishment; restore P, so far as money can, to the pre-tort position. Ordinary damages assessed once and for all → estimate future losses. Nominal (no loss); aggravated (humiliation/distress); exemplary (deliberate, malicious disregard). Injunction = ongoing torts only.
Heads: medical/hospital · rehab · past lost income (calc) · future earning capacity · pain and suffering → total → × (100 − CN%).
Calcs: 6 mo of $95k = $47,500 · 12 mo of $60k = $60,000 · 25% = ÷4 · 30% = 3×10%.

Law: Negligence generally

What this means:

A tort is a "civil wrong": something one person does to another that lets the victim sue for money (damages), as opposed to a crime. Negligence is the tort of carelessly hurting someone. On the balance of probabilities means "more likely than not" — that is how much P has to prove. The CLA is the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld), the Queensland statute that sets the rules for breach (s 9) and causation (s 11).

WRITE — Law

A tort is a civil wrong that allows the injured person to sue the wrongdoer for damages. A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. To succeed, [P] must establish on the balance of probabilities that: (1) [D] owed them a duty of care; (2) [D] breached that duty by failing to meet the standard of the reasonable person; and (3) the breach caused [P]'s loss, which is within the scope of [D]'s liability. Any defences and the remedies available must then be considered. The common law applies together with the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA').

1. Duty of care

What this means:

A duty of care is a legal obligation to take reasonable care not to hurt someone. An established category is a relationship the courts have ALREADY decided always carries a duty of care, so you don't have to argue it — e.g. a driver always owes a duty to other people on the road. A public authority (a council or government "Authority") is different: it only owes a duty if the danger was hidden, not if it was obvious.

WRITE — Law (always write this first)

The onus is on [P] to establish that [D] owed them a duty of care. In Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, Mrs Donoghue drank a ginger beer that her friend had bought, a decomposed snail floated out of the opaque bottle, and the manufacturer was held to owe her a duty of care even though there was no contract between them. Lord Atkin's 'neighbour principle' requires a person to 'take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour', being persons 'so closely and directly affected' by the act that they ought reasonably to be in contemplation. Later cases recognised established categories of relationship in which a duty of care automatically arises.

2. Breach of duty

What this means:

Breach means D did not take the care a sensible ("reasonable") person in D's position would have taken. A precaution is a step that stops the harm (a sign, a barrier, a repair, closing the area). Section 9(1) of the CLA has three requirements, (a), (b) and (c); s 9(2) gives four factors the court weighs to decide (c). Write a sentence for EVERY one — markers noted students "missed the factors in s 9(2)".

WRITE — Law

The existence of a duty does not automatically mean it was breached. Under s 9(1) CLA, a person breaches their duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if three requirements are met. Under s 9(1)(a), the risk must have been foreseeable, meaning a risk the person knew or ought reasonably to have known about. Under s 9(1)(b), the risk must have been not insignificant. Under s 9(1)(c), in the circumstances a reasonable person in their position would have taken those precautions. At common law, the reasonable person is someone of normal intelligence, credited with the perception of the surrounding circumstances and the knowledge that the relevant person would normally possess.
Under s 9(2) CLA, in deciding whether a reasonable person would have taken precautions, the court considers four factors. Under s 9(2)(a), it considers the probability that the harm would occur if care were not taken. Under s 9(2)(b), it considers the likely seriousness of the harm; in Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951), an employer breached its duty by failing to give goggles to a worker who was already blind in one eye, because harm to him would be so serious. Under s 9(2)(c), it considers the burden of taking precautions to avoid the risk. Under s 9(2)(d), it considers the social utility of the activity that creates the risk; in Watt v Hertfordshire County Council (1954), there was no breach because the urgent rescue of a life justified carrying unsecured equipment in a vehicle, which then injured a fireman.
In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket ball was hit out of the ground and struck a woman standing on the road outside. Balls had very rarely cleared the ground in many years, so the probability of harm was extremely low, and the burden of eliminating that risk, such as building a much higher fence or closing the ground, was high. The court held that a reasonable person would not have taken further precautions, so there was no breach.

3. Causation

What this means:

Causation asks whether D's carelessness actually caused EACH loss, and whether that loss is not too far-fetched. A head of damage is one separate loss (the medical bill, the lost wages, the rehab). WRITE — Law

Merely creating a risk is not actionable: the harm must actually occur, must flow from the breach and must not be too remote. Under s 11(1) CLA, a decision that a breach caused harm has two elements. Under s 11(1)(a), factual causation requires that the breach was a necessary condition of the occurrence of the harm. Under s 11(1)(b), scope of liability requires that it is appropriate for the scope of the liability of the person in breach to extend to the harm so caused.
Factual causation is tested by asking whether, 'but for' [D]'s carelessness, [P] would still have suffered the harm. In Yates v Jones [1990], Yates was injured in a car accident caused by Jones and, while in hospital, a friend suggested she take heroin for the pain. She became addicted, but the addiction was held not to have been caused by the accident: it was caused by the friend's actions, which broke the chain of causation.
[Only if there are 2+ causes:] The 'but for' test is not appropriate where harm has several causes. The court then applies the 'material contribution' test: whether the defendant's conduct increased the risk of injury and that risk eventuated, whether or not other factors also contributed. The breach need not be the sole cause: Chappel v Hart (1998) (McHugh J). In Cook v ACT Racing Club Inc [2001], an overheated sauna dehydrated a jockey, who then suffered a heart attack. Although dehydration was only one cause, the sauna significantly increased the risk of injury, so causation was established.
Under the scope of liability test, the type of harm suffered must have been a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the breach and not too remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961], where oil spilt into Sydney Harbour later caught fire and burned a wharf, and because fire damage was not a foreseeable type of harm the defendant was not liable. In Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016), the plaintiff was injured in a motorcycle accident and turned to illegal drugs to cope with his pain without following medical advice. This was a deliberate, voluntary and unreasonable act that was not part of prescribed or recommended treatment, so causation was not established for the drug intake. However, his medical and psychological treatment costs were recoverable as the 'ordinary and natural consequences' of the accident, and lost income from his resignation was not recoverable because there were other causes unrelated to the accident.

4. Defences

What this means:

A defence is a reason D pays less, or nothing, even though D was negligent. Contributory negligence = P was partly to blame for their own injury, so P's money is cut by a percentage. Voluntary assumption of risk = P knew the exact danger and freely accepted it, so D pays nothing; it is very hard to prove, and markers penalised students who raised it when P did not know of the danger. An obvious risk is one a reasonable person in P's position would have seen.

WRITE — Law

Even where all the elements of negligence are established, a defendant may reduce its liability by proving a defence. If the plaintiff contributed in some way to their own loss or injury, liability will be apportioned between the defendant and the plaintiff and damages reduced accordingly (contributory negligence): Ingram v Britten [1994], where the plaintiff drove a tractor at speed but the defendant had failed to secure a metal frame to it; Manley v Alexander, where the plaintiff drank 12 stubbies and slept in the middle of the road before the defendant negligently drove over him.
[Only if P knew the specific danger, or the danger was obvious:] Voluntary assumption of risk, which can relieve the defendant of all liability, is very difficult to establish. The plaintiff must have had full knowledge and appreciation of the precise risk that eventuated and freely and willingly accepted it: Agar v Hyde (2000), where rugby players injured in a collapsed scrum were held to have voluntarily assumed the risks inherent in the game. Under ss 13–14 CLA, where a risk is obvious to a reasonable person, the plaintiff is presumed to have been aware of it unless they prove on the balance of probabilities that they were not; under s 15 there is no duty to warn of an obvious risk; and under ss 17–19 a defendant is not liable for harm from an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity.

5. Remedies

What this means:

Damages = the money the court orders D to pay. Ordinary (compensatory) damages try to put P back where they would have been without the accident. They are assessed "once and for all" — P can't come back later — so future losses must be claimed now. Add up only the losses you said were caused (Causation), then cut the total by P's contributory negligence %. No calculators: pick round percentages.

WRITE — Law

The typical remedy for a tort is an award of damages, the primary purpose of which is to compensate the plaintiff, not to punish the defendant. Ordinary damages aim to restore the plaintiff, so far as money can do so, to the position they would have been in had the tort not been committed. They are assessed 'once and for all', so future losses must be estimated at the time of the award. Exemplary damages may be awarded where the defendant deliberately and maliciously disregarded the plaintiff's rights.

Full mini-answer: 2026 S1 Oliver v Skyline

Open the whole answer, stitched together (≈1,250 words)

Whether Oliver can recover damages from Skyline Events ('Skyline') depends on whether each element of the tort of negligence (duty, breach and causation) can be established, and whether any defences apply.

Issue

Can Oliver bring an action against Skyline for the tort of negligence?

Law: Negligence generally

A tort is a civil wrong that allows the injured person to sue the wrongdoer for damages. A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. Oliver must establish on the balance of probabilities that (1) Skyline owed him a duty of care; (2) Skyline breached that duty; and (3) the breach caused his loss, which is within the scope of Skyline's liability. Defences and remedies must then be considered under the common law and the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA').

1. Duty of care

Law. In Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, a manufacturer owed a duty to the consumer of a ginger beer containing a decomposed snail, despite no contract. Lord Atkin's neighbour principle requires reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that one can reasonably foresee would injure persons so closely and directly affected that they ought to be in contemplation. An occupier of premises owes a duty to all persons entering to ensure the premises are safe: Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987).

Application. Skyline organised the festival and controlled the site, including the temporary flooring, so it was the occupier. Oliver entered as a patron (it is assumed he held a ticket). Similar to the supermarket in Zaluzna, this falls within the established occupier–entrant category, and it was reasonably foreseeable that carelessness about the flooring could injure a patron. Skyline is a private organiser, not a public authority.

Conclusion. Skyline owed Oliver a duty of care.

2. Breach of duty

Law. Under s 9(1) CLA, a person breaches their duty by failing to take precautions if (a) the risk was foreseeable; (b) it was not insignificant; and (c) a reasonable person in their position would have taken those precautions. Under s 9(2), the court considers (a) the probability of harm; (b) its likely seriousness (Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951): no goggles for a one-eyed worker, breach); (c) the burden of precautions; and (d) social utility (Watt v Hertfordshire County Council (1954): rushing to save a life justified the risk, no breach). In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket ball very rarely left the ground, so the low probability and high burden meant no breach.

Application. The risk was foreseeable because Skyline actually knew of it: two attendees reported it and a staff member inspected it at 2pm. It was not insignificant because the flooring shifted when walked on near the busy main stage. A reasonable organiser would have cordoned off the area, placed physical signs and repaired it; Skyline only posted on Facebook. The probability of harm was high, as crowds would cross the area into the night, looking at the stage, many after drinking. The likely harm was serious; as in Paris v Stepney, this demanded more than minimal precautions. The burden was low: barrier tape or a sign is quick and cheap. Skyline may argue a staff shortage prevented repairs, but staffing was within its control and a sign needed minimal resources. Unlike the emergency in Watt, nothing urgent justified leaving a known hazard. Unlike Bolton v Stone, the risk was reported, likely and cheap to prevent. A Facebook post is unlikely to reach patrons already on site at night, so it was not reasonable.

Conclusion. Skyline breached its duty under s 9 CLA.

3. Causation

Law. Under s 11(1) CLA, (a) the breach must be a necessary condition of the harm (factual causation), and (b) it must be appropriate for liability to extend to the harm (scope of liability). Factual causation uses the 'but for' test; in Yates v Jones [1990], an addiction to heroin suggested by a friend was caused by the friend, not the car accident. Where there are several causes, the material contribution test asks whether the breach increased the risk and the risk eventuated: Chappel v Hart (1998); in Cook v ACT Racing Club Inc [2001], an overheated sauna was only one cause of a jockey's heart attack, but causation was established. The harm must be a reasonably foreseeable type, not remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]. In Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016), illegal drug use against medical advice was a voluntary act breaking the chain, but medical and psychological treatment costs were 'ordinary and natural consequences'.

Application. (a) Back injury, medical expenses ($36,000) and lost income. But for Skyline's failure to repair or cordon off the flooring, Oliver would not have begun to fall. The medical evidence shows the injury was caused by the flooring combined with his reaction to SoundCo's noise, so the material contribution test applies: Skyline's breach increased the risk of injury and it eventuated, because Oliver was already falling when the noise occurred. Similar to Cook, the breach need not be the sole cause. Unlike Yates, the noise was not an independent later choice; it combined with the existing danger at that moment. A serious back injury is exactly the harm foreseeable from unstable flooring, and medical costs and lost income are its ordinary and natural consequences (Schneider). Unlike Overseas Tankships, it is not remote.

(b) Addiction and rehabilitation ($5,000). But for the injury, Oliver would not have been prescribed strong pain medication or become addicted. Unlike Yates (heroin suggested by a friend) and Schneider (illegal drugs against medical advice), Oliver took the medication 'in accordance with medical advice', so no voluntary, unreasonable act broke the chain. The rehabilitation cost is a reasonably foreseeable consequence and within scope.

Conclusion. Skyline's breach caused all of Oliver's losses, which are within its scope of liability.

4. Defences

Law. If a plaintiff contributed to their own injury, liability is apportioned and damages reduced: Ingram v Britten [1994] (tractor driven at speed, frame unsecured); Manley v Alexander (12 stubbies, slept on the road). Voluntary assumption of risk requires full knowledge of the precise risk, freely accepted: Agar v Hyde (2000); under ss 13–15 CLA, a plaintiff is presumed aware of an obvious risk.

Application. Oliver had consumed several drinks and was looking at the stage, not the ground. Similar to Manley, his intoxication likely affected his balance; like the plaintiff in Ingram, he failed to take reasonable care for his own safety. However, the floor shifted unexpectedly and patrons are expected to watch the stage, so Skyline remains primarily responsible. It is assumed he had not read the Facebook post. A reduction of around 25% is likely. Unlike the rugby players in Agar v Hyde, Oliver did not know the flooring was unstable, and it was not obvious, so voluntary assumption of risk fails.

Conclusion. Oliver's damages are likely to be reduced for contributory negligence, but Skyline has no complete defence.

5. Remedies

Law. Damages compensate, not punish. Ordinary damages restore the plaintiff, so far as money can, to his position had the tort not occurred, and are assessed once and for all.

Application. Oliver may claim medical expenses of $36,000; rehabilitation of $5,000; and lost income of $95,000 ÷ 12 × 6 = $47,500. These total $88,500. He may also claim for pain and suffering and any future losses. Applying a 25% reduction, Oliver would recover 75% of $88,500 = $66,375, plus 75% of any general damages.

Conclusion. Oliver is entitled to ordinary damages of approximately $66,375 plus reduced general damages.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Oliver can bring an action against Skyline in the tort of negligence and is likely to recover damages for his medical expenses, lost income and rehabilitation costs (approximately $66,375 after a 25% reduction for contributory negligence).

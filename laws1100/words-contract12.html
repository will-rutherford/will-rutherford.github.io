1. Agreement (offer, counter-offer, acceptance)

What this means: a contract needs 3 things: agreement, intention and consideration. Agreement = an offer (a firm proposal: "you can buy it for $140,000") plus an acceptance (a clear "yes" to exactly those terms). An invitation to treat = an invitation to start negotiating, not an offer (ads, shop shelves, eBay listings, price lists). A counter-offer = a reply that changes the terms ("How about $120,000?"); it kills the first offer and becomes a new offer. Go through the facts in time order and label each statement.

Write — Issue

Was a contract formed between [A] and [B] for [the sale of the painting]?

Write — Law

A contract is a legally binding promise or agreement. A simple contract may be made verbally, in writing or by conduct, and requires three elements: (1) agreement; (2) an intention to create legal relations; and (3) consideration.
Agreement consists of an offer plus an acceptance. An offer is a proposal by one party to another to immediately enter into a legally binding contract, assessed objectively by asking whether a reasonable person in the recipient's position would believe it to be an offer. An offer must be distinguished from an invitation to treat, which merely invites others to deal; advertisements and price lists are usually invitations to treat, although an advertisement can be an offer to the world at large where it shows a willingness to be bound immediately (Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, where an advertisement promising £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using the smoke ball, backed by £1,000 deposited in the bank, was an offer). Acceptance is a final and unqualified assent to all the terms of the offer, made by words or conduct and judged objectively. If acceptance is qualified, it is a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer and replaces it with a new offer. Acceptance must generally be communicated, and silence is not acceptance (Felthouse v Bindley, where an uncle's statement "if I hear no more… I consider the horse mine" did not create a contract when the nephew said nothing).

2. Intention to create legal relations

What this means: the parties must have meant the deal to be legally binding (enforceable in court), not just a friendly arrangement. In the past courts assumed business deals were binding and family/social deals were not. Now there are no presumptions: you look at all the circumstances (how much money, the words used, the setting, the relationship). Onus = the person saying there is a contract has to prove it. Family members CAN have a binding contract if the deal is serious.

Write — Law

The parties must intend to create legal relations, and the onus is on the party alleging the contract. Traditionally, courts presumed that commercial agreements were intended to be binding and that social or domestic agreements were not, though either presumption could be rebutted (Wakeling v Ripley). In that case, Ripley persuaded his sister and brother-in-law to give up their life in England and move to Sydney to care for him, promising they could live in his house and inherit it. Despite the family relationship, the court found an intention to be bound because of the seriousness of the promises and the sacrifices made in reliance on them. The current approach is to consider the agreement on its own merits, without presumptions, by assessing objectively all the circumstances: the parties' conduct, the context, the language used, the value of any consideration and the nature of the relationship (Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community of SA).

3. Consideration (including past consideration)

What this means: consideration = what each side gives in exchange for the other's promise (money, goods, a service, or a promise to do one of these). It doesn't have to be a fair price (need not be adequate), just something of value. But some things don't count (not sufficient): a vague promise, doing what you already had to do, and past consideration = something already given BEFORE the promise was made. If someone makes an extra promise after the deal is done ("I'll re-frame it for you") and gets nothing new for it, that promise can't be enforced. Treat each promise as a separate issue.

Write — Issue (for a later promise, write it as a separate issue)

Is [B]'s later promise to [re-frame the painting] enforceable by [A]?

Write — Law

For an agreement to be binding, the promisee must provide consideration for the promise received: the price paid, or promised, for the other party's promise. It may be money, goods, services or a promise to provide them (Carlill, where using the smoke ball as directed was consideration). It need not be adequate, only of some legal value (Thomas v Thomas, where £1 a year rent was enough). However, consideration must be sufficient, and past consideration is not good consideration: an act or payment completed before the promise is made cannot be the price of that promise (Roscorla v Thomas). In that case, after a horse had been sold, the seller promised it was sound and free from vice. When it proved vicious, the buyer could not enforce the promise because the only consideration, the purchase price, had been given before the promise was made.

4. Promissory estoppel

What this means: promissory estoppel = even if there is no contract (or no consideration), a court can stop someone going back on a clear promise if the other person relied on it, changed their position, and would be badly hurt. Estopped = not allowed to deny. Detriment = real harm or loss. The lecturer warned this is narrow and often misused: only use it if there is a clear promise, real reliance and real loss, AND no contract/consideration. If the question says "Contract Law 1 only", don't use it.

Write — Issue

Is [Promisor] estopped from going back on its promise to [Promisee] that […]?

Write — Law

A promise is normally enforceable only if it is contained in a contract supported by consideration. However, equity developed promissory estoppel so that a person who, by words or conduct, has led another to believe in a particular state of affairs will not be allowed to go back on it where it would be unjust or inequitable to do so (Moorgate v Twitchings). In Waltons Stores v Maher, Waltons led the Mahers to believe a lease would be completed; the Mahers demolished their building and began building a new one to Waltons' plans while Waltons secretly told its solicitors to "go slow", then withdrew. The High Court held Waltons was estopped from denying a binding agreement. The elements are: (1) a legal relationship exists or is anticipated; (2) a clear and unambiguous promise; (3) reliance by the promisee through a change of circumstances; (4) material detriment if the promise is not kept; and (5) it would be unconscionable for the promisor not to keep it. The court may order specific performance or damages (Giumelli v Giumelli).

5. Term or representation?

What this means: a term = a promise that is part of the contract, so if it is broken you can sue for breach of contract. A representation = something said during the deal that is NOT part of the contract, so you can't sue for breach of contract on it. To decide, the court asks what a reasonable person would think the parties meant, using 5 factors: language, context, timing, who said it (their expertise), and importance (did the other side rely on it?). Write one sentence per factor using the facts.

Write — Issue

Is [A]'s assurance that [the software could handle at least 10,000 concurrent users] an enforceable term of the contract between [Claimant] and [Defendant]?

Write — Law

Contract Law 2 is concerned with identifying the content of the bargain. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect, so its breach is a breach of contract. A representation is a statement made in connection with the contract, which may be true or false, but which does not form part of it and has no promissory effect. To distinguish them, the court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended about the contractual nature of the statement, considering: (1) the language used, since a strong statement such as "I assure you" or "I guarantee" is more likely to be a term; (2) the context in which it was made; (3) the timing, since a statement made immediately before the contract is more likely to be a term; (4) the maker of the statement and their relative knowledge and expertise; and (5) the importance of the statement and whether the other party obviously relied on it. A precise, measurable claim, such as "this machine can process 500 units per hour", is more likely to be a term, while an opinion such as "this machine should be perfect for your business" is a representation.

6. Parol evidence rule and collateral contract

What this means: parol evidence rule ("parol" = spoken) = if the contract is written down and looks complete, you normally can't add things that were said out loud before signing. There are 2 ways around it: (1) the contract was not meant to be wholly in writing (it is partly written, partly spoken), or (2) a collateral contract = a separate mini-contract: "I promise X, if you sign the main contract". Signing the main contract is what you "pay" for that promise. Breaking a collateral contract gets you damages only (you can't cancel the main contract).

Write — Issue

Does the parol evidence rule prevent [Claimant] from relying on [A]'s oral assurance, and if so, can [Claimant] enforce it as a collateral contract?

Write — Law

Under the parol evidence rule, where a contract is reduced to writing and appears to be entire, it is presumed that the writing contains all the terms, and evidence of any previous or contemporaneous oral agreement will not be admitted to add to or vary it (Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor). The rule applies only where the written contract appears to be entire. There are two main exceptions. First, where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, oral evidence may establish further oral terms. Second, the oral statement may form a collateral contract.
A collateral contract requires: (1) an oral statement and a main written contract; (2) that the statement was intended as a promise, made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) that it is not inconsistent with the main written contract; and (4) that entry into the main contract is the consideration for the promise (De Lassalle v Guildford). In that case, before the tenant handed over the signed lease, the landlord orally assured him that the drains were in good order. The lease said nothing about drains, which proved defective, and the assurance was enforced as a collateral contract. Because it is the collateral contract that is breached, the innocent party cannot terminate the main contract but is entitled to damages.

8. Express written term: condition or warranty?

What this means: an express term = a term actually stated (written or spoken). If you signed it, you're bound by it even if you didn't read it. A warranty = a minor term; if broken, damages (money) only. Test: would the client have signed without this promise? If not, it's a condition. (Implied term = a term the court reads in even though nobody said it — only if the contract is silent on something obvious.)

Write — Issue

Write — Law

Express terms are those explicitly agreed by the parties, orally or in writing, and a party who signs a written contract is bound by its terms whether or not they have read them (L'Estrange v Graucob). Once a term is identified, the court determines its importance. A condition is fundamental to the contract, and its breach entitles the innocent party to terminate and claim damages (Poussard v Spiers, where a lead singer's absence from the opening performances allowed the producers to terminate). 9. Disclaimer Issue 1: is it a term?

What this means: a disclaimer (also called an exclusion clause) = a term saying "we're not responsible if X goes wrong". It only protects the business if it is actually part of the contract. There are 3 ways it gets in: (a) it was in a document the customer signed; (b) the customer was given reasonable notice of it BEFORE the contract was made (a sign seen after paying is too late); or (c) prior dealings = the customer had done the same deal many times before and should have known about it. First work out exactly WHEN and WHERE the contract was made.

Write — Issue

Is the disclaimer a term of the contract between [Customer] and [Business]?

Write — Law

A disclaimer is a term of a contract stating that one of the parties will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of their contractual obligations. Disclaimers are also known as exclusion, exemption or limitation of liability clauses. A disclaimer will be a term of the contract if it is: (a) incorporated into a written contract signed by the parties; (b) brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract is formed; or (c) implied into the contract as a result of prior dealings between the parties.
Signed documents. A party who signs a written contract is bound by its terms whether or not they have read them (L'Estrange v Graucob, where Miss L'Estrange was bound by an exclusion clause in small print in an order form for a vending machine that she signed without reading).
Reasonable notice. With an unsigned document, a disclaimer is binding only if it was brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was entered into (Olley v Marlborough Court, where a disclaimer on a notice in a hotel bedroom was not a term because the contract had already been formed at the reception desk). Similarly, in Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking, conditions referred to on a car-park ticket came too late, because the contract was formed at the ticket machine. The court asks whether the document is one a reasonable person would expect to contain contractual terms, and whether the business did what was reasonable to bring the terms to the customer's notice.
Prior dealings. If the parties have made similar contracts in the past containing the disclaimer, it can be implied into the present contract (Balmain New Ferry Co v Robertson). In that case the notice was only visible after the customer paid for the ferry, but it was still a term because Robertson had travelled on the ferry on many previous occasions.

10. Disclaimer Issue 2: does it cover this breach?

What this means: even if the disclaimer is a term, the court reads it carefully. Contra proferentem (Latin, "against the one who put it forward") = if a disclaimer in a business-vs-consumer contract is unclear, the court picks the meaning that is WORST for the business, so general words like "any loss" usually don't cover the business's own carelessness (negligence). Between two businesses, the clause is read neutrally. Scope = a disclaimer never covers things the business did that were completely outside what the contract was for (e.g. a "secure" cloakroom leaving the key on the desk).

Write — Issue

Write — Law

Where a disclaimer is contained in a contract between two businesses, the court presumes equal bargaining power and interprets it neutrally. However, if the disclaimer is uncertain or ambiguous and is contained in a contract between a business and a consumer, the court will interpret it contra proferentem, that is, against the party seeking to rely upon it. If there is more than one way of interpreting the disclaimer, the court will choose the interpretation that favours the consumer and does not apply to the breach (White v John Warwick). There, a clause stating that "nothing in this agreement shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries" to hirers of tricycles was read as excluding only contractual liability, not liability for the owners' negligence when the seat tipped forward and injured the hirer.
Disclaimers will also not exclude liability for acts occurring outside the scope of the contract (Sydney City Council v West). There, a car-park attendant negligently allowed a person to take a car without producing proof of a ticket. Despite a widely drafted clause excluding responsibility for loss "however… caused", the council was liable, because the attendant's act was outside the scope of the contract.

Full mini-answer — 2024 Semester 2, Question 2: GlobalCorp v Technovate (25 marks)

Facts in one line: after 6 months of negotiations, Technovate contracts to build software for GlobalCorp; one week before signing, Alex (head of technology) "assured" it would handle at least 10,000 concurrent users; early on, Damon (junior sales rep) said it would "likely enhance productivity", was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise" operations; it handles only 5,000 users and GlobalCorp loses $250,000; the poorly written contract only says "industry-standard performance capabilities".

Remedies are then considered.

Issue 1: Alex's assurance

Issue. Is Alex's assurance that the software could handle at least 10,000 concurrent users an enforceable term of the contract between GlobalCorp and Technovate?

Law. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract, so its breach is a breach of contract; a representation is a statement made in connection with the contract that does not form part of it. The court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended, considering: (1) the language used; (2) the context; (3) the timing; (4) the maker's relative knowledge and expertise; and (5) the importance of the statement and whether it was relied on. A precise claim such as "this machine can process 500 units per hour" is likely a term.

Under the parol evidence rule, where a contract is in writing and appears to be entire, evidence of prior oral agreements cannot be admitted to add to it (Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor). The rule does not apply where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, or where the oral statement forms a collateral contract. A collateral contract requires: (1) an oral statement and a main written contract; (2) a promise made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) consistency with the main contract; and (4) entry into the main contract as consideration (De Lassalle v Guildford, where a landlord's oral assurance that the drains were in good order, given before the tenant handed over a lease silent on drains, was enforced). Breach of a collateral contract gives damages only.

Application. Language: Alex "assured" GlobalCorp of "at least 10,000 concurrent users", a strong, specific, measurable statement, very like "this machine can process 500 units per hour". Context: it was made in lengthy commercial negotiations for a customised program where scalability was central. Timing: it was made one week before signing. Maker: Alex is head of Technovate's technology department and relied on similar projects; GlobalCorp lacked this expertise. Importance: it "provided GlobalCorp with confidence in the software's scalability", and a 5,000-user limit was "not suitable" for its operations. Technovate may argue it was only an estimate and was not written into the contract; however, the strength of the words, Alex's expertise and GlobalCorp's reliance outweigh this.

Technovate will argue the parol evidence rule excludes the assurance. However, the contract "lacks detail and is very poorly written", does not address Technovate's points and has no entire-agreement clause, so after six months of negotiations it was not intended to be wholly in writing. Alternatively, there is a collateral contract: Alex's oral promise induced GlobalCorp to sign; it is consistent with the "industry-standard" clause; and GlobalCorp gave consideration by entering the main contract. Similar to De Lassalle v Guildford, where the drains assurance induced the tenant to sign a lease silent on drains, Alex's assurance induced GlobalCorp to sign a contract silent on capacity. The software handles only 5,000 users, so the promise is broken.

Opinions such as "I think you'll be very happy with it" are representations, not terms.

GlobalCorp may say productivity mattered to it, but the phrase is too vague to measure.

Breach of a condition allows termination and damages (Poussard v Spiers, where a lead singer missed the opening performances); breach of a warranty gives damages only (Bettini v Gye, missed rehearsals). A term is a condition if the innocent party would not have contracted without it (Associated Newspapers v Bancks, front-page placement).

Conclusion. The disclaimer does not protect the BCC, which is liable for the coat.

Remedies

Melissa may claim damages equal to the coat's value, proved by evidence of its market or replacement value, and must mitigate her loss, e.g. by cooperating with police (Payzu Ltd v Saunders).

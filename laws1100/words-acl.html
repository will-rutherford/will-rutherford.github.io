Preliminary: Is [P] a consumer, and is [D] in trade or commerce?

What this means: The consumer guarantees (promises the law automatically puts into every sale) only protect a "consumer" — and under s 3 that is much wider than "a shopper": anyone who paid $100,000 or less counts, even a business, unless they bought the goods to resell. "In trade or commerce" = the defendant was acting as a business (selling, advertising), not in a private/social setting. Note: ss 18 and 29 don't need a consumer — anyone, even a competitor, can sue.

WRITE — Law:

Under s 3 of the ACL, a person acquires goods or services as a "consumer" if the amount paid was not more than $100,000; or the goods or services were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption; or the goods were a vehicle or trailer acquired principally for transporting goods on public roads. Only one limb need be satisfied. The definition is wide enough to include businesses, to the extent they are not acquiring goods for resale: for example, a restaurant owner buying cutlery, furniture or food items for the menu is a consumer: Star Kingdom Investments v Camatic [2024] FCA 329. Separately, the ACL only applies to conduct "in trade or commerce", which under s 2(1) means trade or commerce within Australia or between Australia and places outside Australia, and includes any business or professional activity, whether or not carried on for profit: Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978).

Issue 1: Misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18)

What this means: A business must not, while doing business, give people a false impression. You must walk through four requirements one by one (person → in trade or commerce → engaged in conduct → misleading), and test "misleading" from the point of view of the target audience including the gullible (the Taco Bell tests + Annand) — NOT your own view. "Intention is irrelevant" = it doesn't matter if they didn't mean to mislead. Students who skipped Taco Bell/Annand got half marks.

WRITE — Law:

Section 18(1) of the ACL provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". Intention or fault is irrelevant. There are four requirements. (1) A "person" is interpreted broadly and includes individuals and corporations. (2) "Trade or commerce" includes any business or professional activity, whether or not for profit: s 2(1); Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978). (3) "Engaging in conduct" includes doing or refusing to do an act, such as making a statement, claim or promise: s 2(2). (4) To mislead is to lead into error; to deceive is to cause a person to believe something false: Weitmann v Katies (1977). The test is objective and it is unnecessary to prove anyone was actually misled.
In Taco Co of Australia v Taco Bell (1982), a Sydney restaurant already trading as "Taco Bell" sued the US Taco Bell chain when it opened in Sydney under the same name; the conduct was held misleading because customers would believe the businesses were connected. The court set two tests: first, identify the relevant section of the public at which the conduct is directed; second, consider the effect on all who come within it, "including the astute and the gullible, the intelligent and the not so intelligent". In Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979), the second test was applied by reference to a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed, but perhaps of somewhat less than average intelligence and background knowledge", though not someone "quite unusually stupid". Only a small percentage of the audience need be misled: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010).

Issue 2: False or misleading representations (s 29)

What this means: s 29 is a list of specific kinds of false claims (about quality, newness, reviews, approval, price, origin…). Name the exact paragraph (e.g. s 29(1)(a)). It overlaps with s 18 — you can say the s 18 analysis applies equally. It matters because the ACCC can get pecuniary penalties (big fines) for s 29 but not for s 18 alone. A "representation" = a statement or impression given about something.

WRITE — Law:

Section 29 provides that a person must not, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or possible supply of goods or services, or the promotion of goods or services, make certain false or misleading representations. Unlike contract law, there is no need to classify the statement as fraudulent, negligent or innocent. There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" in s 29 and "misleading or deceptive" in s 18: ACCC v Coles Supermarkets Australia Pty Ltd [2014] FCA 634 at [40]; Optus Mobile v Telstra [2018] FCA 745. In ACCC v C.I. & Co Pty Ltd [2010] FCA 1511 (the Egg Case), cage eggs were sold labelled "free range" and later "Fresh Range – Omega 3"; consumers had no practical way of telling once the eggs were in the carton. The court held this misleading and a false representation of the eggs' quality and characteristics, describing it as a "cruel deception" involving a "high level of dishonesty".

Issue 3: Consumer guarantees (ss 54, 55, 56 and 64)

What this means: The ACL automatically puts guarantees (legal promises) into every sale to a consumer — e.g. the goods will work properly (s 54), will do the job you told the seller you needed (s 55), and will match how they were described (s 56). The business cannot cancel them by any clause, sign or manual (s 64 — the clause is "void" = has no legal effect). Only for consumers who bought something — never for a competitor.

WRITE — Law:

The ACL contains statutory guarantees that protect consumers who acquire goods in trade or commerce, and any term that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify them is void (s 64). Under s 54, goods supplied to a consumer in trade or commerce (other than by auction) must be of acceptable quality: fit for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied, acceptable in appearance and finish, free from defects, safe and durable, as a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the state and condition of the goods (including hidden defects) would regard as acceptable: Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas Pty Ltd [2015] VCAT 2044; Mitchell v Oz Design Furniture Pty Ltd [2016] VCAT 108 (both defective-furniture claims applying s 54). Under s 55, goods must be reasonably fit for any purpose the consumer makes known, expressly or by implication, to the supplier or a person who conducted the prior negotiations (s 55(2)), and for any purpose the supplier represents they are fit for; the guarantee does not apply if the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable to rely, on the supplier's skill or judgment. Under s 56, goods supplied by description must correspond with that description.

Issue 4: Unconscionable conduct (ss 20, 21, 22)

What this means: "Unconscionable" = so unfair it's against good conscience — a stronger business unfairly taking advantage of its bargaining power or of a customer's weakness (old age, poor eyesight, poor English, grief, pressure). Use s 21 (business dealings); s 20 is the backup that adopts the old case law (Amadio). s 22 is a checklist of factors: procedural = how the deal was made (pressure, couldn't read it); substantive = how harsh the terms are.

WRITE — Law:

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that unfairly takes advantage of a superior bargaining position or of another person's weakness or disadvantage. Section 20 prohibits conduct in trade or commerce that is unconscionable within the meaning of the unwritten law, as in Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, which requires that (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage, (2) the stronger party knew or should have known of it, and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it; Kitto J in Blomley v Ryan (1956) described special disadvantage as arising from "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties". Section 20 does not apply where conduct falls within s 21. Section 21 provides that a person must not, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or possible supply of goods or services to a person (other than a listed public company), engage in conduct that is, in all the circumstances, unconscionable; the court may consider the terms of the contract and the manner in which it is carried out (s 21(4)(c)). Section 22 lists matters the court may consider, and no single factor is determinative: procedural matters (s 22(1)(a), (c), (d)) and substantive matters (s 22(1)(b), (j), (k)). In ACCC v Coles [2014] FCA, Coles breached s 21 by misusing its bargaining power against its suppliers in a way that was "serious, deliberate and repeated".

Issue 5: Unfair contract terms (ss 23, 24, 25, 27)

What this means: A one-sided clause in a standard form contract (same pre-printed terms for everyone, "take it or leave it") is void (cancelled — it has no effect) if it is unfair. Three steps: (1) consumer contract (an individual buying for personal/home use) or small business contract; (2) standard form; (3) the term is unfair under s 24 (big imbalance + not needed to protect the business + causes harm). s 25 is a list of examples. Do ONE ILAC per clause.

WRITE — Law:

Under s 23 of the ACL, a term of a contract is unfair and therefore void if (1) the contract is a consumer contract or a small business contract, (2) it is a standard form contract, and (3) the term is unfair. A consumer contract is a contract for the supply of goods, services or an interest in land to an individual whose acquisition is wholly or predominantly for personal, domestic or household use or consumption: s 23(3). A standard form contract is one that is not individually negotiated, having regard to the matters in s 27(2), such as whether one party prepared it in advance and the other had to "take it or leave it". Under s 24(1), a term is unfair if it (a) would cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations, (b) is not reasonably necessary to protect the legitimate interests of the party advantaged by it, and (c) would cause detriment, financial or otherwise, to a party if relied on. The court must consider the extent to which the term is transparent — in reasonably plain language, legible, presented clearly and readily available — and the contract as a whole: s 24(2)–(3). Section 25 gives examples of unfair terms, including terms permitting one party (but not the other) to terminate the contract (Director v AAPT (2006)), to penalise the other for breach, or to vary the terms (Director v Trainstation (2006)).

Issue 6: Bait advertising, wrongly accepting payment, unsolicited goods (ss 35, 36, 40)

What this means: Three short specific bans. Bait advertising (s 35) = advertising a cheap deal to lure people in, knowing stock will run out almost straight away. Wrongly accepting payment (s 36) = taking money when you don't intend to, or know you can't, supply on time. Inertia selling / unsolicited goods (s 40) = sending things nobody ordered and then demanding payment. Only use the one(s) your facts raise.

WRITE — Law (copy only the sentence(s) for your provision):

Under s 35 of the ACL, a person must not engage in bait advertising — advertising goods or services at a price likely to attract buyers when it knows or should know that it is likely to run out of stock very quickly, as in Repco's "$1 Million Sizzling Sound Sellout". Under s 36, a person must not accept payment for goods or services where it does not intend to supply them, or knows or should know that it will be unable to supply them within the specified time or a reasonable time: Dawson v World Travel (1981); in ACCC v Ozdirect (2009), the ACCC sued a retailer that took payment for electronics it knew or ought reasonably to have known it could not supply within a reasonable time. Under s 40, a person must not assert a right to payment for unsolicited goods unless it reasonably believes it has that right; the recipient need not pay, is not liable for loss or damage to the goods unless wilful and unlawful, and after a period the goods become theirs free of charge.

Remedies (always) and defences

What this means: ALWAYS finish with remedies, with section numbers. Damages = money for your loss — but you must show the loss was caused "because of" the breach. Injunction = court order to stop doing something (e.g. take the label down). Rescission = cancelling the contract and putting both sides back where they started. Pecuniary penalty = a fine paid to the government, sought by the ACCC (the national consumer watchdog). "Nature and extent" = say what remedy, how much, and why it's big or small.

WRITE — Law:

The ACL provides a broader range of remedies than contract or misrepresentation law. A person who suffers loss "because of" a contravention may recover damages (s 236); the court may grant an injunction (ss 232–235), make compensation orders (ss 237–241), and declare a contract void, vary it, refuse to enforce it, or order a refund or repair. The ACCC may seek pecuniary penalties (ss 224–231), which are available for s 29 and s 21 but not s 18 alone, and an adverse publicity order (s 247). In setting a penalty, the court has regard to the nature and extent of the contravention, the loss caused, the circumstances and any previous similar conduct; in ACCC v C.I. & Co, the deliberate, hard-to-detect mislabelling attracted a penalty designed to punish and send a clear message to the industry.

Full mini-answer — 2026 S1 Q2 Emma v GreenPastures (the whole script, blanks filled)

Whether Emma has causes of action against GreenPastures (GP) under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), a Schedule to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), depends upon four issues: whether Emma is a "consumer"; misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18); false or misleading representations (s 29); and the consumer guarantees (ss 54–56). The nature and extent of the available remedies is then considered.

Preliminary: Is Emma a consumer, and is GP in trade or commerce?

Issue: Is Emma, as a restaurant owner, a "consumer" entitled to rely on the consumer guarantees in the ACL?

Law: Under s 3, a person acquires goods as a "consumer" if the amount paid was not more than $100,000, or the goods were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption. Only one limb need be satisfied. The definition includes businesses not acquiring goods for resale; a restaurant owner buying food for the menu is a consumer: Star Kingdom Investments v Camatic [2024] FCA 329.

Application: Emma paid $10,000, well below $100,000, so she is a consumer on the price limb alone. Packaged chicken is also ordinarily acquired for household consumption. GP may argue she re-supplies it to diners; however, she uses it as an ingredient, exactly like the restaurant owner in Star Kingdom.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Emma is a consumer under s 3. In any event, ss 18 and 29 do not require it.

Issue 1: Misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18)

Issue: Can Emma bring an action against GP for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 of the ACL?

Law: Section 18(1) provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". Intention is irrelevant. "Person" includes corporations. "Trade or commerce" includes any business activity: s 2(1); Re Ku-Ring-Gai (1978). Conduct includes making a statement, and silence that maintains a misleading impression: s 2(2); Henjo Investments v Collins (1988). To mislead is to lead into error: Weitmann v Katies (1977). In Taco Bell (1982), the US chain's use of an existing Sydney restaurant's name was misleading; the court asked (1) who is the relevant section of the public and (2) the effect on all within it, "including the astute and the gullible". Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979) uses a person of "somewhat less than average intelligence". In Given v Pryor (1980) an ad read as a whole was misleading though one phrase alone might be puffery; a partly true statement may mislead by its overall impression: Henderson v Pioneer Homes (1980).

Application:

Person and trade or commerce. GP is a business supplying meat across Queensland for profit (s 2(1)).

Conduct. GP labelled the chicken "Farm Fresh – Free Range Certified" with images of chickens on grass, and its representative assured Emma it was genuinely free range. Saying nothing about the caged birds maintained the impression, similar to Henjo.

Taco Bell test 1. The audience is Queensland purchasers of packaged chicken who choose free range for animal-welfare reasons, including restaurant owners like Emma.

Test 2 and Annand. A person of somewhat less than average intelligence would understand "Free Range Certified" with those images to mean every chicken had outdoor access. In fact a substantial proportion were barn raised or caged, so purchasers were led into error. GP may argue some chickens were free range; however, as in Henderson, the overall impression is false.

Puffery. GP may argue "highest animal welfare standards" is puffery. That has some force alone, but similar to Given v Pryor, read with "Certified" and the images, the package conveys a factual impression. "Certified" is not puffery.

Intention. Intention is irrelevant; in fact management approved the labelling believing consumers "would not be able to tell the difference".

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, GP has contravened s 18.

Issue 2: False or misleading representations (s 29)

Issue: Has GP made false or misleading representations to Emma in contravention of s 29 of the ACL?

Law: Section 29 prohibits, in trade or commerce and in connection with the supply or promotion of goods, false or misleading representations that goods are of a particular standard, quality or composition, or have a particular history (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)), or have an approval or performance characteristics (s 29(1)(g); Hartnell v Sharp (1975)). There is no meaningful difference from s 18: ACCC v Coles [2014] FCA 634 at [40]. In ACCC v C.I. & Co [2010] FCA 1511, cage eggs labelled "free range" were a false representation of quality; consumers could not detect it, and North J called it a "cruel deception".

Application: "Free Range" and "raised in natural open environments" represent a standard, composition and history (how the chicken was raised) — s 29(1)(a). "Certified" represents an approval it could not have — s 29(1)(g). The facts are strikingly similar to C.I. & Co: non-free-range product sold as free range, undetectable once packaged, bought because of animal-welfare concerns.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, GP has contravened s 29(1)(a) and (g).

Issue 3: Consumer guarantees (ss 54, 55, 56 and 64)

Issue: Has GP breached the consumer guarantees in ss 54–56 of the ACL in supplying chicken to Emma?

Law: Goods supplied by description must correspond with it (s 56). Goods must be fit for any purpose made known to the supplier or a person who conducted the negotiations (s 55(2)), unless reliance was absent or unreasonable. Goods must be of acceptable quality (s 54; Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas [2015] VCAT 2044). Terms excluding the guarantees are void (s 64).

Application: s 56: the chicken did not correspond with "Free Range Certified". s 55: Emma told GP's representative it was essential the chicken be genuinely free range (s 55(2)(a)); he assured her it was, and she relied on that by signing. Reliance was reasonable — she could not test how processed chicken was raised. s 54: GP will argue the chicken was safe and edible; on balance s 54 adds little. s 64: any term limiting these guarantees is void.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, GP has breached ss 55 and 56; s 54 is less likely.

Remedies

Emma may recover damages under s 236 for her decline in business and reputational harm, provided she proves they were caused by GP's mislabelling; this is likely, as the loss followed directly from the media investigation. She may seek rescission of the two-year agreement and a refund of the portion of the $10,000 for undelivered chicken, and an injunction (ss 232–235). The ACCC may seek pecuniary penalties for the s 29 contraventions (ss 224–231) and an adverse publicity order (s 247). As the conduct was deliberate, widespread and undetectable, as in C.I. & Co, a substantial penalty is likely. GP cannot rely on s 207 or s 208, as management knowingly approved the labelling.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Emma is likely to succeed against GP under ss 18, 29(1)(a) and (g), 55 and 56, and is entitled to damages, rescission with a refund, and an injunction, with significant ACCC penalties likely.

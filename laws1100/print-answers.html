A. 2026 Semester 1, Q1: Oliver v Skyline Events (25 marks), the most recent paper

Question text (click)

Oliver attends a large outdoor music festival organised by Skyline Events (Skyline). The event includes a main stage, several temporary stages and multiple food vendors. Skyline engages SoundCo to install and manage the sound system for the festival.

At around 2pm on the first day of the festival, several attendees notice that a section of temporary flooring near the main stage has become unstable, causing it to shift when walked on. Two attendees report the issue to Skyline staff. In response, a Skyline staff member inspects the area and posts a warning on the festival's official Facebook page advising patrons to take care near the main stage due to uneven flooring. No physical warning signs are placed at the site and no repairs are carried out at the time due to a shortage of staff.

Later that evening, Oliver walks across the uneven section of flooring after consuming several alcoholic drinks. As he approaches the area, he is looking towards the stage and not paying attention to the ground in front of him. As he steps onto the uneven section, the surface shifts beneath him, causing him to lose his balance and he begins to fall. At that moment, a technical malfunction with the equipment operated by SoundCo causes a loud and unexpected burst of noise from one of the speakers. Startled by the noise and already unbalanced, Oliver reacts quickly to try and steady himself, but twists his body awkwardly and falls heavily.

Oliver suffers a serious back injury requiring extended hospitalisation. Medical evidence later suggests that Oliver's injuries were caused by a combination of the unstable flooring and his sudden reaction to the noise. During his hospitalisation, he is prescribed strong pain medication for a prolonged period. Although taken in accordance with medical advice, Oliver develops an addiction to the medication and requires a rehabilitation program to treat his addiction. The rehabilitation program costs $5,000. Oliver also incurs significant medical expenses ($36,000) and a loss of six months' income due to his inability to work. His average annual salary is $95,000.

Oliver comes to you seeking advice against Skyline. He wishes to make a claim for damages under Tort Law. Advise Oliver. Please use ILAC to answer this question.

Answer plan (write this in planning time)
  1. Duty: occupier/organiser → patron (Zaluzna; Donoghue neighbour). Not a public authority. Short.
  2. Breach: s 9(1)(a) known at 2pm (2 reports + inspection); (b) shifting floor + crowds; (c) signs, cordon, repair. s 9(2): probability high; serious; burden low (staff shortage no excuse); social utility ≠ Watt. Facebook-only warning inadequate. Distinguish Bolton.
  3. Causation: (a) back injury + $36k medical + $47.5k income: but-for + multiple causes (SoundCo noise) → material contribution (Chappel, Cook); not a Yates-style break; scope OK (Overseas Tankships). (b) $5k rehab: distinguish Yates and Schneider (prescribed).
  4. Defences: contributory negligence: intoxication (Manley) + not looking (Ingram); ~25%. VAR fails (no knowledge; Facebook; not obvious).
  5. Remedies: $36,000 + $5,000 + $47,500 = $88,500 (+ pain and suffering, future loss); −25% → $66,375. Conclusion.

Whether Oliver can recover damages from Skyline Events ('Skyline') depends on whether he can establish each element of the tort of negligence (duty, breach and causation) and whether any defences reduce Skyline's liability.

Issue

Can Oliver bring an action against Skyline for the tort of negligence?

Law: Negligence generally

A tort is a civil wrong that allows an injured person to sue the wrongdoer for damages. A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. To succeed, Oliver must establish on the balance of probabilities that: (1) Skyline owed him a duty of care; (2) Skyline breached that duty; and (3) the breach caused his loss, which falls within the scope of Skyline's liability. Defences and remedies must then be considered. The common law applies together with the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA').

1. Duty of care

Law

In Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, a manufacturer was held to owe a duty of care to the ultimate consumer of a ginger beer containing a decomposed snail. Lord Atkin's 'neighbour principle' requires reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that one can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure one's 'neighbour', being persons so closely and directly affected that they ought reasonably to be in contemplation. Later cases recognised established categories of duty. Relevantly, an occupier of premises owes a duty of care to all persons entering the premises to ensure that the premises are safe: Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987).

Application

Skyline organised the festival and controlled the site, including the temporary flooring near the main stage. It was therefore the occupier. Oliver entered the site as a festival patron (it is assumed he held a valid ticket). The relationship falls within the established occupier–entrant category in Zaluzna. It was also reasonably foreseeable that carelessness about the safety of the flooring could injure a patron walking near the main stage, so Oliver was Skyline's 'neighbour'. Skyline is a private event organiser, not a public authority, so the hidden-risk analysis applicable to public authorities is not required.

Conclusion

Skyline owed Oliver a duty of care.

2. Breach of duty

Law

A duty of care is not automatically breached. Under s 9(1) CLA, a person breaches their duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if (a) the risk was foreseeable, being one they knew or ought reasonably to have known about; (b) the risk was not insignificant; and (c) in the circumstances a reasonable person in their position would have taken those precautions. Under s 9(2), the court considers (a) the probability of harm, (b) the likely seriousness of the harm, (c) the burden of taking precautions and (d) the social utility of the activity. In Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951) an employer breached its duty by failing to give goggles to a worker blind in one eye, because harm to him would be so serious. In Watt v Hertfordshire County Council (1954) the social utility of rushing to save a life justified the risk of carrying unsecured equipment, so there was no breach.

In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket ball was hit out of the ground and struck a woman on the road. Because balls had very rarely left the ground over many years, the probability of harm was very low and the burden of eliminating the risk was high, so a reasonable person would not have taken further precautions and there was no breach.

Application

Foreseeability (s 9(1)(a)). Skyline actually knew of the risk. By about 2pm, two attendees had reported the unstable flooring and a Skyline staff member inspected it and acknowledged the danger by posting a warning about 'uneven flooring'. The risk was therefore foreseeable.

Not insignificant (s 9(1)(b)). The flooring shifted when walked on and was located near the main stage, the busiest part of a large festival, through which many patrons would pass. The risk was not insignificant.

Reasonable person (s 9(1)(c) and s 9(2)). A reasonable festival organiser would have cordoned off the area, placed physical warning signs and repaired or replaced the section. The s 9(2) factors support this:

  • Probability (a): The probability of harm was high. Large crowds would walk across the area for the rest of the day and into the evening, often in poor light, looking at the stage rather than the ground, and in many cases after drinking alcohol.
  • Seriousness (b): A fall on shifting flooring in a crowd can cause serious injuries such as back and head injuries, as in fact occurred. As in Paris v Stepney, the seriousness of the potential harm demanded more than minimal precautions.
  • Burden (c): The burden was low. Barrier tape, a physical sign or rerouting patrons would have been quick and inexpensive. Skyline may argue that a staff shortage made repairs impossible at the time. However, staffing levels were within Skyline's own control, and even if repairs had to wait, cordoning the area or placing a sign required minimal resources.
  • Social utility (d): A music festival has entertainment and commercial value, but unlike the emergency rescue in Watt v Hertfordshire, nothing urgent justified leaving a known hazard in place.

Skyline will argue that it took reasonable precautions by posting a warning on its official Facebook page. However, patrons already on site, watching performances in the evening, are unlikely to check the festival's Facebook page. The warning also did not identify the exact location and left the hazard in place, so it was not what a reasonable person would have done.

This case is distinguishable from Bolton v Stone. There, the harm was an extremely rare event that the defendant could not practically prevent. Here, the danger was specific, reported, inspected and likely to cause injury, and could have been avoided at little cost.

Margin note: the "Similar to / Unlike Bolton v Stone" sentence is the case analysis the feedback says HD answers do. Always compare the probability AND the burden.

Conclusion

Skyline breached its duty of care to Oliver under s 9 CLA.

3. Causation

Law

Under s 11(1) CLA, a breach causes harm if (a) the breach was a necessary condition of the harm (factual causation) and (b) it is appropriate for the scope of the defendant's liability to extend to the harm (scope of liability). Factual causation is usually tested with the 'but for' test. However, where harm has several causes, the court applies the 'material contribution' test: whether the defendant's conduct increased the risk of injury and that risk eventuated. The breach need not be the sole cause: Chappel v Hart (1998). In Cook v ACT Racing Club Inc [2001], an overheated sauna that dehydrated a jockey was held to have caused his heart attack even though dehydration was only one cause, because it significantly increased the risk of injury. An intervening act may break the chain of causation. In Yates v Jones [1990], a plaintiff injured in a car accident became addicted to heroin suggested by a friend while in hospital, and the addiction was held to have been caused by the friend, not the accident.

Under the scope of liability test, the type of harm must be a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the breach and not too remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]. In Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016), a plaintiff injured in a motorcycle accident deliberately turned to illegal drugs outside medical advice. This was held to be a voluntary, unreasonable act not forming part of prescribed treatment, so causation failed for the drug use. However, medical and psychological treatment costs were recoverable as the 'ordinary and natural consequences' of the accident.

Application

(a) Back injury, medical expenses ($36,000) and lost income.

Factual causation: But for Skyline's failure to repair, cordon off or signpost the flooring, Oliver would not have stepped onto the unstable section, lost his balance and begun to fall. However, the medical evidence shows that his injuries were caused by a combination of the unstable flooring and his sudden reaction to the noise from SoundCo's equipment. Because there are multiple causes, the material contribution test is more appropriate. Skyline's breach plainly increased the risk of injury, and that risk eventuated: Oliver was already unbalanced and falling because of the flooring when the noise occurred. Similar to Cook v ACT Racing Club, where the overheated sauna was only one cause of the heart attack, the flooring need not be the sole cause for Skyline to be liable.

Skyline may argue that SoundCo's malfunction was an intervening act that broke the chain of causation, as the friend's suggestion did in Yates v Jones. This argument is weak. Unlike Yates, where a third party's independent and later choice caused a separate harm (the addiction), here the noise occurred 'at that moment' while Oliver was already falling, and combined with the existing danger to produce a single injury. Further, a loud noise from speakers at a music festival is not unforeseeable, and SoundCo was engaged by Skyline itself. (Oliver may also have a separate claim against SoundCo for its malfunctioning equipment, but this advice is confined to Skyline.)

Scope of liability: A serious back injury is exactly the type of harm that is reasonably foreseeable from a patron falling on unstable flooring. Hospitalisation, medical expenses and loss of income while unable to work are the ordinary and natural consequences of that injury, as recognised in Schneider. Unlike the unforeseeable fire damage in Overseas Tankships, this harm is not remote.

(b) Addiction and rehabilitation program ($5,000).

Factual causation: But for the back injury, Oliver would not have been hospitalised or prescribed strong pain medication, and would not have become addicted. The breach is therefore an indirect but necessary condition of the rehabilitation cost.

Scope of liability: Skyline will rely on Yates v Jones and Schneider v Smith to argue the addiction is too remote. Both cases are distinguishable. In Yates, the addiction was to heroin suggested by a friend, which was an independent act of a third party. In Schneider, the plaintiff deliberately took illegal drugs without reporting his symptoms or following medical advice. By contrast, Oliver took medication that was prescribed to him in hospital and took it 'in accordance with medical advice'. There was no deliberate, voluntary or unreasonable act on his part to break the chain of causation. Consistent with Schneider, where treatment costs were recoverable as ordinary and natural consequences, dependence on prescribed pain relief after a serious injury and the cost of treating it are reasonably foreseeable consequences of Skyline's breach.

Margin note: applying BOTH tests separately to EACH head of damage under headings is what separated HD from Credit in the Tut Sub 2 feedback.

Conclusion

Skyline's breach caused Oliver's back injury, medical expenses, lost income and rehabilitation costs, all of which fall within the scope of its liability.

4. Defences

Law

If a plaintiff contributed in some way to their own injury, liability is apportioned between the parties and damages reduced (contributory negligence): Ingram v Britten [1994], where the plaintiff drove a tractor at speed but the defendant had failed to secure its metal frame; Manley v Alexander, where the plaintiff drank 12 stubbies and slept on the road before being negligently driven over. Voluntary assumption of risk is a complete defence but is very difficult to establish. The plaintiff must have had full knowledge and appreciation of the precise risk and freely accepted it: Agar v Hyde (2000). Under ss 13–15 CLA, a plaintiff is presumed aware of an obvious risk, and there is no duty to warn of one.

Application

Contributory negligence: Skyline will argue that Oliver contributed to his injury. He had consumed several alcoholic drinks and was looking towards the stage rather than at the ground in front of him. Similar to Manley v Alexander, where the plaintiff's intoxication contributed to his injury, Oliver's drinking likely affected his balance and awareness. Like the plaintiff in Ingram v Britten, he failed to take reasonable care for his own safety. On the other hand, the flooring shifted unexpectedly, and patrons at a music festival can be expected to look at the stage. Skyline's failure to address a known hazard therefore remains the primary cause. If Oliver had read the Facebook warning, his responsibility would be greater, but the facts do not say this and it is assumed he had not. On balance, a reduction of around 25% is likely.

Voluntary assumption of risk: This defence is unlikely to succeed. Unlike the rugby players in Agar v Hyde, who knew the inherent risks of their sport, there is no evidence Oliver knew the flooring was unstable. The only warning was online. The instability was not an obvious risk under ss 13–15 CLA, because the flooring only shifted when stepped on and walking across a festival floor is not an obviously risky activity.

Conclusion

Oliver's damages are likely to be reduced for contributory negligence, but Skyline has no complete defence.

5. Remedies

Law

The typical remedy in tort is compensatory damages. These aim to restore the plaintiff, so far as money can, to the position he would have been in had the tort not occurred, and they are assessed once and for all, so future losses must be estimated.

Application

Oliver may claim ordinary damages for:

  • medical expenses: $36,000;
  • rehabilitation program: $5,000;
  • lost income: six months of a $95,000 annual salary = $95,000 ÷ 12 × 6 = $47,500.

These total $88,500. Oliver may also claim an amount for pain and suffering from a serious back injury and extended hospitalisation and, because damages are assessed once and for all, any future medical costs or loss of earning capacity supported by medical evidence (the facts do not indicate any, so this is noted as an assumption). Applying a 25% reduction for contributory negligence, Oliver would recover 75% of $88,500 = $66,375, plus 75% of any general damages awarded.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Oliver can bring an action against Skyline in the tort of negligence and is likely to recover damages for his medical expenses, lost income and rehabilitation costs (approximately $66,375 after a reduction for contributory negligence).

B. 2025 Semester 2, Q1: Liam v Gladstone Recreation Authority (25 marks)

Question text (click)

Located in the city of Gladstone is a public swimming facility known as the Oceanside Leisure Centre. This facility includes an outdoor artificial lake, which is used for water activities and maintained by the Gladstone Recreation Authority (GRA).

In June 2024, during routine maintenance, GRA staff discovered that an underground drainage pipe, located in a deeper part of the lake, had become partially exposed during a storm. The exposed pipe created a strong current, posing a significant risk to swimmers. Internal reports recommended either closing the lake for a period of time while the problem was fixed or placing warning signs to alert swimmers of the potential danger. However, due to an upcoming summer festival that relied on the lake for activities, GRA officials decided not to take immediate action, opting instead to assess the issue after the festival.

During the festival, Liam, a 25-year-old competitive swimmer, entered the lake in a designated swimming zone and swam into the deeper water. Unaware of the exposed pipe, he was suddenly pulled under the water by the strong current. At the time of the incident, signs prohibiting swimming outside of designated areas were posted around the lake. Witnesses reported that Liam had been warned by GRA staff not to swim out too far from the shoreline, but he ignored these warnings and swam into the deeper section of the lake.

Though lifeguards were able to rescue him, Liam suffered severe lung damage due to water inhalation and required prolonged hospitalisation. The hospital fees totalled $189,000. Furthermore, Liam now experiences chronic respiratory issues, affecting his ability to train and compete in swimming events that previously earned him $60,000 each year. Liam was unable to find suitable work for 12 months after his release from hospital. Finally, Liam became addicted to the prescription medication that he was given in hospital. He sought treatment through a drug rehabilitation program, which cost $8,000.

Liam comes to you seeking advice. He wishes to make a claim for damages under Tort Law. Advise Liam on all possible arguments. Please use ILAC to answer this question.

Answer plan
  1. Duty: public authority as occupier of an artificial lake. Hidden risk (current from exposed pipe) → Nagle, Swain; distinguish Romeo, Vairy (obvious, natural). GRA: deep water is obvious + signs.
  2. Breach: s 9(1)(a) internal reports = actual knowledge; (b) "significant risk"; (c) reports themselves recommended closure or signs. s 9(2): probability (festival crowds), seriousness (drowning), burden (signs cheap), social utility (festival ≠ Watt). Distinguish Bolton.
  3. Causation per head: (a) $189k hospital: but-for (would he have heeded a sign?) + scope. (b) lost earnings, 12 months × $60k + future capacity: other causes? (Schneider resignation point). (c) $8k rehab: prescription → distinguish Yates, Schneider.
  4. Defences: CN strong (ignored staff warning + signs; Ingram). VAR/obvious risk: fails, as he was unaware of the pipe (Agar v Hyde); ss 17–19 dangerous recreational activity arguable but weak.
  5. Remedies: $189,000 + $60,000 + $8,000 = $257,000 + future loss of earning capacity + pain and suffering; −30% → $179,900. Exemplary? (deliberate decision). Conclusion.

Whether Liam can recover damages from the Gladstone Recreation Authority ('GRA') depends on whether GRA, as a public authority, owed him a duty of care, whether it breached that duty, whether the breach caused each of his losses, and whether any defences apply.

Issue

Can Liam bring an action against GRA for the tort of negligence?

Law: Negligence generally

A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. Liam must establish on the balance of probabilities that GRA (1) owed him a duty of care, (2) breached that duty, and (3) caused his loss through that breach, with the loss falling within the scope of GRA's liability. Defences and remedies must then be considered under the common law and the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA').

1. Duty of care

Law

Under the neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, a person must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that could reasonably foreseeably injure persons closely and directly affected by them. An occupier owes a duty to persons entering its premises to ensure they are safe: Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987). Public authorities often control public spaces such as waterways and may be occupiers. However, it is questionable whether they automatically owe a duty of care. This depends on whether there is a hidden risk requiring a warning or prevention, or whether the risk is so obvious to a reasonable person that no duty exists. In Nagle v Rottnest Island Authority (1993), a submerged rock in an area signposted as a 'Swimming area' was a hidden risk, and because the sign encouraged swimming there a duty to warn was owed. In Swain v Waverley (2005), a submerged sandbar between the flags at a patrolled beach was a hidden risk giving rise to a duty to warn. By contrast, in Romeo v Conservation Commission (1998) (an unfenced cliff top) and Vairy v Wyong Shire Council (2005) (diving from a natural rock platform at an unpatrolled beach) the risks were obvious and no liability arose.

Application

GRA is a public authority that maintains and controls the artificial lake at the Oceanside Leisure Centre, so it is the occupier of the lake. Similar to Nagle, GRA marked out a 'designated swimming zone' and was running a festival that relied on the lake, which actively encouraged people to swim there. Like the submerged rock in Nagle and the sandbar in Swain, the exposed pipe was underwater and invisible, and the strong current it created was a hidden risk that Liam was 'unaware' of.

GRA will argue, relying on Romeo and Vairy, that the risk of swimming into deep water away from the shore is obvious, particularly to a competitive swimmer, and that signs prohibited swimming outside designated areas. However, those cases concerned natural features, such as cliffs and rock platforms, whose dangers anyone could see. Here, the lake is artificial, the hazard was created by GRA's own drainage infrastructure, and the danger that materialised was not deep water itself but a concealed current. A reasonable person would not have known about it.

Conclusion

GRA owed Liam a duty of care to warn of, or prevent, the hidden risk created by the exposed pipe.

2. Breach of duty

Law

Under s 9(1) CLA, a person breaches their duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if (a) the risk was foreseeable, being one they knew or ought reasonably to have known about, (b) it was not insignificant, and (c) a reasonable person in their position would have taken those precautions. Under s 9(2), the court considers the probability of harm, the likely seriousness of the harm (Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951)), the burden of taking precautions, and the social utility of the activity (Watt v Hertfordshire County Council (1954), where the urgency of saving a life justified the risk taken). In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket club was not in breach when a ball struck a person outside the ground, because such hits were extremely rare and the burden of eliminating that risk was high.

Application

Foreseeability (s 9(1)(a)). GRA actually knew of the risk. Its own staff discovered the exposed pipe during routine maintenance, and internal reports identified the danger.

Not insignificant (s 9(1)(b)). GRA's own reports described the current as 'posing a significant risk to swimmers', so the risk was clearly not insignificant.

Reasonable person (s 9(1)(c) and s 9(2)). A reasonable authority would have either closed the lake or posted specific warning signs, which are exactly the precautions its internal reports recommended.

  • Probability (a): The probability of harm was high. A festival would bring large numbers of swimmers into the lake, and it was foreseeable that some, especially strong swimmers, would move into the deeper water where the pipe was located.
  • Seriousness (b): Being pulled under by a strong current can cause drowning, death or, as here, severe lung damage. As in Paris v Stepney, where the seriousness of the consequences required precautions, the gravity of the potential harm here was extreme.
  • Burden (c): GRA will argue that closing the lake during the festival would have been costly and disruptive. However, the alternative precaution, warning signs about the current or roping off the deeper section, was cheap and quick and would not have stopped the festival.
  • Social utility (d): The festival had community and recreational value. However, unlike the emergency rescue in Watt v Hertfordshire, it was not urgent, and its commercial and recreational purposes do not justify exposing swimmers to a known and significant danger.

GRA may point to the general signs prohibiting swimming outside designated areas and the staff warnings not to swim too far out. However, these were generic and did not tell swimmers about the specific danger of the current. This case is clearly distinguishable from Bolton v Stone. There, the risk was remote and costly to eliminate. Here, the risk was known, documented as significant and likely to eventuate during a festival, and simple precautions were recommended but deliberately postponed.

Conclusion

GRA breached its duty of care to Liam under s 9 CLA.

3. Causation

Law

Under s 11(1) CLA, Liam must show (a) factual causation, meaning the breach was a necessary condition of the harm, tested by asking whether 'but for' the breach the harm would have occurred, and (b) that it is appropriate for GRA's scope of liability to extend to the harm, meaning the type of harm was reasonably foreseeable and not too remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]. In Yates v Jones [1990], an addiction to heroin suggested by a friend while the plaintiff was in hospital after a car accident was held to have been caused by the friend, not the accident. In Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016), the plaintiff's deliberate use of illegal drugs, outside medical advice, to cope with pain was not caused by the accident. However, medical and psychological treatment costs were recoverable as 'ordinary and natural consequences', and losses from his resignation were not recoverable because there were other causes unrelated to the accident.

Application

(a) Lung damage and hospital fees ($189,000). Factual causation: But for GRA's failure to close the lake or warn of the current, Liam would not have been pulled under. GRA will argue that Liam ignored staff warnings and signs, so he would have ignored any further warning as well. However, the warnings he received were general ('don't swim too far'). A specific warning of a dangerous current from an exposed pipe, or closure of the lake, would likely have prevented a competitive swimmer from entering that area. Closure would certainly have done so. Scope of liability: Severe lung damage from water inhalation is precisely the type of harm that is foreseeable from a strong underwater current, and the hospital fees are its ordinary and natural consequence. Both tests are satisfied.

(b) Lost earnings and chronic respiratory issues. Factual causation: But for the incident, Liam would not have developed chronic respiratory issues and would have continued to earn around $60,000 a year from swimming. Scope of liability: Loss of income while recovering, and reduced capacity to compete, are foreseeable consequences of serious lung damage to an athlete. GRA may argue, relying on Schneider, where lost income from the plaintiff's resignation was refused because of other causes, that Liam's inability to find 'suitable work' for 12 months reflects other factors, such as the job market or his own choices. No such external causes appear on the facts. It is assumed the 12 months of lost income resulted from his injury, so this loss is likely within scope.

(c) Addiction and rehabilitation ($8,000). Factual causation: But for the injury, Liam would not have been given the prescription medication in hospital or become addicted. Scope of liability: This case is distinguishable from Yates v Jones, where the addictive drug (heroin) was suggested by a friend, and from Schneider, where the plaintiff deliberately chose illegal drugs outside medical advice. Here, the medication was prescribed to Liam in hospital as part of his treatment. Assuming he took it as directed (the facts do not suggest otherwise), there was no voluntary, unreasonable act breaking the chain. Consistent with Schneider, the cost of treating a consequence of medical treatment is an ordinary and natural consequence of the injury and is within GRA's scope of liability.

Conclusion

GRA's breach caused Liam's hospital fees, lost earnings and rehabilitation costs, all of which are within the scope of GRA's liability.

4. Defences

Law

Where the plaintiff contributed to their own injury, liability is apportioned and damages reduced: Ingram v Britten [1994]; Manley v Alexander. Voluntary assumption of risk is a complete defence but is very difficult to establish. The plaintiff must have had full knowledge and appreciation of the precise risk that eventuated and freely accepted it: Agar v Hyde (2000). Under ss 13–14 CLA, a plaintiff is presumed aware of an obvious risk unless they prove otherwise; under s 15 there is no duty to warn of an obvious risk; and under ss 17–19 there is no liability for harm from an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity.

Application

Contributory negligence: This is GRA's strongest argument. Liam ignored signs prohibiting swimming outside designated areas and ignored direct warnings from GRA staff not to swim far from the shore, and he deliberately swam into deeper water. Similar to the plaintiff in Ingram v Britten, whose own careless conduct contributed to his injury despite the defendant's failure, Liam failed to take reasonable care for his own safety. As a competitive swimmer, he should have appreciated the importance of following safety instructions. Against this, he did start in a designated zone and could not have known of the hidden current. A significant reduction, perhaps around 30%, is likely.

Voluntary assumption of risk / obvious risk: GRA may argue that Liam, an experienced swimmer who ignored warnings, voluntarily assumed the risk of deep-water swimming, or that this was an obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity under ss 17–19 CLA. However, unlike the rugby players in Agar v Hyde, who knew the precise risks inherent in a scrum, Liam was 'unaware of the exposed pipe'. The precise risk that eventuated, a strong current, was hidden, not obvious, so he cannot be presumed aware of it under ss 13–14. Recreational swimming in a managed lake is also unlikely to be a 'dangerous' recreational activity. This defence is likely to fail.

Conclusion

Liam's damages are likely to be reduced for contributory negligence, but GRA has no complete defence.

5. Remedies

Law

Tort damages are compensatory and aim to restore the plaintiff, so far as money can, to his position had the tort not occurred. Ordinary damages are assessed once and for all, so future losses must be estimated. Exemplary damages may be awarded where the defendant deliberately and maliciously disregarded the plaintiff's rights.

Application

Liam may claim:

  • hospital fees: $189,000;
  • lost income for 12 months after release: 12 months of $60,000 a year = $60,000 (plus income lost during his hospitalisation, which is not quantified in the facts);
  • drug rehabilitation: $8,000.

These total $257,000. Because damages are assessed once and for all, Liam should also claim an estimated amount for future loss of earning capacity, since his chronic respiratory issues affect his ability to train and compete, and an amount for pain and suffering. Applying a 30% reduction for contributory negligence, Liam would recover 70% of $257,000 = $179,900, plus 70% of any future and general damages. Liam may also argue for exemplary damages because GRA consciously chose to put the festival ahead of known safety recommendations. However, as GRA's conduct was not malicious, such an award is unlikely.

Overall conclusion

Question text

She has strong personal views about animal welfare and promotes to customers that her meals are prepared using genuinely free-range products. Before entering into an agreement with GP, Emma informs GP's representative that it is essential the chicken be genuinely free range, as this is central to both her business model and her personal ethical standards. Several months into the agreement, a media investigation reveals that a substantial proportion of the chicken sold as "free range" by GP was not produced in free-range conditions. Emma receives customer complaints, suffers reputational harm and experiences a decline in business.

  2. s 18: 4 requirements; label + images + rep's assurance; Taco Bell/Annand; puffery ("highest welfare standards") v specific ("Free Range Certified"); intention irrelevant but management knew.
  3. s 29(1)(a) quality/composition/history + (g) approval ("Certified"); C.I. & Co Egg Case is near-identical; ACCC v Coles.

Whether Emma has causes of action against GreenPastures (GP) under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), a Schedule to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), depends upon four issues: whether Emma is a "consumer"; misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18); false or misleading representations (s 29); and the consumer guarantees (ss 54–56). The nature and extent of the available remedies is then considered.

Preliminary Issue: Is Emma a "consumer"?

Issue: Is Emma, as a restaurant owner, a "consumer" entitled to rely on the consumer guarantees in the ACL?

Law: Under s 3 of the ACL, a person acquires goods as a "consumer" if the amount paid was not more than $100,000, or the goods were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption. Only one limb need be satisfied. The definition is wide enough to include businesses, to the extent that they are not acquiring goods for resale. For example, a restaurant owner who buys food items for their menu is a consumer, because food is of a kind ordinarily acquired for household consumption: Star Kingdom Investments v Camatic [2024] FCA 329.

Application: Emma paid $10,000 upfront, well below the $100,000 threshold, so she is a consumer on the price limb alone even though the chicken is acquired for her business. Further, packaged chicken is plainly of a kind ordinarily acquired for household consumption. GP may argue that Emma acquired the chicken to re-supply it to diners and therefore falls outside the definition. However, Emma does not resell the packaged chicken; she uses it as an ingredient in her meals, exactly like the restaurant owner buying food for the menu in the lecture example and Star Kingdom.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Emma is a consumer under s 3. In any event, ss 18 and 29 do not require her to be a consumer.

Why: the question flags "small restaurant" deliberately. Markers want s 3 dealt with briefly and a note that ss 18/29 don't depend on it (Sub 5 feedback).

Issue 1: Misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18)

Issue: Can Emma bring an action against GP for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 of the ACL?

Law: Section 18(1) provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". Intention or fault is irrelevant. There are four requirements.

Person. "Person" is interpreted broadly and includes corporations.

In trade or commerce. Under s 2(1), this includes any business or professional activity, whether or not carried on for profit: Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978).

Engaged in conduct. Under s 2(2), this includes making a statement, claim or promise, and silence where it maintains a misleading impression: Henjo Investments v Collins (1988).

Misleading or deceptive. To mislead is to lead into error; to deceive is to cause a person to believe something false: Weitmann v Katies (1977). The test is objective and it is unnecessary to prove anyone was actually misled. In Taco Co of Australia v Taco Bell (1982), a Sydney restaurant already trading as "Taco Bell" sued the US Taco Bell chain, which began trading in Sydney under the same name; the conduct was held misleading because customers would believe the two were connected. The court established two tests: first, identify the section of the public to which the conduct is directed; second, consider the effect on all within it, "including the astute and the gullible". In Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979) the second test was framed by reference to a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed, but perhaps of somewhat less than average intelligence", though not someone "quite unusually stupid". Only a small percentage of the audience need be misled: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010).

Mere puffery is not necessarily misleading, but in Given v Pryor (1980) an advertisement read as a whole conveyed a misleading impression (that homes could be built on land) even though one phrase alone might have been puffery. A statement may also mislead through its overall impression even if partly true: Henderson v Pioneer Homes (1980).

Application:

Person and trade or commerce. GP is a business, and therefore a legal person, supplying packaged meat across Queensland for profit, which is a business activity in trade or commerce within Australia under s 2(1).

Engaged in conduct. GP engaged in conduct in two ways. First, it labelled its chicken "Farm Fresh – Free Range Certified" with images of chickens on green grass and the statements "raised in natural open environments" and "produced to the highest animal welfare standards". Secondly, GP's representative assured Emma directly that the product met her requirement that it be genuinely free range. Further, by packaging all products as "free range" and saying nothing about the barn-raised and caged birds, GP maintained a misleading impression, similar to the non-disclosure in Henjo.

Misleading or deceptive — Taco Bell test 1. The relevant section of the public is Queensland purchasers of packaged chicken, particularly those who, like Emma and her customers, choose free-range products because of animal-welfare concerns, including restaurant owners buying for ethically marketed menus.

Taco Bell test 2 and Annand. Considering all within that group, including a person of somewhat less than average intelligence and background knowledge, the words "Free Range Certified", together with images of chickens roaming outdoors, would be understood to mean that every chicken in the pack had access to an outdoor area. In fact, a substantial proportion were barn raised or caged. Purchasers were therefore led into error. GP may argue that some chickens were genuinely free range, so the label is partly true. However, as in Henderson v Pioneer Homes, the overall impression of the packaging — that this product is free range — is false for a substantial part of the product.

Puffery. GP may argue that "produced to the highest animal welfare standards" is mere puffery that no purchaser would take literally. That argument has some force for that phrase alone. However, similar to Given v Pryor, the packaging must be read as a whole: combined with the specific, verifiable claim "Free Range Certified" and the images, it conveys a clear factual impression about how the chickens were raised. The word "Certified" is not puffery at all.

Representative's assurance. The representative's assurance was a specific statement of fact given in response to Emma's express requirement. It was false and Emma relied on it in entering the two-year agreement.

Intention. Intention is irrelevant under s 18, so GP cannot escape liability by arguing that consumers would not notice. In fact, senior management approved the labelling knowing that free range requires outdoor access and believing consumers "would not be able to tell the difference". This aggravates the conduct.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, GP has engaged in misleading or deceptive conduct in contravention of s 18.

Issue 2: False or misleading representations (s 29)

Issue: Has GP made false or misleading representations to Emma in contravention of s 29 of the ACL?

Law: Section 29 provides that a person must not, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or promotion of goods, make false or misleading representations, including that goods are of a particular standard, quality, value, grade or composition, or have had a particular history (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)), or that goods have a sponsorship, approval, performance characteristics, uses or benefits (s 29(1)(g)). There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" and "misleading or deceptive": ACCC v Coles Supermarkets Australia Pty Ltd [2014] FCA 634 at [40].

In ACCC v C.I. & Co Pty Ltd [2010] FCA 1511 (the "Egg Case"), the respondents sold large quantities of cage eggs labelled "free range", and later "Fresh Range – Omega 3" with packaging that maintained a free-range impression. Consumers had no practical way of telling how the eggs had been produced once they were in the carton. North J held the conduct misleading and a false representation of the eggs' quality and characteristics, describing it as involving a "high level of dishonesty" and a "cruel deception" of consumers who bought free range because of animal-welfare concerns.

Application: The labels and the representative's assurance were made in connection with the supply of chicken to Emma.

s 29(1)(a). "Free Range" and "raised in natural open environments" represent that the chicken is of a particular standard, quality and composition, and has had a particular history (how it was raised). For a substantial proportion of the product this was false.

s 29(1)(g). "Certified" represents that the product has an approval or certification it could not truly have, given that a substantial part of the product did not meet the free-range requirement that GP knew of.

Comparison with the Egg Case. The facts are strikingly similar to C.I. & Co. As there, GP sold non-free-range animal products as free range; as there, once processed and packaged, purchasers like Emma had no practical way of detecting the difference — which is exactly what GP's management relied on. Emma, like the consumers in the Egg Case, bought the product specifically because of her animal-welfare concerns. This is the same kind of hard-to-detect, deliberate deception the court described as a "cruel deception".

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, GP has contravened s 29(1)(a) and (g).

Issue 3: Consumer guarantees (ss 54, 55, 56)

Issue: Has GP breached the consumer guarantees in ss 54–56 of the ACL in supplying chicken to Emma?

Law: The ACL implies guarantees into the supply of goods to consumers, and any term that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify them is void (s 64).

s 56 — Description. Goods supplied by description must correspond with that description.

s 55 — Disclosed purpose. Goods must be reasonably fit for any purpose the consumer makes known, expressly or by implication, to the supplier or a person who conducted the prior negotiations (s 55(2)), and for any purpose the supplier represents they are fit for. The guarantee does not apply if the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable to rely, on the supplier's skill or judgment.

s 54 — Acceptable quality. Goods must be fit for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied, acceptable in appearance and finish, free from defects, safe and durable, as a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with their state and condition, including hidden defects, would regard as acceptable: Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas Pty Ltd [2015] VCAT 2044.

Application:

s 56. The chicken was supplied by the description "Free Range Certified". A substantial proportion did not correspond with that description, so s 56 is breached.

s 55. Before contracting, Emma expressly told GP's representative that it was essential the chicken be genuinely free range because it was central to her business model and ethical standards. This was a particular purpose made known to the person who conducted the negotiations (s 55(2)(a)). The representative assured her the product met those requirements, and Emma relied on that assurance by entering the agreement. Her reliance was reasonable: she had no way to test how processed chicken was raised and GP held itself out as an ethical supplier. Chicken that is not genuinely free range is not reasonably fit for Emma's disclosed purpose of serving genuinely free-range meals.

s 54. This is the weakest guarantee. GP will argue that the chicken was safe, edible and free from defects, and so fit for the purpose for which chicken is commonly supplied — eating. Emma may respond that a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the hidden "defect" (that it was not free range as labelled) would not regard it as acceptable. On balance, s 54 is unlikely to add much beyond ss 55 and 56.

s 64. Any term of the supply agreement that purports to limit GP's liability for these guarantees would be void.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, GP has breached ss 55 and 56, though a claim under s 54 is less likely to succeed.

Remedies: nature and extent

The ACL provides a broader range of remedies than contract or misrepresentation law.

Damages (s 236). Emma may recover damages for loss suffered "because of" GP's contraventions. Her loss includes the decline in business and reputational harm following the media investigation, and customer complaints. She must prove causation: that her lost business was caused by GP's mislabelling, rather than other factors. This should be possible because the loss arose directly from customers learning that the "free range" chicken she promoted was not free range. Her own marketing of free-range meals was made in reliance on GP's representations.

Rescission, refund and variation. The court may declare the two-year agreement void, refuse to enforce it, vary it or order a refund. Emma would seek rescission of the agreement, restoring the parties to their original positions as far as possible, and a refund of the portion of the $10,000 relating to undelivered chicken (and arguably the delivered non-free-range chicken). Since she is only several months into a two-year term, substantial restitution is still possible.

Injunction (ss 232–235). An injunction may be sought to stop GP continuing to label non-free-range chicken as "Free Range Certified".

Penalties and public enforcement. Emma may report GP to the ACCC, which may seek pecuniary penalties (ss 224–231) for the s 29 contraventions, compensation orders for other affected purchasers (ss 237–241) and an adverse publicity order (s 247). The extent of any penalty depends on the nature and extent of the contravention, the loss caused and the circumstances. Here the contravention was deliberate (approved by senior management), widespread (all products across Queensland), and designed to be undetectable. As in C.I. & Co, where the penalty was intended to send a clear message to the egg industry, a substantial penalty is likely.

Defences. GP cannot rely on s 207 (reasonable mistake of fact) or s 208 (act of another person or a cause beyond its control), as senior management knowingly approved the labelling.

Overall conclusion: Emma is likely to succeed against GP under ss 18, 29(1)(a) and (g), 55 and 56 of the ACL, and is entitled to damages for her lost business and reputational harm, rescission of the supply agreement with a refund, and an injunction, with the ACCC likely to seek significant penalties.

Marks: every element gets its own sub-heading; the Egg Case is used in BOTH Law (facts + held) and Application ("strikingly similar… as there…"); both sides argued on puffery, partial truth, resale, s 54; remedies address "nature and extent" explicitly (what, how much, and why heavy).

Layla recently started a small home-based baking business and needed a heavy-duty food mixer that could withstand regular, intensive use. She visited HomePro Appliances on the Gold Coast, a large retailer of kitchen and household equipment. She approached a young staff member, Tyler, who was arranging stock at the front counter and wearing a polo shirt embroidered with the store's logo.

  3. s 29(1)(a) standard/quality + (g) performance characteristics/uses.

Whether Layla can make a claim against HomePro Appliances (HomePro) under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), a Schedule to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), depends upon four issues: whether Layla is a "consumer"; the consumer guarantees (ss 54 and 55); misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18); and false or misleading representations (s 29). Order choice: here the product failing is the heart of the facts, so guarantees can go first. Either order is fine — just signpost it in the opener.

Preliminary Issue: Is Layla a "consumer"?

Issue: Is Layla, who bought the mixer for her baking business, a "consumer" under the ACL?

Law: Under s 3, goods are acquired by a "consumer" if the amount paid was not more than $100,000, or the goods were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption. The definition is wide enough to include businesses, to the extent they are not buying products for resale: Star Kingdom Investments v Camatic [2024] FCA 329.

Application: Layla paid $2,500, well below $100,000. Food mixers are also goods of a kind ordinarily acquired for household use, and HomePro is a retailer of household equipment. Layla bought the mixer to use in her business, not to resell it. Her business purpose does not prevent her being a consumer, just as the restaurant owner buying equipment in Star Kingdom was a consumer.

Conclusion: Layla is a consumer under s 3 of the ACL.

Issue 1: Consumer guarantees (ss 55, 54 and 64)

Issue: Has HomePro breached the consumer guarantees in ss 54 and 55 of the ACL in supplying the WhipMixer100 to Layla?

Law: The ACL guarantees protect consumers who acquire goods in trade or commerce. Any term that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify the guarantees is void (s 64).

s 55 — Fitness for disclosed purpose. Under s 55(1), goods must be reasonably fit for any disclosed purpose and for any purpose for which the supplier represents they are reasonably fit. A disclosed purpose is a particular purpose, whether or not a common one, that the consumer makes known expressly or by implication to the supplier or to a person by whom any prior negotiations were conducted (s 55(2)(a)). The guarantee does not apply if the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable for the consumer to rely, on the skill or judgment of the supplier.

s 54 — Acceptable quality. Goods must be of acceptable quality: fit for all purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied, acceptable in appearance and finish, free from defects, safe and durable, as a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the state and condition of the goods (including hidden defects) would regard as acceptable: Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas Pty Ltd [2015] VCAT 2044; Mitchell v Oz Design Furniture Pty Ltd [2016] VCAT 108.

Application:

s 55 — disclosed purpose. Layla expressly told Tyler she needed a powerful, reliable mixer for thick doughs and batters, used at least five hours daily, and asked whether it suited "commercial-style use". This is a particular purpose made known expressly. Tyler was a HomePro staff member, wearing a uniform embroidered with the store logo, who conducted the negotiations leading to the sale, so the purpose was disclosed to the supplier through a person conducting the negotiations (s 55(2)(a)). Tyler also represented that the mixer was fit for that purpose: "It will do the job you want and will mix all day", and "Yeah, for sure".

Reliance. Layla made it clear she was relying on Tyler's advice and bought the mixer "based on Tyler's recommendation". HomePro will argue reliance was unreasonable because Tyler said "the boss isn't in", that he did not usually work in that section, and that his knowledge came from his mum's use. These facts do give some support to HomePro. However, Tyler was the HomePro employee on duty, in uniform, who chose to give a confident, specific recommendation; there were no signs suggesting the mixer was only for domestic use; and an ordinary customer is entitled to rely on a large retailer's staff. Bob's later statement that Tyler was untrained and "not authorised" is an internal matter between HomePro and its staff, and does not change what Layla was reasonably entitled to rely on at the time of sale.

The mixer overheated and stopped working within weeks of the use Layla disclosed. It was therefore not reasonably fit for her disclosed purpose.

s 54 — acceptable quality. A $2,500 mixer — the most expensive in the store — that overheats and stops working within weeks is arguably not durable or free from defects. HomePro will argue that mixers are commonly supplied for domestic use, that the mixer was fit for that purpose, and that it failed only because of commercial use. This argument has some force, so s 54 is less certain than s 55. However, a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the fact that this premium mixer cannot cope with sustained use would arguably not regard it as acceptable for its price.

s 64 — the warranty limitation. Bob's reliance on the manual's statement that commercial use "voided the warranty" fails. Any term that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify the guarantees is void under s 64. In any event, the limitation was only in the manual, which Layla had not seen before buying.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, HomePro has breached s 55, and possibly s 54, and cannot rely on the warranty limitation.

Issue 2: Misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18)

Issue: Can Layla bring an action against HomePro for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 of the ACL?

Law: Section 18(1) provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". Intention is irrelevant. The requirements are: (1) a person, which includes a corporation; (2) in trade or commerce, which under s 2(1) includes any business or professional activity (Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978)); (3) engaging in conduct, which under s 2(2) includes making a statement or promise; and (4) conduct that is misleading or deceptive, meaning it leads into error (Weitmann v Katies (1977)). A representation about a future matter is taken to be misleading if the person had no reasonable grounds for making it (s 4; ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010)).

The test is objective. In Taco Co of Australia v Taco Bell (1982), where the US Taco Bell chain's use of the same name as an existing Sydney restaurant was held misleading, the court required (1) identifying the relevant section of the public, and (2) considering the effect on all within it, "including the astute and the gullible". Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979) applies this to a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed". Where conduct is directed at one person, as here, the question is its effect on that person in the circumstances. Mere puffery is not necessarily misleading, but in Given v Pryor (1980) statements read as a whole conveyed a misleading impression.

Application:

Person and trade or commerce. HomePro is a corporation operating a large retail business, which is clearly trade or commerce (s 2(1)). Tyler made the statements while working on the shop floor as HomePro's staff member, so his statements are treated as HomePro's conduct. (I assume Tyler was acting in the course of his employment.)

Engaged in conduct. Tyler made statements and a recommendation about the mixer (s 2(2)).

Misleading or deceptive. The relevant audience is customers like Layla — small-business owners seeking advice on appliances. Statements such as "It's solid" and "It's a really decent machine" are likely mere puffery, and "This is the most expensive mixer we have" appears to be true. However, the statements "It will do the job you want", "will mix all day" and "Yeah, for sure" in answer to whether it suited commercial-style use were specific claims about performance. A customer of below-average background knowledge would understand them to mean the mixer could handle five hours of heavy daily use. In fact, the mixer was not designed for commercial use. Like the advertisement in Given v Pryor, the overall impression of Tyler's recommendation was misleading.

Future matter (s 4). "It will mix all day" is a representation about the mixer's future performance. Tyler had no reasonable grounds for it: he admitted he did not usually work in that section and relied only on his mother's domestic use. It is therefore taken to be misleading under s 4.

Intention. It is irrelevant that Tyler may honestly have believed his advice.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, HomePro, through Tyler, engaged in misleading or deceptive conduct in contravention of s 18.

Issue 3: False or misleading representations (s 29)

Issue: Did HomePro make false or misleading representations to Layla in contravention of s 29 of the ACL?

Law: Section 29 prohibits, in trade or commerce and in connection with the supply of goods, false or misleading representations that goods are of a particular standard or quality (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)) or have particular performance characteristics, uses or benefits (s 29(1)(g); Hartnell v Sharp (1975)). There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" and "misleading or deceptive": ACCC v Coles Supermarkets Australia Pty Ltd [2014] FCA 634.

Application: Tyler's statements were made in connection with the supply of the mixer. By saying the mixer would "mix all day" and was suitable "for sure" for commercial-style use, HomePro represented that it had performance characteristics and uses (s 29(1)(g)) and was of a standard and quality (s 29(1)(a)) that it did not have, as Bob later confirmed it was not designed for commercial use. For the reasons under s 18 (ACCC v Coles), these representations were false or misleading.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, HomePro contravened s 29(1)(a) and (g).

Remedies

As a consumer, Layla is entitled to remedies for breach of the guarantees: the court may order a refund, replacement or repair, or compensation. Because the mixer failed entirely within weeks and is unfit for the purpose she disclosed, she should seek a full refund of $2,500 rather than repair. HomePro's refusal to provide a refund or replacement is not justified, and the "warranty void" limitation is ineffective under s 64.

Under s 236, Layla may also recover damages for loss suffered because of the contraventions, such as lost income from baking orders she could not fulfil while the mixer was not working, provided she proves this loss. She may also report HomePro to the ACCC, which may seek pecuniary penalties for the s 29 contraventions (ss 224–231). HomePro cannot rely on s 207 or s 208: the contravention arose from its own staff member's advice and HomePro did not take reasonable precautions, such as labelling the mixer as domestic-only or training staff.

Overall conclusion: Layla is likely to succeed against HomePro for breach of s 55 (and possibly s 54) and for contraventions of ss 18 and 29, and is entitled to a refund of $2,500 and damages for any proven business losses.

C · 2024 Semester 2, Q1 — Lisa v PrintPro (PP5000 printer) 25 marks

Question text

Lisa owns a graphic design business called Creative Visions. She wants to purchase a high-quality inkjet printer specifically for producing high-resolution marketing materials, including posters and brochures. While researching options, Lisa comes across a company called PrintPro, which advertises its latest model, the ProPrint5000 (PP5000). The advertisement for the PP5000 states that it is "the most reliable inkjet printer on the market."

Lisa contacts PrintPro and speaks with Tom, the Senior Sales Manager. During their conversation, Lisa asks Tom about the printer's ability to produce vibrant colours and its longevity under heavy use. Tom assures her that the PP5000 is designed for high-demand environments and guarantees excellent colour quality. Lisa then says, "I assume the company hasn't yet received any customer feedback about the PP5000, given that it has only just entered the market?" Tom avoids answering the question and redirects the conversation to again talk about colour quality.

Based upon the advertisement and Tom's assurances, Lisa decides to purchase the PP5000 for $50,000. After setting it up, she quickly encounters major functioning issues, including poor colour output and frequent paper jams that occur after the machine has been used consistently for more than 5 minutes. Frustrated, she tries to contact PrintPro for support, but cannot reach Tom or any other representative.

Upon further investigation, Lisa discovers an online blog where multiple people were complaining about colour quality issues with the PP5000, as well as a technical flaw in the design of the printer that caused ongoing paper jams. A number of bloggers explain how the previous year, PrintPro had sent them the PP5000 for testing prior to its release to the market. The problems had been reported to Tom, the Senior Sales Manager, who had done nothing to rectify the issues prior to its release to the market.

Lisa comes to you seeking advice. Advise Lisa as to whether she has any claims against PrintPro under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL)? Please use ILAC to answer this question.

Answer plan (5 lines)
  1. Preliminary: consumer — $50,000 ≤ $100,000 → yes despite business use; not resale.
  2. s 18: (a) ad "most reliable inkjet printer on the market" — puffery v Given v Pryor + PrintPro knew of design flaw; (b) Tom's assurances ("designed for high-demand", "guarantees excellent colour") — future matter s 4, no reasonable grounds; (c) Tom's evasion = silence (Henjo).
  3. s 29(1)(a) standard/quality; (g) performance characteristics; Optus v Telstra, ACCC v Coles.
  4. Guarantees: s 54 (design flaw, jams after 5 min, poor colour = defects, not fit for common purpose); s 55 (disclosed high-res + heavy use to Tom).
  5. Remedies: refund/rescission of $50,000, damages s 236 (lost work), ACCC penalties + s 247; no s 207/208 (Tom knew).

Whether Lisa has claims against PrintPro under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), a Schedule to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), depends upon four issues: whether Lisa is a "consumer"; misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18); false or misleading representations (s 29); and the consumer guarantees (ss 54 and 55). Remedies are then considered.

Preliminary Issue: Is Lisa a "consumer"?

Issue: Is Lisa, who purchased the PP5000 for her graphic design business, a "consumer" under the ACL?

Law: Under s 3, a person acquires goods as a "consumer" if the amount paid was not more than $100,000, or the goods were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use. The definition extends to businesses, to the extent they are not acquiring goods for resale: Star Kingdom Investments v Camatic [2024] FCA 329.

Application: Lisa paid $50,000, which is not more than $100,000. Although a $50,000 printer for high-demand environments is probably not a kind of good ordinarily acquired for household use, only one limb need be satisfied. Lisa acquired the printer to use in her business, not to resell.

Conclusion: Lisa is a consumer under s 3 on the price limb.

Issue 1: Misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18)

Issue: Can Lisa bring an action against PrintPro for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 of the ACL?

Law: Section 18(1) provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". Intention or fault is irrelevant. There are four requirements.

Person — interpreted broadly to include corporations. In trade or commerce — under s 2(1), includes any business or professional activity, whether or not for profit: Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978). Engaged in conduct — under s 2(2), includes making statements and refusing to do an act; silence may be conduct where failing to disclose creates or maintains a misleading impression: Henjo Investments v Collins (1988). A representation about a future matter is taken to be misleading if made without reasonable grounds (s 4; ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010)).

Misleading or deceptive — to mislead is to lead into error: Weitmann v Katies (1977). In Taco Co of Australia v Taco Bell (1982), the US Taco Bell chain's use of the same name as an existing Sydney restaurant was held misleading. The court set two tests: identify the relevant section of the public, then consider the effect on all within it, "including the astute and the gullible". Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979) tests this by reference to a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed, but perhaps of somewhat less than average intelligence". Only a small percentage need be misled: Powerballwin.com.

Puffery is not necessarily misleading, but in Given v Pryor (1980) an advertisement read as a whole conveyed the misleading impression that homes could be built on land. In Optus Mobile v Telstra [2018] FCA 745, Telstra's argument that "unlimited" in its advertising conveyed "nothing definitive" failed, and the claims contravened ss 18 and 29.

Application:

Person and trade or commerce. PrintPro is a company that advertises and sells printers for profit, so it is a person acting in trade or commerce (s 2(1)). Tom, as Senior Sales Manager, acted on PrintPro's behalf.

Engaged in conduct. PrintPro engaged in conduct in three ways: (1) the advertisement; (2) Tom's assurances; and (3) Tom's evasion of Lisa's question about customer feedback (s 2(2)).

(1) The advertisement — "the most reliable inkjet printer on the market". The relevant section of the public is businesses and individuals seeking high-quality printers, including those of below-average technical knowledge (Taco Bell; Annand). PrintPro will argue this is mere puffery — a superlative no reasonable buyer would take literally. However, "reliable" is a measurable quality, and PrintPro had already been told by testers of a design flaw causing ongoing paper jams. Like Telstra's "unlimited" in Optus v Telstra, and the advertisement in Given v Pryor, the claim in context conveys a factual impression that the printer is dependable, which was false. On balance it is likely to be misleading, even if only a small percentage of readers relied on it (Powerballwin.com).

(2) Tom's assurances. Tom told Lisa the PP5000 was "designed for high-demand environments" and "guarantee[d] excellent colour quality". These are specific statements, not puffery, and Lisa relied on them. To the extent they predict future performance, they are representations about a future matter. Tom had no reasonable grounds for them: he had been told by testers of colour problems and the paper-jam design flaw. They are therefore taken to be misleading under s 4.

(3) Tom's silence. When Lisa said she assumed PrintPro had received no customer feedback, Tom avoided the question and redirected to colour quality. Similar to Henjo Investments v Collins, where the failure to correct a misleading impression was conduct, Tom's evasion maintained Lisa's false belief that there were no known problems, when in fact testers had reported them to him.

Intention. Although Tom's knowledge makes the conduct more serious, intention is irrelevant to whether s 18 is breached.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, PrintPro has engaged in misleading or deceptive conduct in contravention of s 18.

Issue 2: False or misleading representations (s 29)

Issue: Has PrintPro made false or misleading representations to Lisa in contravention of s 29 of the ACL?

Law: Section 29 prohibits, in trade or commerce and in connection with the supply or promotion of goods, false or misleading representations that goods are of a particular standard, quality or grade (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)) or have performance characteristics, uses or benefits (s 29(1)(g); Hartnell v Sharp (1975)). There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" and "misleading or deceptive": ACCC v Coles Supermarkets Australia Pty Ltd [2014] FCA 634 at [40]; Optus Mobile v Telstra [2018] FCA 745.

Application: The advertisement and Tom's assurances were made in connection with the promotion and supply of the PP5000. "Most reliable" and "excellent colour quality" represent that the printer is of a particular standard and quality (s 29(1)(a)); "designed for high-demand environments" represents a performance characteristic and use (s 29(1)(g)). Given the jams after five minutes of consistent use and the poor colour output — both known to PrintPro — these representations were false. As in Optus v Telstra, the same representations contravene both s 18 and s 29.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, PrintPro has contravened s 29(1)(a) and (g).

Issue 3: Consumer guarantees (ss 54 and 55)

Issue: Has PrintPro breached the consumer guarantees in ss 54 and 55 of the ACL?

Law: Under s 54, goods supplied to a consumer must be of acceptable quality: fit for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied, acceptable in appearance and finish, free from defects, safe and durable, as a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the state and condition of the goods (including hidden defects) would regard as acceptable: Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas Pty Ltd [2015] VCAT 2044; Mitchell v Oz Design Furniture Pty Ltd [2016] VCAT 108. Under s 55, goods must be reasonably fit for any purpose the consumer makes known to the supplier, and any purpose the supplier represents they are fit for, unless the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable to rely, on the supplier's skill or judgment. Any term excluding these guarantees is void (s 64).

Application:

s 54. The PP5000 has a technical design flaw causing paper jams after five minutes of use and produces poor colour. Printing is the very purpose for which printers are commonly supplied, and a printer that jams after five minutes is not fit for that purpose, is not free from defects and is not durable. A reasonable consumer, fully acquainted with this hidden design flaw, would not regard a $50,000 printer with these faults as acceptable. PrintPro has little answer to this.

s 55. Lisa asked Tom specifically about vibrant colour and longevity under heavy use, making known, at least by implication, that she needed it for high-resolution marketing materials and heavy use (s 55(2)(a)). Tom, the Senior Sales Manager, represented it was designed for high-demand environments. Lisa bought it "based upon the advertisement and Tom's assurances", so she relied on PrintPro's skill and judgment, and it was reasonable to rely on a senior manager of the manufacturer. The printer is not fit for that purpose.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, PrintPro has breached ss 54 and 55.

Remedies

Lisa may seek an order rescinding the contract or declaring it void and a refund of the $50,000, restoring the parties to their original positions, or alternatively replacement or compensation. Given the faults are fundamental design flaws that cannot be fixed by simple repair, a refund is the most appropriate remedy. Under s 236 she may recover damages for loss suffered because of the contraventions, such as lost work or the cost of outsourcing printing while the PP5000 was unusable, provided she proves the loss. She may also report PrintPro to the ACCC, which may seek pecuniary penalties for the s 29 contraventions (ss 224–231), compensation orders for other purchasers (ss 237–241), and an adverse publicity order (s 247) given the deliberate release of a product with known faults. PrintPro cannot rely on s 207 (reasonable mistake of fact) or s 208, because its own Senior Sales Manager knew of the faults and did nothing.

Overall conclusion: Lisa is likely to succeed against PrintPro under ss 18, 29(1)(a) and (g), 54 and 55 of the ACL, and is entitled to a refund of $50,000 and damages for any proven consequential business losses.

Model answer 1 — 2025 Semester 1, Question 2: Juan and Evergreen Solar 25 marks

Question text

Evergreen Solar Pty Ltd (ES) is a company that provides premium solar panel installations for residential properties across rural Queensland. One afternoon, Juan, an 85-year-old Spanish immigrant who lives on a remote property, is visited by a sales representative named Jake.

Jake asks Juan about his current electricity costs and whether he has considered switching to solar power. Juan explains that his electricity bills have been steadily rising and his property is often impacted by power outages. He also mentions that, since the recent death of his wife, he is suffering from depression and really struggling with the upkeep of his family home. Jake tells Juan about a special six-year solar package, promising that the system can be installed within a week and that it will "instantly cut his power bills in half" and "provide guaranteed energy independence." Jake hands Juan a 35-page contract, assuring him that it is "risk-free".

Juan informs Jake that he cannot sign the contract because his eyesight is poor, and he still has difficulty understanding English. He says that his stepson (who is currently away) now helps him with all major financial decisions. Jake reassures Juan that this is a "once-in-a-lifetime deal" and insists that he sign immediately to secure a limited-time discount. Trusting Jake's assurances, Juan reluctantly signs the contract and pays a $2,500 deposit.

Unbeknown to Juan, clause 10(a)(ii) of the contract states that the purchaser must pay a $4,000 exit fee if they wish to cancel the contract, whilst clause 22(b)(vi) allows ES to terminate the contract at any time without penalty.

Two weeks later, before installation begins, a devastating bushfire sweeps through the region, destroying power infrastructure and making it impossible for ES to complete the installation. The company informs Juan that it could take over a year before solar installations can resume. However, ES insists that Juan must continue making monthly payments under the contract or pay the $4,000 exit fee if he wishes to terminate the contract (per clause 10(a)(ii)).

Juan comes to you seeking legal advice. Advise Juan on whether the contract should be set aside, considering any relevant legal principles in Contract Law 3. Please use ILAC to answer this question.

Answer plan (5 lines)
  1. Issue 1 — Unconscionable conduct (Amadio, Blomley v Ryan): 85, migrant, poor English, poor eyesight, depression, grief, remote, stepson away; Jake told directly; pressure, 35 pages, "risk-free", one-sided clauses. STRONGEST.
  2. Issue 2 — Misrepresentation: "risk-free" = existing fact, false (exit fee) → fraudulent/reckless (Derry v Peek) or at least innocent (Oscar Chess); "cut bills in half"/"guaranteed" = puff/future. Dismiss undue influence & duress in a line.
  3. Issue 3 — Unilateral mistake / non est factum: good reason not to read; but knew it was a solar contract → weak; Taylor v Johnson arguable.
  4. Issue 4 — Frustration: bushfire (Taylor v Caldwell) — impossible for 1+ year, no fault, not provided for, unjust.
  5. Remedies: rescission, bars checked (2 weeks, no affirmation, deposit returnable), $2,500 back, no $4,000 fee; damages if fraud. Overall conclusion.

Whether Juan can have his contract with Evergreen Solar Pty Ltd (ES) set aside depends on whether his consent was vitiated by unconscionable conduct, misrepresentation or mistake, and whether the contract has in any event been discharged by frustration. Each is considered in turn, followed by remedies.

A contract will only be enforceable against a party who entered into it willingly, with free and voluntary consent. Factors that affect genuine consent are called vitiating factors and include misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence and unconscionable conduct. Where established, the contract may be void, or voidable at the option of the innocent party.

Issue 1: Unconscionable conduct

Issue

Can Juan have the contract with ES set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Although mere harshness of a term is not enough at common law, equity will intervene where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). As Kitto J stated in Blomley v Ryan, this arises whenever one party "is at a special disadvantage in dealing with the other party because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands."

Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, a contract will be set aside where: (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage in relation to the stronger party; (2) the stronger party knew or ought to have known of that disadvantage; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it, so that it was unconscientious to procure the agreement in the circumstances. In Amadio, an elderly Italian migrant couple with limited written English signed a guarantee and mortgage over their property for their son's company's debts, believing it was limited to $50,000 and six months when it was unlimited. The bank manager brought the document to their home, knew of their limited English and the company's financial difficulties, and did not explain the document. The High Court held the bank had acted unconscionably and set the guarantee aside.

Case analysis: give the facts of Amadio in the Law section so you can compare them point by point in Application. Feedback for Tutorial 7 said HD answers needed "meaningful case analysis", not just naming cases.

Application

Special disadvantage. Juan was under a serious special disadvantage. He is 85 years old, an immigrant who "still has difficulty understanding English", has poor eyesight, and is suffering from depression following the recent death of his wife. He lives on a remote property and relies on his stepson, who was away, for all major financial decisions. These are exactly the "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties" described in Blomley v Ryan. Juan's position is very similar to the Amadios, who were elderly migrants with limited English; Juan's situation is arguably worse, as his poor eyesight meant he could not read the 35-page contract at all, and his grief and depression further impaired his ability to protect his own interests.

Knowledge. Jake knew of Juan's disadvantage because Juan told him directly: Juan said he could not sign because his eyesight was poor and he had difficulty with English, mentioned his depression and his wife's death, and explained that his stepson helped him with financial decisions. Like the bank manager in Amadio, who knew of the Amadios' limited English, Jake had actual knowledge, which is the strongest form of this element.

Unfair advantage. Jake took unfair advantage of Juan's disadvantage. Rather than allowing Juan to wait for his stepson, Jake told him it was a "once-in-a-lifetime deal" and "insisted that he sign immediately" for a limited-time discount, and assured him the 35-page contract was "risk-free". Similar to the bank manager in Amadio, who did not explain the document or suggest independent advice, Jake did not explain any of the terms. The contract is also one-sided: clause 10(a)(ii) imposes a $4,000 exit fee on Juan, while clause 22(b)(vi) allows ES to terminate at any time without penalty. ES may argue that Juan is an adult who chose to sign and that the price itself was fair. However, the equitable doctrine in Amadio looks at how the agreement was procured, and Jake's pressure, after being told Juan could not read the contract and wanted his stepson's help, was clearly unconscientious.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Juan can have the contract set aside for unconscionable conduct, as all three elements in Amadio are satisfied.

Issue 2: Misrepresentation

Issue

Can Juan rescind the contract with ES on the basis of misrepresentation by Jake?

Law

A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact made by one party to another which induces that party to enter the contract, making the contract voidable. It must be: (1) a false statement of fact by the representor to the representee; (2) concerning an existing fact or past event, not future intention; (3) made before or at the time the contract is concluded; and (4) intended to induce and in fact inducing the contract. Opinions, puffs, promises and statements of future intention are not misrepresentations.

A misrepresentation is fraudulent if made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly (Derry v Peek). In that case, company directors stated in a prospectus that the company had the right to use steam trams when government consent was still needed; because the directors honestly believed the statement, it was not fraudulent. A negligent misrepresentation is an honest but careless statement made where there is a special relationship giving rise to a duty of care, about a serious or business matter, which the maker knows will be relied on, where reliance is reasonable and damage results. An innocent misrepresentation is made without intention to deceive (Oscar Chess Ltd v Williams, where a seller honestly described a car as a 1948 model based on a false log book). Rescission is available for all types, but damages only for fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation.

Application

Statement of existing fact. Jake made three statements. The statements that the system would "instantly cut his power bills in half" and "provide guaranteed energy independence" relate to future results and are likely to be characterised as puffs or promises rather than statements of existing fact. Juan could argue that the word "guaranteed" turns this into an assertion of fact about what the system is capable of, but this is the weaker argument. By contrast, the assurance that the contract was "risk-free" is a statement about an existing fact — the content of the contract Jake was handing to Juan.

Falsity. The "risk-free" statement was false: clause 10(a)(ii) imposes a $4,000 exit fee on Juan, while ES may terminate without penalty. At the very least, Jake's silence about this clause turned his assurance into a misleading half-truth.

Timing and inducement. The statement was made before Juan signed, and was intended to and did induce him to sign — Juan was initially unwilling, but signed "trusting Jake's assurances".

Type. As ES's sales representative handing over ES's own standard contract, Jake either knew of the exit fee or was reckless as to whether his statement was true. Unlike the directors in Derry v Peek, who genuinely believed their statement, it is difficult to see how Jake could honestly believe a contract with a $4,000 exit fee was "risk-free". The misrepresentation is therefore likely fraudulent. If Jake honestly did not know of the clause, it would be at least negligent, given he was in a position of specialist knowledge about a business transaction and knew Juan was relying on him, or alternatively innocent, as in Oscar Chess.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Jake's "risk-free" assurance was a fraudulent, or at least negligent, misrepresentation, entitling Juan to rescind the contract and claim damages.

Issue 3: Unilateral mistake and non est factum

Issue

Is Juan's contract with ES void for unilateral mistake or non est factum?

Law

Generally, a mistake by one party does not make a contract unenforceable (caveat emptor). A unilateral mistake will only make a contract void or unenforceable where the party made a serious mistake about a fundamental aspect of the contract and the other party knew or ought to have known of the mistake and sought to take advantage of it (Taylor v Johnson, where a buyer who knew the seller mistakenly believed the price was per acre rather than in total had the contract set aside against him). Non est factum, a limited form of unilateral mistake, may make a contract void where a person had a good reason for not reading it, such as being blind or unable to read English, and the mistake is about the fundamental nature of the document.

Application

Juan had a good reason for not reading the contract: his poor eyesight and difficulty understanding English, both of which he told Jake about. However, non est factum requires a mistake about the fundamental nature of the document. Juan knew he was signing a contract for a solar package; his mistake was about a particular term, the exit fee. Non est factum is therefore unlikely to succeed. Juan has a better argument for unilateral mistake: he believed the contract carried no risk, Jake knew Juan could not read it, and similar to the buyer in Taylor v Johnson, Jake took advantage of that mistake by urging him to sign immediately. ES would argue that a mistake about an exit fee is not fundamental to a solar installation contract.

Conclusion

Non est factum is unlikely to apply, and unilateral mistake is arguable but weaker than Juan's claims for unconscionable conduct and misrepresentation.

Issue 4: Frustration

Issue

Has Juan's contract with ES been discharged by frustration as a result of the bushfire?

Law

A contract may be discharged by frustration where an event that is not the fault of either party makes performance impossible, or only possible in a way neither party anticipated; it must be substantially different, not merely more difficult or expensive. A contract is frustrated if: (1) a supervening event has made performance impossible or impossible in the way originally envisaged; (2) neither party caused it; (3) the contract did not provide for it; and (4) it would be unjust to compel the parties to proceed. In Taylor v Caldwell, a music hall hired for concerts burnt down before the concerts took place, and the contract was frustrated.

Application

The bushfire is a supervening natural disaster, like the fire in Taylor v Caldwell, which destroyed power infrastructure and made installation impossible. Neither Juan nor ES caused it. ES may argue that performance is only delayed rather than impossible. However, the system was promised "within a week"; a delay of over a year in a six-year package, while Juan makes monthly payments for nothing, is substantially different from what the parties envisaged. Clause 10(a)(ii) deals with the purchaser choosing to cancel, not with a natural disaster preventing performance, so the contract does not appear to provide for this event (it is assumed there is no force majeure clause, as none is mentioned). It would be unjust to compel Juan to keep paying or pay a $4,000 exit fee for a service ES cannot provide.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the contract has been frustrated, releasing Juan from his future obligations, including the monthly payments and the exit fee.

Remedies

The primary remedy for a vitiating factor is rescission, an equitable remedy by which the court declares the contract terminated and, as far as possible, restores the parties to their original positions. The right to rescind is lost if restitution is impossible, the contract is affirmed, there is delay, third party rights intervene, the contract is executed, or the party seeking relief has acted unconscientiously. Damages are available for fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation but not innocent misrepresentation, and the plaintiff must mitigate their loss (Payzu Ltd v Saunders).

None of the bars apply. Juan has acted within two weeks, unlike the buyer in Leaf v International Galleries who waited five years; he has not affirmed the contract (the system was never installed); restitution is simple because only a $2,500 deposit has been paid; no third party rights are involved; the contract has not been executed; and Juan has not acted unconscientiously. The court would rescind the contract, order ES to refund the $2,500 deposit, and Juan would not be liable for the $4,000 exit fee or further monthly payments. If the misrepresentation is fraudulent or negligent, Juan may also claim damages for any further loss, subject to his duty to mitigate.

Overall conclusion

Juan should be advised that the contract is likely to be set aside, primarily for unconscionable conduct and also for Jake's misrepresentation that it was "risk-free", entitling him to rescission and a refund of his $2,500 deposit; in any event, the bushfire has likely frustrated the contract, releasing him from further payments and the exit fee.

Issue 1: Undue influence

Issue

Can Jeffrey and Max have David's transfer of the apartment to Eveline set aside on the basis of undue influence?

Law

Undue influence occurs where a party has a degree of controlling influence and power over another person and dominates that person's will — "every case where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". A transaction is voidable at the election of the weaker party where: (1) the parties are in a pre-existing relationship; (2) one party has a controlling influence over the other; (3) the stronger party takes advantage of that influence for their own benefit; and (4) the weaker party is not exercising independent judgement, so there is no genuine free and voluntary consent.

Certain recognised relationships automatically raise a presumption of undue influence, such as parent and child, doctor and patient, lawyer and client, and religious adviser and disciple (Allcard v Skinner). In other relationships, the party alleging undue influence must show that the other party exercised a dominance or controlling influence over them so that they did not act freely and voluntarily. Courts have recognised that such influence may arise in relationships including spouses, employer and employee, and carer and patient (Johnson v Buttress). Once this is shown, the onus shifts to the stronger party to prove that the transaction was the "pure voluntary and well-understood act" of the weaker party.

In Johnson v Buttress, Mr Buttress, an elderly widower who was illiterate and of limited intelligence, became dependent on Mrs Johnson after his wife's death and transferred his only significant asset, his home, to her without proper independent advice. The High Court found a relationship of influence and dependence, held that Mrs Johnson had not shown the transfer was his free and well-understood act, and set the transfer aside.

Application

Pre-existing relationship. David and Eveline were in a pre-existing relationship of carer and patient: David lives with Eveline, who is his carer, and she was also a friend of his deceased wife. This is not one of the automatically presumed relationships in Allcard v Skinner, so Jeffrey and Max must show dominance. However, carer and patient is a relationship in which the courts have recognised controlling influence may arise, as in Johnson v Buttress.

Controlling influence. Eveline had a controlling influence over David. David is 89, a widowed disability pensioner with a limited ability to read, write and speak English, who suffers from depression and is "dependent upon Eveline to look after him". Eveline manages some of his personal and financial affairs. Very similar to Mr Buttress, who became dependent on Mrs Johnson after his wife died, David became reliant on Eveline, a friend of his late wife, after he was widowed. Eveline also "convinces" David to revoke his will and transfer the apartment, showing her influence over his decisions.

Issue 2: Unconscionable conduct

Issue

Can Jeffrey and Max have the transfer of the apartment to Eveline set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Equity will intervene where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger), that is, where one party is at a special disadvantage "because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands" (Kitto J, Blomley v Ryan). Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, the transaction will be set aside where: (1) the weaker party was under a special disadvantage in relation to the stronger party; (2) the stronger party knew or ought to have known of it; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it. In Amadio, elderly Italian migrants with limited written English signed an unlimited guarantee and mortgage for their son's company debts, believing it was limited; the bank manager knew of their poor English and the company's difficulties but did not explain the document, and the High Court set the guarantee aside.

Application

Special disadvantage. David was at a special disadvantage: he is 89, a disability pensioner, suffers from depression, has limited ability to read, write and speak English, and depends on Eveline for his care. Similar to the Amadios, who were elderly migrants with limited English, David could not properly understand the documents he signed, and his depression is the kind of "illness… impaired faculties" referred to in Blomley v Ryan.

Knowledge. Eveline knew of David's disadvantage. She lives with him, is his carer, and manages his personal and financial affairs. She is in a far better position to know of his weakness than the bank manager in Amadio, who was still held to know of the Amadios' limited English.

Unfair advantage. Eveline took unfair advantage of David's position. She asked for the apartment herself, convinced him to revoke his will and transfer it immediately, and arranged a solicitor of her own choice. As in Amadio, where the bank did not ensure the Amadios understood the transaction, Eveline did nothing to ensure David understood its effect or obtained independent advice, and the transaction was entirely for her benefit. Eveline may argue that the transfer was a genuine gift arising out of their friendship, but given David did not fully understand his decision, it was unconscientious for Eveline to procure it.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the transfer can also be set aside for unconscionable conduct, as each element in Amadio is satisfied.

Remedies

The primary remedy is rescission, an equitable remedy by which the court sets the transaction aside and, as far as possible, restores the parties to their original positions. The right to rescind is lost if restitution is impossible, the transaction has been affirmed, there has been delay, third party rights have intervened, or the party seeking relief has acted unconscientiously (Allcard v Skinner, where the claim failed due to delay).

None of these bars appear to apply. Jeffrey and Max acted as soon as the will was read, so unlike in Allcard v Skinner there is no delay. David did not affirm the transfer, as he died soon after. Restitution is possible because Eveline still holds the apartment, and it is assumed she has not sold it to a third party; if she has, rescission may be barred by third party rights. Jeffrey and Max have not acted unconscientiously. The court would therefore order that the apartment be transferred back to David's estate, to be dealt with according to law (the effect of the revocation of the will on whether Jeffrey and Max inherit is a matter of succession law outside this question). Damages are not relevant, as there is no misrepresentation.

Model answer 1 — 2025 Semester 2, Question 2 (StageCorp v CT Events) 25 marks

Question text (click)

In mid-2022, Callum and Tessa launched an event planning business in Sydney called CT Events, specialising in small corporate functions and private celebrations with fewer than 100 guests. To minimise costs, they did not buy equipment but instead arranged to hire equipment from a hire company. They created a website and developed a growing social media presence under the CT Events name, and the business steadily expanded.

In early 2023, Callum and Tessa invited their friend, Robert, to join the business. Robert brought considerable experience in design and business management. Although the business continued operating as CT Events and no formal agreement was made, Callum and Tessa began introducing Robert to clients as part of the team, and the promotional materials were updated to include him.

They all began working out of a shared office and managed finances through a joint account. Profits from business projects were split equally, although each person occasionally pursued individual freelance work. CT Events’s business decisions were made informally, with disagreements resolved by majority vote.

In August 2024, while Callum and Tessa were working at an event, Robert signed a $180,000 contract with StageCorp Pty Ltd to purchase a custom-built stage. He paid an $18,000 deposit from the joint account and signed the agreement on behalf of CT Events. Robert told StageCorp’s representative that his long-term goal was to expand the business into large-scale concerts and festivals, and that owning stage equipment would help them remain competitive – provided he could convince Callum and Tessa to support the idea.

Two months later, the stage was delivered to the office. Callum and Tessa, outraged by the purchase, informed StageCorp that CT Events would not be paying for it, stating that the stage would be returned. They argued Robert had no authority to make the purchase on behalf of the business.

Answer plan (5 lines)
  1. Issue 1 Partnership? 4 reqs — 3 persons; business since 2022 (Khan v Miah for Robert); in common (joint account, equal profits, majority vote, “part of the team”) vs freelance work (Degiorgio); profit → YES.
  2. Issue 2 Authority? Express — none. Implied — hire-only + majority vote override (Construction v Hexyl) → no.
  3. Apparent s 8 — kind of business? (small events vs concerts: Mercantile v Garrod) usual way? ($180k purchase when they only hire: Goldberg) believed partner ✔ (holding out); no notice ✘ (“provided he could convince Callum and Tessa”: Crouch) → NO.
  4. Issue 3 Robert personally liable — breach of warranty of authority (Oliana Foods) → likely YES.
  5. Remedies: primary claim vs Robert; alternative vs all three partners jointly (Kendall v Hamilton) if a court finds apparent authority.

Whether StageCorp Pty Ltd (‘StageCorp’) can recover the remaining $162,000 depends upon three issues: first, whether Callum, Tessa and Robert carry on CT Events in partnership; second, whether Robert had authority to bind CT Events and its partners to the stage contract; and third, if he did not, whether Robert is personally liable to StageCorp. As CT Events operates in Sydney, the New South Wales partnership legislation would technically apply; this answer applies the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) (‘PA’), on the assumption that the relevant provisions are equivalent.

Issue 1: Partnership

Issue: Were Callum, Tessa and Robert carrying on CT Events in partnership under the PA, such that each was the agent of the others?

Law: A partnership is “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit”: PA s 5(1). No registration or formalities are required, and the partnership agreement may be written, oral or wholly implied from the parties’ conduct. A partnership exists whenever the four requirements are satisfied, regardless of the parties’ intention. Each partner is both a principal and an agent of the others, and each has unlimited personal liability for the firm’s debts: Kendall v Hamilton (1879).

First, there must be at least two persons, and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115. Second, they must be carrying on a business, which includes a trade, occupation or profession (PA s 3) and requires some continuity or repetition of activity; a one-off project is usually not a partnership: Smith v Anderson (1880). A partnership begins from the date the parties begin working together in an identifiable business: Khan v Miah [2000]. Third, the business must be carried on in common, meaning each acts on behalf of the others as well as themselves. An employer and employee are not partners, because the employer does not act on the employee’s behalf: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Fourth, the business must be carried on with a view of profit, even if a loss is in fact made: Minter v Minter (2000).

Application:

Persons. There are three persons — Callum, Tessa and Robert — well within the limit of 20. This requirement is satisfied.

Carrying on a business. CT Events has operated as an event planning business since mid-2022, with a website, a growing social media presence and a steadily expanding client base. Unlike Smith v Anderson, this is a continuing trade, not a single project. Robert joined in early 2023 and began working out of the shared office; consistent with Khan v Miah, he became a partner from the time he began working in the business, despite no formal agreement being made.

In common. The three work from a shared office, manage finances through a joint account, split profits equally and resolve disagreements by majority vote. Callum and Tessa introduced Robert to clients “as part of the team” and updated promotional materials to include him. These facts show each acting on behalf of the others, not one acting for an employer. Callum and Tessa may argue that Robert was merely brought in for his design and management experience, and that each person’s individual freelance work shows they were not truly in business together. However, unlike the employee in Degiorgio v Dunn, Robert receives an equal share of profits, has access to the joint account and has an equal vote — none of which is consistent with employment. Occasional freelance work does not change the character of the CT Events business itself (although it may raise a separate issue of competing with the firm under s 33 PA, which is not relevant to StageCorp).

View of profit. Profits from projects are split equally, so the business is clearly carried on with a view of profit (Minter v Minter).

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Callum, Tessa and Robert are partners in CT Events under s 5(1) PA, so Robert is an agent of the firm and of Callum and Tessa.

Issue 2: Robert’s authority to purchase the stage

Issue: Are CT Events and its partners bound by the $180,000 contract Robert signed with StageCorp, on the basis that Robert had authority under the law of agency?

Law: Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. Where the agent acts within their authority, the contract is between the principal and the third party. Authority may be actual (express or implied) or apparent.

Actual authority is “a legal relationship between principal and agent created by a consensual agreement to which they alone are parties”: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express authority is given in writing or orally. Partners have implied authority to do the usual things necessary to carry on the firm’s business, such as buying goods of the kind usually employed in that business and incurring debts; but implied authority may be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985). Implied authority will not always be found: Peterson v Moloney (1951).

Apparent authority. Under s 8 PA, every partner is an agent of the firm, and acts “for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm” bind the firm and the partners, unless the partner had no authority and the third party knew this, or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Accordingly, where a partner lacks actual authority, the firm is still bound if: (1) the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm, judged by what the firm’s usual business actually is and what transactions such a firm would usually engage in: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) the business was carried on in the usual way, because otherwise the third party should suspect a lack of authority: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person to be a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect that the partner lacked authority, relying in good faith and without notice: Crouch v IPG (2013). This reflects the general rule that apparent authority depends on the principal holding the agent out, not on the agent’s own claims: Essington v Regency (2004).

Notice: s 8 gives FOUR requirements for partners — use them (not just the three general agency elements) when it is a partnership question.

Application:

Express actual authority. There is no evidence that Callum or Tessa expressly authorised Robert to buy a stage. The purchase was made while they were working at an event, and they were “outraged” on learning of it. Robert himself acknowledged he still needed to “convince Callum and Tessa”. There was no express authority.

Implied actual authority. StageCorp will argue that buying event equipment is a usual thing necessary to carry on an event planning business. However, CT Events deliberately did not buy equipment — it hired it to minimise costs — and decisions were made by majority vote. That established practice operates, as in Construction v Hexyl, to override any implied authority for one partner alone to commit the firm to a $180,000 purchase. As in Peterson v Moloney, implied authority is unlikely to be found.

Apparent authority — business of the kind carried on by the firm. StageCorp will argue that a stage is event equipment and CT Events is an events business. However, applying Mercantile v Garrod, the court looks at what CT Events’ business actually is: small corporate functions and private celebrations of fewer than 100 guests. A custom-built stage was, on Robert’s own words, for expanding into “large-scale concerts and festivals” — a different kind of business. This requirement is probably not met.

Usual way. Even if the stage were within the firm’s kind of business, CT Events’ usual way was to hire equipment, not buy it, and a $180,000 custom-built purchase by one partner is out of proportion to a small events business. As in Goldberg v Jenkins, a transaction not carried out in the usual way should have caused StageCorp to suspect a lack of authority.

Knew or believed Robert to be a partner. This requirement is satisfied. Robert signed “on behalf of CT Events” and paid the deposit from the joint account, and Callum and Tessa themselves held Robert out by introducing him to clients as part of the team and including him in promotional materials. Unlike Essington v Regency, the holding out came from the principals, not merely from Robert.

No knowledge or suspicion of lack of authority. This is StageCorp’s greatest difficulty. Robert told StageCorp’s representative that the purchase was part of his own “long-term goal” and would help — “provided he could convince Callum and Tessa to support the idea”. StageCorp was therefore on notice that Callum and Tessa had not yet agreed, and that Robert needed their approval. StageCorp did not rely in good faith and without notice (Crouch v IPG); a reasonable supplier would have sought confirmation from the other partners before accepting a $180,000 order.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Robert had no actual or apparent authority, so CT Events and Callum and Tessa are not bound by the stage contract.

Issue 3: Robert’s personal liability

Issue: Can StageCorp hold Robert personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

Law: Where an agent lacks authority, the agent is personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed to make the contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent did not in fact have authority; and (3) the third party relied on the representation and would not have contracted without it: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]. The agent may also be liable in deceit, negligence or under s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law.

Application: First, Robert signed the agreement “on behalf of CT Events” and paid the deposit from the firm’s joint account, representing that he was contracting for the firm. Second, as concluded in Issue 2, he had no authority. Third, StageCorp would not have built a $180,000 custom stage for Robert personally; it contracted because it believed an established business stood behind the order. Robert may argue that StageCorp cannot have relied on any claim of authority, because he disclosed that he still needed his partners’ support. However, by nonetheless signing a binding contract in the firm’s name and paying a deposit from the firm’s account, Robert represented that the firm was committed, and StageCorp proceeded on that basis.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Robert is personally liable to StageCorp for breach of warranty of authority.

Remedies and advice

StageCorp’s best course is to claim damages against Robert personally for breach of warranty of authority, for the loss caused by having no enforceable contract with CT Events — up to the $162,000 balance, less what StageCorp can recover by taking back and reselling the stage, which Callum and Tessa have offered to return. In the alternative, StageCorp may argue that Robert had apparent authority under s 8 PA. If a court accepted that argument, the firm would be bound and Callum, Tessa and Robert would be jointly and personally liable for the $162,000 (Kendall v Hamilton), as a partnership is not a separate legal entity, and StageCorp could recover from their personal assets. Given Robert’s statement that he still needed to convince his partners, this alternative is weaker. Separately, Callum and Tessa may pursue Robert for breach of his fiduciary duties to the partnership (for example, failing to disclose matters affecting the firm under s 31 PA) to recover the $18,000 deposit taken from the joint account, but that is a matter between the partners.

Kevin and Maxine’s words (“employee”, “company card”) are bait. Testing the partnership requirements is how you neutralise “employee”.

Issue 1: Partnership

Issue: Are Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne partners in Pop Rocks under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) (‘PA’), such that Roxanne is an agent of the others?

Law: A partnership is “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit”: PA s 5(1). It exists whenever four requirements are met, regardless of formality or intention, and each partner is both principal and agent of the others (PA s 8), with unlimited personal liability (Kendall v Hamilton (1879)). The requirements are: (1) at least two persons (and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115); (2) carrying on a business — a trade, occupation or profession (s 3) with continuity, not a one-off transaction (Smith v Anderson (1880)); (3) in common — each acting for the others as well as themselves, which distinguishes partners from an employer and employee (Degiorgio v Dunn (2004)); and (4) with a view of profit (Minter v Minter (2000)).

Application: There are three persons. Pop Rocks has performed in major cities across Australia for two years, generating $800,000 in profit — a continuing trade, unlike the one-off venture in Smith v Anderson. The profit requirement is plainly satisfied.

The key dispute is whether Roxanne is acting “in common” or is, as Kevin and Maxine claim, their employee. In support of their claim, Roxanne is paid a $50,000 salary and holds the role of marketing manager, which resembles employment as in Degiorgio v Dunn. However, Roxanne receives the same 20% profit share as Maxine and Kevin, and has the same right to spend from the shared account. These are the rights of a co-owner acting on behalf of all members, not an employee. Her salary is expressly paid only to compensate her “extra responsibilities”, which does not change her status as a band member.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne are partners, so Roxanne is an agent of the firm and of Maxine and Kevin.

Issue 2: The electronic drum set

Issue: Are Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne (the firm) liable to Bob for the $36,000 drum set, on the basis that Roxanne had authority to purchase it under the law of agency?

Law: Agency is a legal relationship in which an agent is authorised to act on behalf of a principal in dealings with a third party. If the agent acts within their authority, the contract binds the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability. Authority may be actual or apparent.

Actual authority is created by consensual agreement between principal and agent: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. It may be express (written or oral) or implied. A partner has implied authority to do the usual things needed to carry on the firm’s business, including buying goods of the kind usually employed in that business; however, implied authority can be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985).

Apparent authority arises where the principal leads a third party to reasonably believe the agent has authority, by appointing the agent to a position or allowing them to act in the past; the principal is bound even if it expressly told the agent not to act. In Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), a company secretary hired cars in the company’s name without actual authority, partly for personal use; the company was bound because such contracts were within the secretary’s usual, and therefore apparent, authority. For partners, s 8 PA provides that the firm is bound where the partner: (1) carries on business of the kind carried on by the firm (Mercantile v Garrod (1962)); (2) in the usual way (Goldberg v Jenkins (1889)); (3) the third party knew or believed them to be a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of authority, relying in good faith without notice (Crouch v IPG (2013)). The partnership agreement binds only the partners, not third parties.

Application:

Express authority. The members agreed that each could spend up to $50,000 on upgrading musical equipment, with the permission of at least one other member. The drum set is musical equipment and its $36,000 price is within the limit. However, there is no evidence that Roxanne obtained Maxine’s or Kevin’s permission — both were furious to learn of the purchase. Roxanne therefore acted outside her express authority.

Implied authority. Buying band equipment is a usual part of carrying on a band’s business. However, as in Construction v Hexyl, the members’ express agreement requiring permission overrides any wider implied authority. Roxanne lacked actual authority.

Apparent authority. First, a drum set is exactly the type of goods a touring band uses, and was bought to upgrade equipment for the band’s Christmas performances; it is business of the kind carried on by the firm. Second, it was carried out in the usual way: Roxanne “regularly buys band equipment” from Bob “on behalf of the band”, paid on the card linked to the band’s shared account, and had the drum set sent to the “normal address”, the band’s recording studio. Third, Bob knew Roxanne as a member of the band who bought on its behalf; Maxine and Kevin held her out as having authority by allowing these purchases in the past. Similar to Panorama, where the secretary’s position made the car hire look normal, Roxanne’s established role made this purchase appear authorised. Fourth, there is no evidence that Bob knew of the internal permission requirement, which binds only the members. Maxine and Kevin may argue that the 20% discount should have made Bob suspicious; however, the discount was explained by Bob’s regular dealings with the band and his being a fan, and does not suggest any lack of authority.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Roxanne had apparent authority, so Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne are liable for the drum set and are not entitled to a refund.

Issue 3: The limited-edition painting

Issue: Are Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne liable to Bob for the $10,000 painting, on the basis that Roxanne had authority to purchase it under the law of agency?

Law: The law set out in Issue 2 applies. In addition, apparent authority requires a representation by the principal; an agent cannot create their own authority: Essington v Regency (2004), where an agent had no apparent authority to vary a contract because the principal had done nothing to represent that the agent could do so.

Application: Roxanne had no express authority, because a painting is not “musical equipment” and no permission was obtained; nor any implied authority, because buying artwork is not a usual thing needed to carry on a band’s business. As to apparent authority, first, a signed painting of Roxanne’s “favourite musician” is a personal item, not a transaction a band would usually engage in (Mercantile v Garrod). Second, it was not done in the usual way: Roxanne asked Bob to send it to her home address rather than the band’s studio, and as in Goldberg v Jenkins, this departure should have made Bob suspect a lack of authority. Third, while Maxine and Kevin had held Roxanne out as able to buy band equipment, they had never represented that she could buy art; as in Essington v Regency, Roxanne’s own instruction to “put it on the same card” cannot create authority. Bob may argue he reasonably assumed any purchase on the band’s card was authorised, but given the personal nature of the item and the home delivery, that reliance is not reasonable.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Roxanne had no authority to buy the painting, so Maxine and Kevin are not liable for it.

Issue 4: Roxanne’s personal liability for the painting

Issue: Can Bob hold Roxanne personally liable for the painting?

Law: An agent who lacks authority is personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed to contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent had no authority; and (3) the third party relied on that representation and would not otherwise have contracted: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020].

Application: By telling Bob to put the painting “on the same card” — the band’s card — Roxanne represented that the band was paying. She had no authority (Issue 3), and Bob, who discounted the item because of his relationship with the band, accepted payment on the basis that the band’s card would honour it. Alternatively, if Roxanne is treated as having bought the painting for herself, she is simply the buyer and personally liable under the contract of sale.

Conclusion: Either way, Roxanne is personally liable to Bob for the $10,000.

Remedies

Bob may retain the $36,000 for the drum set; the firm is bound, and Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne are jointly and personally liable for it (Kendall v Hamilton). As to the painting, Bob may need to refund the $10,000 charged to the band’s account, but he may recover $10,000 (or damages for breach of warranty of authority) from Roxanne personally, or seek the return of the painting. Bob should also be advised that Maxine and Kevin may separately pursue Roxanne for breach of her fiduciary duty as a partner not to use her position for private profit and to account for it (Birtchnell v Equity (1929); s 32 PA) — but that is a matter between the partners.

  2. Apparent: Patrizia didn’t know of $1m limit ✔; Cristina held Davo out (appointed him, her sign directs all enquiries to him) ✔ — but Davo’s own claim can’t count (Essington); reliance reasonable ✔ (Panorama) → contract binds Cristina.

  4. Conclusion: no rescission, no claim against Patrizia; damages from Davo (≥$150,000).

Cristina’s position depends upon two issues: first, whether Davo had authority to bind Cristina to the sale to Patrizia; and second, whether Davo breached the fiduciary duties he owed to Cristina.

Issue 1: Davo’s authority

Issue: Did Davo have authority to act on behalf of his principal, Cristina, when accepting Patrizia’s $850,000 offer, so that Cristina is bound by the contract?

Law: Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. The relationship gives rise to obligations on both principal and agent which are both contractual and fiduciary in nature. An agent may be authorised by actual authority — express or implied — or by apparent authority. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability.

Express actual authority is authority expressly given by the principal to the agent, in writing (including by deed, such as a power of attorney) or orally; actual authority arises from a consensual agreement to which principal and agent alone are parties: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Implied actual authority is the authority to do all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the express instructions, or needed to give them business efficacy; for example, a real estate agent appointed to sell a house has implied authority to give buyers information about it. Implied authority is not found merely because it would be convenient: Peterson v Moloney (1951).

Apparent authority: if the principal allows a third party to believe the agent has authority, the principal will be bound to honour the agent’s dealings with the third party even if the agent was not actually authorised, and even if the principal told the agent not to act that way. The three requirements are that: (1) the third party did not know the agent lacked actual authority; (2) the principal held the agent out as having authority, for example by appointing them to a particular position; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on the principal’s representation. In Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), a company secretary without actual authority hired cars in the company’s name; the company was bound because the contracts fell within the usual authority of the secretary’s position. However, the representation must come from the principal: an agent cannot create their own authority by claiming it: Essington v Regency (2004).

Application: A relationship of principal and agent exists: Cristina is the principal, Davo is the agent and Patrizia is the third party.

Actual authority. Cristina gave Davo express authority, in writing, to sell the house to the first buyer offering over $1 million, and to put up a “For Sale” sign directing enquiries to him. Davo also had implied authority to do things necessarily incidental to that task, such as answering enquiries, showing Patrizia through the house and providing information about it. However, in accepting $850,000, Davo acted outside his actual authority, which was expressly confined to offers over $1 million.

Apparent authority. As was the case in Panorama, where the company was bound by contracts within the usual authority of the position it had given the secretary, Patrizia would argue that Davo had apparent authority. First, Patrizia did not know of the $1 million limit — Davo said nothing to her about it. Second, Cristina herself held Davo out as her agent with authority to deal with buyers: she appointed him as her selling agent and instructed him to erect a sign at her property stating “All enquiries to David Barnes”. Cristina will rely on Essington v Regency to argue that Davo’s statement that he was “authorized by the owner to accept any offer that I determine to be reasonable” came only from the agent and cannot create authority. That is correct as far as it goes, but Patrizia does not rely on Davo’s words alone; Cristina’s own appointment of Davo and her sign are representations by the principal. Third, Patrizia, a first home buyer, saw the sign, contacted the agency it named and dealt with the agent Cristina had appointed; her reliance was reasonable.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Davo had apparent authority to act on behalf of Cristina, so Patrizia can enforce the contract against Cristina and the contract will not be rescinded.

Issue 2: Davo’s fiduciary duties

Issue: Did Davo breach the fiduciary duties he owed to Cristina?

Law: Agents owe fiduciary duties to their principal as a result of the relationship of trust and confidence between them. These include the duty to follow the principal’s lawful instructions, a breach of which makes the agent personally liable to the principal for the resulting harm (Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830)); to act in the principal’s interests and not put the agent’s interests in conflict with them (Hewson v Sydney (1968)); to exercise due care, skill and diligence, failing to do what a reasonable agent would do in the circumstances (Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991)); and to communicate relevant information to the principal (John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002)). These duties may also be imposed as contractual duties under the agency agreement.

Application: It is likely that Davo breached his fiduciary duties to Cristina because: first, he disobeyed her lawful, written instruction to sell only for over $1 million, as in Bertram Armstrong v Godfray; second, accepting $850,000 for a house valued at around $1.2 million was contrary to Cristina’s interests, and is not what a reasonable agent exercising due care and skill would do; and third, he did not communicate the below-threshold offer to Cristina before accepting it — although she was overseas, there is no suggestion she could not be contacted. Since the instructions were in writing, this is also a breach of the terms of the contract of agency, which amounts to a breach of contract by Davo.

Conclusion: Davo has breached his fiduciary duties and his contract of agency with Cristina.

Remedies

On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely decide that Patrizia can enforce the contract under apparent authority, so Cristina cannot rescind it, and she has no claim against Patrizia, an innocent third party who acted in good faith. However, Cristina is entitled to damages from Davo for the loss caused by his breaches — at least the $150,000 difference between the sale price and her $1 million minimum (and arguably more, given the $1.2 million valuation). Davo has no right to remuneration for the sale unless the agency agreement provides for it, and he cannot claim an indemnity for acting outside Cristina’s instructions.

Model answer 1 · 2024 Semester 2 Q2: GlobalCorp v Technovate (Contract Law 2) 25 marks

Question text

After six months of negotiations, a software development company, Technovate, entered into a written contract with GlobalCorp, to create a customised software program.

One week before the contract was signed, the head of Technovate's technology department, Alex, assured GlobalCorp that the software would be capable of handling at least 10,000 concurrent users based on similar projects completed for other clients. This assurance provided GlobalCorp with confidence in the software's scalability.

Damon, a junior sales representative had also mentioned at the beginning of the negotiation period, that the software would "likely enhance productivity", but did not provide any research or statistics to support his claims. Damon also claimed that the software was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise GlobalCorp's business operations".

After buying and installing the software, GlobalCorp discovered that it could only handle 5,000 concurrent users, significantly less than what was promised by Alex. This was not suitable for GlobalCorp's business operations and GlobalCorp experienced significant challenges and financial losses amounting to $250,000. Furthermore, contrary to Damon's claims, the software did not lead to any measurable increase in productivity and did not revolutionise GlobalCorp's business operations.

GlobalCorp comes to you seeking legal advice about the enforceability of the statements made by Alex and Damon. Upon reviewing the written contract, it appears to lack detail and is very poorly written. Answer plan (5 lines)

  2. Issue 1, Alex: term or representation (5 factors) → PER (Mercantile Bank) → exception 1 (not wholly in writing) → exception 2 (collateral contract, De Lassalle, 4 elements).

The available remedies are then considered.

Issue 1: Alex's assurance about 10,000 concurrent users

Issue

Is Alex's assurance that the software could handle at least 10,000 concurrent users an enforceable term of the contract between GlobalCorp and Technovate?

Law

Terms and representations. Once a contract is formed, Contract Law 2 identifies the content of the bargain. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect, so its breach is a breach of contract. A representation is a statement made in connection with the contract, which may be true or false, but which does not form part of the contract and has no promissory effect. To decide which a statement is, the court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended about its contractual nature, having regard to: (1) the language used, since strong statements such as "I assure you" are more likely to be terms; (2) the context in which it was made; (3) the time it was made, since a statement immediately preceding the contract is more likely to be a term; (4) the maker of the statement and their relative knowledge and expertise; and (5) the importance of the statement, including whether the other party obviously relied on it. The lecture examples show that a precise, measurable claim, such as "this machine can process 500 units per hour", is more likely to be a term.

Parol evidence rule. Where a contract has been reduced to writing and appears to be entire, it is presumed that the writing contains all of its terms, and evidence of any previous or contemporaneous oral agreement will not be admitted to add to or vary it: Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor. The rule only applies where the written contract appears to be entire. There are two main exceptions. First, where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, oral evidence may be used to establish oral terms. Second, the oral statement may form a collateral contract.

Collateral contract. A collateral contract is a separate, pre-existing contract formed by a promise that induces the other party to enter into the main contract. The elements are: (1) an oral statement and a main written contract; (2) the oral statement was intended as a promise, made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) the collateral contract is not inconsistent with the main written contract; and (4) entry into the main contract is the consideration for the promise: De Lassalle v Guildford. In that case, before the tenant handed over the signed lease, the landlord orally assured him that the drains were in good order. The lease said nothing about the drains, which proved defective. The assurance was enforced as a collateral contract. Because it is the collateral contract that is breached, the innocent party cannot terminate the main contract but may claim damages.

Case analysis in the Law section (facts + outcome of De Lassalle) is what moves an answer into the 6–7 band. Then compare it in Application.

Application

Term or representation. Language: Alex "assured" GlobalCorp that the software would handle "at least 10,000 concurrent users". This is a strong, specific and measurable statement, very like the lecture example "this machine can process 500 units per hour", and unlike vague opinions such as "this machine should be perfect for your business". Context: the statement was made in the course of lengthy commercial negotiations for a customised program, where scalability was a central concern. Timing: it was made one week before the contract was signed, i.e. close to formation, which suggests a term. Maker: Alex is the head of Technovate's technology department and based his statement on similar projects for other clients. He had expert knowledge that GlobalCorp lacked, so GlobalCorp was entitled to rely on him. Importance: the assurance "provided GlobalCorp with confidence in the software's scalability", and capacity was critical, since a 5,000-user limit was "not suitable" for GlobalCorp's operations. GlobalCorp obviously relied on it.

Technovate may argue that Alex was only giving an estimate based on past projects, and that because the parties signed a written contract that does not mention user capacity, they did not intend the statement to be contractual. However, the strength of the words, Alex's expertise and GlobalCorp's obvious reliance outweigh this. A reasonable person would regard the assurance as a promise.

Parol evidence rule. Technovate will argue that the written contract does not mention concurrent users, so the parol evidence rule prevents GlobalCorp from adding Alex's oral assurance to it. However, the rule only applies where the writing appears to be entire. Here the contract "lacks detail and is very poorly written" and "does not specifically address any of the points made by Technovate". There is no indication of an entire-agreement clause. Given six months of negotiations and the failure to record the key technical requirements, the better view is that the parties did not intend the contract to be wholly in writing, so the first exception applies and Alex's assurance may be proved as an oral term.

Collateral contract. In the alternative, GlobalCorp can rely on a collateral contract. (1) There is an oral statement by Alex and a main written contract. (2) The assurance was a promise ("would be capable of handling at least 10,000 concurrent users"), given to persuade GlobalCorp to sign, and it did induce GlobalCorp to contract. (3) It is not inconsistent with the written contract. It is consistent with, and adds detail to, the clause requiring "industry-standard performance capabilities". (4) GlobalCorp provided consideration by entering into the main contract. This is very similar to De Lassalle v Guildford: just as the landlord's assurance about the drains induced the tenant to sign a lease that was silent on drains, Alex's assurance about capacity induced GlobalCorp to sign a contract that was silent on capacity. The software handles only 5,000 users, so the promise has been broken.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Alex's assurance is enforceable, either as a term of a contract not wholly in writing or, alternatively, as a collateral contract, and Technovate is in breach.

Issue 2: Damon's statements

Issue

Are Damon's statements that the software would "likely enhance productivity", was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise GlobalCorp's business operations" enforceable terms of the contract?

Law

A "mere puff" is a statement that is clearly not intended to be taken seriously by the receiver, and it is often found in advertising, for example "Red Bull gives you wings to fly". It does not create a contractual obligation. In Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, the company argued its advertisement was a mere puff, but the court held that it was a serious promise because the company had deposited £1,000 in the bank "showing our sincerity". Statements of opinion such as "I think you'll be very happy with it" are representations, not terms. The five factors set out in Issue 1 also apply.

Application

Damon's claims that the software was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise" GlobalCorp's operations are exaggerated, unquantifiable sales talk of the kind a reasonable business would not take literally. Unlike Carlill, where the £1,000 deposit showed sincerity, Damon offered no research or statistics to back his claims. The statement that the software would "likely enhance productivity" is tentative ("likely") and an opinion about the future, similar to "I think you'll be very happy with it". Applying the factors, the language was weak, the statements were made at the beginning of the negotiation period, about six months before the contract was signed, and Damon was a junior sales representative rather than a technical expert. Nothing suggests GlobalCorp relied on these statements rather than on Alex's specific assurance. GlobalCorp might argue that productivity was important to it, but "enhanced productivity" is too vague to measure.

Conclusion

Damon's statements are mere puffery, or at most non-contractual representations, and are not enforceable terms of the contract.

Issue 3: the written "industry-standard" clause

Issue

Has Technovate breached the express term requiring "industry-standard performance capabilities", and is that term a condition or a warranty?

Law

Express terms are those explicitly agreed by the parties, and a party is bound by the terms of a written contract it has signed: L'Estrange v Graucob. Once a term is identified, the court determines its importance. A condition is fundamental to the contract, and its breach entitles the innocent party to terminate and sue for damages: Poussard v Spiers, where a singer's absence from the opening performances allowed the producers to terminate. A warranty is a minor term whose breach gives damages only: Bettini v Gye, where missing rehearsals gave damages only. The test of essentiality asks whether the promise was so important to the innocent party that it would not have entered the contract without it: Associated Newspapers v Bancks, where a promise to publish a cartoonist's work on the front page was a condition.

Application

The clause is an express written term binding on Technovate. However, it is vague. Whether a 5,000-user capacity falls below "industry-standard performance capabilities" is a question of evidence that the facts do not supply. I assume expert evidence would be needed. GlobalCorp should therefore treat this clause as a supporting argument rather than its main claim. If Alex's assurance is a term (Issue 1), capacity of 10,000 users was essential to GlobalCorp. Like the front-page placement in Bancks and the opening performances in Poussard, it goes to the heart of the bargain, because software that cannot handle GlobalCorp's users is "not suitable" for its business. GlobalCorp would probably not have entered the contract without it, so it is likely a condition rather than a minor warranty as in Bettini v Gye.

Conclusion

The written clause is a term, but proving its breach is uncertain. The capacity promise, if it is a term, is likely a condition.

Remedies

Damages aim to place GlobalCorp in the position it would have been in had the contract been properly performed, and they are available for any breach of contract and for breach of a collateral contract. If Alex's assurance is a term and a condition, GlobalCorp may terminate the contract and claim damages, including its $250,000 of losses. If the assurance is enforced only as a collateral contract, GlobalCorp cannot terminate the main contract but may recover damages for the $250,000 loss caused by the software's inability to handle 10,000 users. GlobalCorp must take reasonable steps to mitigate its loss, for example by seeking an upgrade or a replacement system promptly: Payzu Ltd v Saunders. No remedy is available for Damon's statements. Where damages are inadequate, a court may order specific performance, requiring Technovate to deliver software meeting the promised capacity.

Overall: The most appropriate course of action is for GlobalCorp to sue Technovate for breach of Alex's assurance, as a term or, alternatively, as a collateral contract, and claim damages of $250,000. It should not rely on Damon's statements.

Melissa purchases a ticket to the musical "Cats", which is playing at Brisbane's Convention Centre (BCC).

Whether the disclaimer will effectively protect the BCC from liability for breach of contract depends upon two separate issues: (1) whether the disclaimer is in fact a term of the cloakroom contract; and (2) whether the disclaimer will be interpreted as applying to the breach in question. As instructed, it is assumed that a contract was formed between Melissa and the BCC when she paid $10 to store her coat.

Issue 1

Issue

Is the disclaimer a term of the cloakroom contract between Melissa and the BCC?

Law

A disclaimer is a term of a contract stating that one of the parties will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of its contractual obligations. Disclaimers are also known as exclusion, exemption or limitation of liability clauses. A disclaimer will be a term of the contract if it is: (a) incorporated into a written contract that has been signed; (b) brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract is formed; or (c) implied into the contract as a result of prior dealings.

Signed contract. A party who signs a written contract is bound by its terms whether or not they have read them: L'Estrange v Graucob. In that case, Miss L'Estrange signed an order form for a vending machine without reading the small print, which excluded all warranties. When the machine failed, she was bound by the exclusion clause.

Reasonable notice. With an unsigned document, an exclusion clause is binding only if it was brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was entered into: Olley v Marlborough Court. There, a hotel's disclaimer was on a notice in the guests' bedroom, which they saw only after the contract had been made at the reception desk, so it was not a term. Similarly, in Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking, conditions referred to on a car-park ticket were not a term because the contract had already been formed at the ticket machine. The court asks whether the document is of a kind a reasonable person would expect to contain contractual terms, and whether the business did what was reasonable to bring the terms to the customer's attention.

Application

Signed contract. Melissa signed the cloakroom contract. The disclaimer appears in a separate piece of paper containing the terms and conditions of that contract, which Melissa did not sign. The BCC will argue that, because the terms and conditions document formed part of the cloakroom contract and was handed over as part of the signing process, Melissa is bound by it as part of the signed contract. Similar to L'Estrange v Graucob, Melissa's decision not to read it ("I don't need to read this") does not help her. Melissa may argue that the disclaimer was not in the document she actually signed. The facts do not say whether the signed contract referred to the separate terms. I assume it did, since the document is described as containing "the terms and conditions of the cloakroom contract".

Reasonable notice. Even if the separate page is treated as an unsigned document, the disclaimer was brought to Melissa's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed. The attendant handed her the terms before she signed and before she paid the $10, and the disclaimer was "clearly visible" in that document. A document headed as the terms and conditions of a contract is exactly the kind of document a reasonable person would expect to contain contractual terms. This is unlike Olley and Thornton, where the terms were only seen after the contract had been formed. Here, the BCC did everything reasonable to give notice, and Melissa chose not to read it.

Prior dealings. There is no evidence that Melissa had used the cloakroom before, so prior dealings (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson) do not need to be relied upon.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the disclaimer is a term of the cloakroom contract, because it was part of the contract Melissa signed or, alternatively, was brought to her attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed.

Issue 2

Issue

Will the disclaimer protect the BCC from liability for the theft of Melissa's fur coat?

Law

Where a disclaimer is contained in a contract between two businesses, the courts presume equal bargaining power and interpret it neutrally. However, where the disclaimer is uncertain or ambiguous and is contained in a contract between a business and a consumer, the court will interpret it contra proferentem, that is, against the interests of the party seeking to rely on it. If there is more than one way of interpreting the disclaimer, the court will choose the interpretation that makes it less likely that the disclaimer applies to the breach: White v John Warwick. In that case, a clause in a tricycle hire agreement that "nothing in this agreement shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries" was read narrowly, as excluding only contractual liability and not liability for the owners' negligence when the seat came loose and injured the hirer.

Further, a disclaimer will not exclude liability for acts occurring outside the scope of the contract: Sydney City Council v West. There, a car-park attendant negligently allowed a person to take a car without producing proof of a ticket. Despite a widely drafted clause excluding responsibility for loss "however … caused", the council was liable, because the attendant's act was outside the scope of the contract.

Application

Contra proferentem. The cloakroom contract is between a business, the BCC, and a consumer, Melissa, so the disclaimer will be read against the BCC. The disclaimer is ambiguous. It is unclear whether "any loss or damage to the property of any of its patrons … however that loss or damage may arise" was intended to cover loss caused by patrons themselves or by unavoidable and unforeseen events, or whether it extends to loss caused by the BCC's own careless or negligent conduct. The clause does not expressly mention negligence. As there is more than one interpretation, the court will choose the one that goes against the BCC. Similar to White v John Warwick, where general words did not exclude the owners' negligence, the BCC will remain liable for loss caused by its own fault: its attendant leaving the cloakroom unattended with the key to Locker 5 lying in view on the front desk.

Scope. Melissa will argue that the whole purpose of the cloakroom contract was the secure storage of her belongings. The cloakroom is described as "a secure place … in which people safely store their belongings", and she paid $10 for that service. This is almost identical to Sydney City Council v West. Just as the purpose of the car park was that only the customer could retrieve the car on producing a ticket, the purpose of the cloakroom was that only Melissa could retrieve her coat from a locked locker. Leaving the cloakroom unattended with the locker key on display, so that a thief could simply pick it up and open Locker 5, is a negligent act that is the opposite of what the contract required. It falls outside the scope of what a reasonable person would contemplate occurring within this contract. The BCC may argue that "however that loss or damage may arise" is broad enough to cover theft, but the clause in West was equally broad and still did not protect the council.

Conclusion

The stronger argument is that, although the disclaimer is a term of the contract, it does not protect the BCC from liability for its own negligent conduct, which was outside the scope of the contract. Therefore the BCC is liable for the loss of Melissa's coat.

Remedies

Melissa may claim damages for breach of contract, which aim to put her in the position she would have been in had the contract been performed, i.e. had her coat been returned safely. She can therefore recover the value of the fur coat. The facts do not state its value, so evidence of its market value or replacement cost will be needed. Melissa must take reasonable steps to mitigate her loss (Payzu Ltd v Saunders), for example by cooperating with the police in case the coat is recovered.

Issue 1

Issue

Was a contract formed between Jack and Mary for the sale of the painting?

Law

A contract is a legally binding promise or agreement. A simple contract may be made verbally, in writing or by conduct, and it requires three elements: (1) agreement; (2) intention to create legal relations; and (3) consideration.

Element 1: Agreement. Agreement consists of an offer plus an acceptance. An offer is a proposal by one party to another to immediately enter into a legally binding contract, assessed objectively by asking whether a reasonable person in the recipient's position would regard it as an offer. An offer must be distinguished from an invitation to treat, such as most advertisements and price lists, which merely invites others to make offers. However, an advertisement may be an offer to the world where it shows a willingness to be bound immediately: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. Acceptance occurs when the offeree indicates, by words or conduct, a final and unqualified assent to all the terms of the offer. Acceptance must be unqualified, otherwise it will be considered a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer and puts forward a new offer on different terms. A counter-offer differs from a mere rejection, which simply refuses the offer. Acceptance is judged objectively and may be made by conduct, but silence alone is not acceptance: Felthouse v Bindley.

Element 2: Intention. The parties must intend to create legal relations, and the onus is on the party alleging the contract to prove it. Traditionally, courts presumed that social or domestic agreements were not intended to be binding, but this presumption could be rebutted: Wakeling v Ripley. In that case, Ripley, an elderly man living in Sydney, persuaded his sister and her husband to leave England and move to Sydney to look after him, promising they could live in his house and would inherit it. When he later evicted them, the court held that, despite the family relationship, there was an intention to be legally bound, given the seriousness of the promises and the significant sacrifices made in reliance on them. Today this is an objective test, and the correct approach is to consider the agreement on its own merits without presumptions. All of the relevant circumstances and the parties' conduct are considered, including the conduct of the parties, the context in which the agreement was made, the language used, the value of any consideration and the nature of the relationship: Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community of SA Inc.

Element 3: Consideration. The common law requires that, for an agreement to be binding, the promisee must provide consideration for the promise received. Consideration is the price paid, or promised, by each party for the other's promise, and it may take the form of money, goods or services, or a promise to provide them: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. It need not be adequate, only of some legal value: Thomas v Thomas.

Application

To determine whether a contract was formed between Jack and Mary, it is necessary to apply the three elements.

Element 1: Agreement. Jack's question, "Would you be willing to sell it to me?", was a mere inquiry, not an offer. Mary's eBay listing at $150,000 was an advertisement and, unlike the advertisement in Carlill, merely an invitation to treat. The offer was made when Mary said, "you can buy it for $140,000": a firm, certain proposal, communicated to Jack, to sell immediately at a stated price. A counter-offer was made when Jack replied, "How about $120,000?" This was not a rejection but a counter-offer, because it rejected the $140,000 offer while proposing a new price, and Mary's original offer was then no longer open for acceptance. A further counter-offer occurred when Mary responded, "Yes. That's a deal. I will deliver it to your address by next week. Is that okay?" Although Mary said "yes", she added a new term about delivery and asked for Jack's agreement, so her response was qualified. Acceptance occurred when Jack nodded and handed Mary a cheque for $120,000. This was acceptance by conduct, objectively showing his final and unqualified assent to the sale at $120,000 with delivery by next week. It was not silence, as in Felthouse v Bindley, but positive acts. Alternatively, if Mary's "Yes. That's a deal" is treated as an acceptance of Jack's $120,000 offer, agreement was reached at that point. Either way, there was agreement.

Element 2: Intention. The correct approach is to consider the agreement between Jack and Mary on its own merits, viewing all the conduct objectively. Mary may argue that, as siblings chatting over coffee at her home, they were in a social or domestic setting and did not intend legal consequences. However, the nature of the relationship is no longer decisive (Ermogenous). As was the case in Wakeling v Ripley, where the court found that family members intended to be bound because the arrangement was serious and significant, the parties here clearly intended to be legally bound. Various facts support this. (1) The value of the consideration was very large, $120,000. (2) The parties engaged in genuine price negotiation from $140,000 down to $120,000, typical of a commercial bargain. (3) Mary used commercial language: "That's a deal." (4) Jack immediately wrote a cheque for the full price, a formal step. (5) The parties arranged delivery to Jack's address by a set time. (6) The painting was already listed for sale on the open market on eBay, showing Mary was dealing with it commercially. (7) Jack is an art collector who buys expensive paintings, so this was the type of transaction he enters into in a business-like way. Just as the Wakelings' sacrifices showed seriousness in Wakeling, Jack's payment of $120,000 shows the seriousness of this arrangement.

Element 3: Consideration. Jack provided consideration in the form of the $120,000 cheque. Mary provided consideration in the form of her promise to transfer the painting and deliver it to Jack's address. This is sufficient consideration. Even though the price was below the eBay listing, consideration need not be adequate (Thomas v Thomas).

Conclusion

A court would likely decide that a contract was formed between Jack and Mary for the sale of the painting for $120,000, including delivery by the following week.

Issue 2

Issue

Is Mary's promise to re-frame the painting enforceable by Jack?

Law

A promise is only enforceable as part of a contract if the promisee has provided consideration for it. Consideration must be sufficient, and past consideration is not good consideration. An act or payment completed before a promise is made cannot be the price of that promise: Roscorla v Thomas. In that case, after the sale of a horse had been concluded, the seller promised the buyer that the horse was sound and free from vice. The horse proved vicious, but the buyer could not enforce the promise, because the only consideration he had given, the purchase price, had been provided before the promise was made.

Application

Mary's promise to re-frame the painting is separate from her promise to sell and deliver it. The contract of sale was complete when Jack accepted and gave Mary the cheque at her home. Mary's promise to re-frame was made later, when Jack phoned her after arriving home. The payment by Jack for the purchase of the painting was made before Mary's promise. Therefore that payment is past consideration and cannot support the new promise. This is very similar to Roscorla v Thomas. Just as the seller's promise about the horse's soundness was made only after the sale had been concluded and the price paid, Mary's promise to put a new frame on the painting was made only after the sale had been agreed and the $120,000 handed over. Jack did not offer any additional payment or anything else in return for the new frame. Ultimately, no consideration was provided by Jack for Mary's promise to re-frame the painting.

Conclusion

Mary's promise to re-frame the painting cannot be enforced, as past consideration is not sufficient consideration.

Advice to Mary

Mary formed a binding contract to sell the painting to Jack for $120,000 and has performed it by delivering the painting by the following week. Jack is not entitled to his money back, as there has been no breach of the contract of sale, and Mary is not legally obliged to re-frame the painting.

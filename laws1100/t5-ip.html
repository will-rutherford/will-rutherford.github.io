1 · Learn it in 20 minutes

What IP is: an intangible creation (product of intellectual effort, e.g. the expression of an idea or a trade mark). IP law gives the owner the right to stop others copying, using or exploiting it without permission. Once rights expire, the IP enters the public domain.

A. Copyright — the TWO issues (W13 revision slide 25: "Issue 1: Protection (3 elements); Issue 2: Infringement (3 elements)") + defences

Copyright = the right to prevent unauthorised copying of the expression of an idea (book, song, film, photo). It does not protect the idea itself. Automatic under the CA.

Issue 1 — Protection: 3 requirements

  1. A 'work' or 'subject matter other than works' (s 32). Four works: literary (text — book, email, manual, lyrics, timetable, contract, ad — Mirror Newspapers v Queensland Newspapers [1982]); musical (arrangement of notes); dramatic (dance, choreography, film script); artistic (paintings, photos, graphic designs, buildings/plans — Clarendon v Henley (1999)). Single words, titles, headlines and slogans are usually too short — Fairfax Media v Reed International (2010). Subject matter other than works: sound recordings (s 89), films (s 90), TV/sound broadcasts (s 91 — Network Ten v TCN (2004)), published editions (s 92). Multiple layers can exist at once: a song = lyrics (literary) + composition (musical) + track (sound recording).
  2. Original (s 32). Doesn't mean new or unique — it must originate from the author with independent intellectual effort, not copied. Low threshold. Independent creation of the same thing is not infringement. Mere compilations of facts may not be original unless effort went into selection/arrangement — IceTV v Nine Network (2009); Dynamic Supplies v Tonnex [2011]. Originality applies to works, not subject matter other than works.
  3. Expressed in material form. Ideas, facts, methods, systems aren't protected — only their expression — John Fairfax & Sons v Australian Consolidated Press (1960). "Material form" = "any form (whether visible or not) of storage" (s 10). Telling someone your idea gives no copyright: authorship turns on who created the particular expression — Donoghue v Allied Newspapers (1938) (a jockey told his stories to a journalist who wrote them up; the journalist, who wrote the expression, was the author).

Boomerang Investments v Padgett [2020]: lyrics "love is in the air" not original enough as a literary work, but the way the line sounded was copied — infringed as a musical work.

Extent, duration, ownership

  • s 31 exclusive rights: reproduce in material form; publish; perform in public; communicate to the public; make an adaptation.
  • s 33 duration: literary/dramatic/musical/artistic — 70 yrs after creator's death (or first publication); film/sound recording — 70 yrs after publication; broadcasts — 50 yrs; published editions — 25 yrs.
  • Ownership: separate from owning the physical object (buying a DVD ≠ owning the film's copyright). General rule: author is first owner (s 35(2)). Employee making the work in pursuance of employment → employer owns (s 35(6)) — must be an employee (not contractor) AND made under their duties (Redrock Holdings v Hinkley (2001)). Independent contractors keep copyright unless agreed. Commissioned private/domestic works (e.g. photos) → commissioner owns (s 35(5)). Agreement can change all of this (s 35(3)). Academics: university IP policies may let academics keep copyright in scholarly works.

Issue 2 — Infringement: 3 requirements

Infringement = exercising any s 31 exclusive right without the owner's permission.

  1. Substantial part copied — qualitative not quantitative; importance not amount. Hawkes & Son v Paramount Film Service (1934): short but distinctive, recognisable bit of a march in a newsreel = substantial. EMI Songs v Larrikin Music (2011): two bars of "Kookaburra" in the flute riff of "Down Under" = substantial.
  2. Objective similarity — exact copy clearly similar; otherwise compare the protected expression, not the general idea. Zeccola v Universal Studios (1982): "Great White" copied more than the idea of a killer shark from "Jaws" — characters, situations, sequence of events → infringed.
  3. Causal connection — the copy was derived from the original, not independently created. Inferred from access + similarity. Copying can be subconscious: Bright Tunes v Harrisongs Music (1976) (George Harrison had heard "He's So Fine"; "My Sweet Lord" subconsciously copied it → infringed).

Defences & remedies

  • No substantial part copied → no infringement.
  • Fair dealing (CA ss 40–43, 103A–104): research or study; criticism or review; parody or satire; reporting news; judicial proceedings / professional advice. Must be fair — depends on purpose and extent of use.
  • Remedies (s 115): damages (s 115(2)–(3)); injunction (s 115(2)); account of profits (s 115(2)–(3)).
Moral rights are not in the TW8 slides. Don't build an answer around them. If you want to, add one line at most, and cite no case.

B. Trade marks (TMA)

What: a distinctive word, phrase or symbol used in trade to show a connection between a business and its product (s 17 TMA). Can be letters, words, names, devices, packaging, shape, colour, sound or scent. Coca-Cola v All-Fect (1999): Coke's registered contour-bottle shape stopped All-Fect selling cola lollies shaped like the bottle. BP v Woolworths (2004): BP tried to register the colour green for service stations (colour marks are possible, but they must be distinctive).

Registration is required for TMA protection ("VERY IMPORTANT TO REGISTER"). Registrable only if it: has no prescribed sign (e.g. national emblems); can be represented graphically; is distinctive (not everyday terms like "Olympic", geographical indications like "champagne", or quality words — Beecham v Colgate-Palmolive (2002)); is not scandalous / contrary to law; is not likely to deceive or confuse; and is not substantially identical with or deceptively similar to another registered or pending mark for similar goods / closely related services (s 44) — Torpedoes v Thorpedo (2003) ("THORPEDO" too close to the registered "TORPEDOES" for clothing).

Process: search the IP Australia database → TM Headstart → apply in particular class(es) → examined → if accepted, advertised in the Official Journal → 3 months for opposition → registered. Renew every 10 years, indefinitely.

Extent: protects only the classes registered — unless the mark is well known in Australia, in which case use on unrelated goods can be stopped (Eastman v John Griffiths (1898) — KODAK; Virgin Enterprises v Klapsas (2001)).

Infringement — 3 elements (slide 46): (1) the owner's TM is registered under the TMA; (2) the defendant uses a mark substantially identical with or deceptively similar to it; (3) on goods/services the same as or closely related to those it is registered for. Example: Arnott's "Iced VoVo" vs Krispy Kreme's "Iced Vo-Vo" doughnut.

Defences: the defendant's own name or place of business; descriptive use (kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, other characteristic); comparative advertising; the owner's implied consent; prior continuous use (from before the owner registered or used the mark).

Remedies: injunction + either damages or account of profits. Unregistered TM → sue in passing off or under ACL s 18.

C. Passing off (tort — also TW4 Other torts; see T2)

The defendant misrepresents themselves or their product as having a connection with the plaintiff. Plaintiff must show:

  1. a misrepresentation (express or implied) that the defendant's goods/services are connected with the plaintiff, or have the plaintiff's endorsement or approval;
  2. made in the course of trade;
  3. intended to deceive potential purchasers.

Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989): a shoe ad parodied the famous knife scene from Crocodile Dundee. Viewers would assume Paul Hogan had approved it, so it was passing off. Overlaps with ACL s 18: see Taco Co v Taco Bell (1982) (the same restaurant name suggested a connection) and McWilliam's Wines v McDonald's (1980) (confusion alone is not enough) — T6. Remedies (tort): damages; injunction.

D. Patents (Patents Act 1990)

A limited monopoly for a new technology, in return for publicly disclosing how it works. Must be registered with the Patents Office (IP Australia). Standard patent: 20 yrs (slide also says 20–25), fully examined, must be inventive. Innovation patent: 8 yrs, formalities check, only innovative (a minor, non-trivial advance).

Patentable if: (1) manner of manufacture — a product, composition or process that is invented, not an abstract idea or natural phenomenon (NV Philips v Mirabella (1995); Grant v Commissioner of Patents (2006) — a legal/financial asset-protection scheme wasn't patentable); (2) novel compared with the prior art base — not already published or publicly used (Windsurfing v Petit (1983)); (3) inventive (not obvious to a skilled person in the field) or innovative (non-trivial difference); (4) useful (specific, substantial, credible, practical use with commercial potential).

Infringement: the patented technology is copied, used or exploited without consent. The court may refuse damages or an account of profits if the defendant didn't know it was patented.

E. Designs (Designs Act 2003)

A design is the overall appearance of a product resulting from one or more visual features (s 5). It protects appearance only, not how the product works. Must be registered, and must be new (not identical to the prior art base) and distinctive (not substantially similar in overall impression to the prior art). Owner's exclusive rights: make, import, sell or hire, use in trade, authorise others. Lasts 5 yrs + one 5-yr renewal, then public domain. Infringed by making, importing, selling, hiring or offering a product with a design identical or substantially similar in overall impression to the registered design, without consent.

F. Confidential information — breach of confidence

Information disclosed on a confidential basis (trade secrets: formulae, accounts, strategies, recipes, customer lists, drawings). Tort of breach of confidence. Requirements:

  1. the information is confidential in nature (not trivial, not public knowledge);
  2. it was given in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence — express and accepted, or implied from context, e.g. employer/employee (NP Generations v Feneley (2001));
  3. unauthorised use or threatened use.

It doesn't need to be in material form: Fraser v Thames Television (1984) — an idea for a TV series, pitched verbally, was protected. Contract angle (W13: "both Torts and Contractual issues"): a confidentiality agreement removes doubt about the obligation; employment contracts may have restraint of trade clauses, which must be reasonable (they can't stop someone earning a living forever) — breach of these is breach of contract (see T3).

The slides frame breach of confidence as a TORT plus contract. They don't discuss "equity", so use the slides' framing and say "tort".

A · COPYRIGHT

ISSUE 1 — PROTECTION
Is [the lecture / photo / manual] protected by copyright under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth)?
LAW
Intellectual property is an intangible product of intellectual effort, and IP law gives its owner the right to prevent others from copying, using or exploiting it without permission. Copyright is the legal right to prevent unauthorised copying of the expression of an idea; it does not protect the idea itself. In Australia copyright is regulated by the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) ("CA") and protection is automatic, with no registration required. A creation is protected if three requirements are met: (1) it is a 'work' or 'subject matter other than works'; (2) it is original; and (3) it is expressed in a material form.
(1) Work. Section 32 of the CA extends protection to original literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works. A literary work is the expression of an idea in the form of text, such as a book, article, email, manual or written information (Mirror Newspapers v Queensland Newspapers [1982]), although single words, titles and slogans are usually too short to be protected (Fairfax Media v Reed International (2010)). The CA also separately protects 'subject matter other than works', including sound recordings (s 89), films (s 90), broadcasts (s 91) and published editions (s 92), so several layers of copyright may exist in the one product.
(2) Original. A work is original if it originates from the author and involves independent intellectual effort rather than being copied; it need not be new or unique, and the threshold is low (s 32). A mere compilation of facts may lack originality unless sufficient effort went into its selection or arrangement (IceTV v Nine Network (2009)).
(3) Material form. Copyright does not protect ideas, facts or methods, only their expression (John Fairfax & Sons v Australian Consolidated Press (1960)). 'Material form' means 'any form (whether visible or not) of storage' (s 10). In Donoghue v Allied Newspapers (1938), a jockey told his stories to a journalist, who wrote them up; the journalist, as the person who created the particular expression, was the author, and the jockey had no copyright merely because he supplied the ideas.
APPLICATION
Work: [Item] is likely a [literary/musical/dramatic/artistic] work because [it is written text / a composition / a photo…]. In addition, the [recording] is itself 'subject matter other than works' (s [89]).
Original: [Author] independently prepared [it] using their own skill and effort and did not copy it, which easily satisfies the low threshold.
Material form: [It was written down / saved / recorded], which is a form of storage under s 10. (Against / unscripted:) However, [the spoken presentation] was not stored in any form until [it was recorded]. Similar to Donoghue, where authorship depended on who created the expression, here [the speaker] created the words, while [the recorder] merely captured them…
CONCLUSION
Therefore, it is likely that copyright subsists in […].
OWNERSHIP (sub-issue — use whenever the creator is an employee/contractor/commissioned)
Who owns the copyright in [the work]?
LAW
Ownership of copyright is separate from ownership of the physical object. Generally the author is the first owner (s 35(2)). However, where a work is created by an employee in pursuance of the terms of their employment, the employer generally owns it (s 35(6)); the creator must be an employee rather than an independent contractor, and the work must be created under their employment duties (Redrock Holdings v Hinkley (2001)). An independent contractor generally keeps copyright unless otherwise agreed; a person who commissions a work for a private or domestic purpose is generally the first owner (s 35(5)); and the default rules may be changed by agreement (s 35(3)). University IP policies may allow academics to keep copyright in certain scholarly works.
APPLICATION
[X] is an employee of [Y] and prepared [the work] as part of their duties to [teach/design…], so under s 35(6) [Y] is likely the owner. However, the facts do not say whether [Y] has an IP policy or agreement (s 35(3)) letting [X] keep copyright; if so, [X] would own it.
ISSUE 2 — INFRINGEMENT
Have [D1 and D2] infringed the copyright in [the work]?
LAW
Under s 31 of the CA, the copyright owner has the exclusive rights to reproduce the work in a material form, publish it, perform it in public, communicate it to the public and make an adaptation. Copyright is infringed when a person exercises any of these rights without permission, and it requires: (1) a substantial part was copied; (2) objective similarity between the original and the copy; and (3) a causal connection between them.
(1) Substantial part. This is qualitative, not quantitative: it looks at the importance of what was copied rather than the amount. In Hawkes & Son v Paramount Film Service (1934), a short but distinctive and recognisable part of a musical work in a film was substantial; in EMI Songs Australia v Larrikin Music (2011), two bars of "Kookaburra" in the flute riff of "Down Under" were substantial.
(2) Objective similarity. An exact copy is clearly objectively similar; otherwise the court compares the particular expression, not the general idea. In Zeccola v Universal Studios (1982), "Great White" shared not only the idea of a killer shark with "Jaws" but also its characters, situations and sequence of events, and so infringed.
(3) Causal connection. The copy must be derived from the original rather than created independently. This may be inferred from access plus similarity, and the copying need not be conscious: in Bright Tunes v Harrisongs Music (1976), George Harrison had heard "He's So Fine" and subconsciously copied it in "My Sweet Lord".
APPLICATION
Exclusive rights: By [recording / photocopying], [D1] reproduced the work in a material form; by [selling / uploading], [D2] published it or communicated it to the public, without [owner]'s permission.
Substantial part: [The whole / the key part] was copied. Even in Hawkes and EMI v Larrikin, where only small parts were taken, the copying was substantial; here, a fortiori, […].
Objective similarity: [A recording/photocopy] is an exact reproduction, so unlike Zeccola, where the court had to compare characters and plot, the similarity here is obvious.
Causal connection: [D1] had direct access and deliberately copied the original. This is stronger than Bright Tunes, where even subconscious copying was enough.
CONCLUSION
Therefore, [D1 and D2] have likely infringed [owner]'s copyright, subject to any defence.
DEFENCES
Can [D] rely on a defence?
LAW
A person accused of infringement may argue that no substantial part was copied, or rely on an exception in the CA. Under the fair dealing provisions (CA ss 40–43 and 103A–104), copyright material may be used without permission where the dealing is fair and is for research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, reporting news, or judicial proceedings or professional advice. Whether a dealing is fair depends on the circumstances, including the purpose and extent of the use.
APPLICATION
For: [D] may argue the copy was for [research or study] because […]. Against: the dealing is unlikely to be fair, because [the whole work was copied / it was done secretly against policy / it was sold for profit, which is a commercial purpose, not study].
CONCLUSION
The fair dealing defence is therefore unlikely to succeed [for the sale].
REMEDIES
[Owner] may seek an injunction to restrain further infringement (s 115(2)), and either damages to compensate its loss (s 115(2)–(3)) or an account of the profits [D] made, e.g. from [the sales] (s 115(2)–(3)).

B · TRADE MARK INFRINGEMENT

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for infringement of its registered trade mark under the Trade Marks Act 1995 (Cth)?
LAW
A trade mark is a distinctive word, phrase or symbol used in commercial dealings to show a connection between a business and its product (s 17 TMA). It may be letters, a word, a name, a device, an aspect of packaging, a shape, a colour, a sound or a scent: in Coca-Cola v All-Fect (1999), Coca-Cola's registered contour bottle shape stopped All-Fect importing and selling lollies in the same shape. Trade marks must be registered to be protected under the TMA, and a registered mark can be renewed every 10 years indefinitely.
A registered trade mark is infringed if: (1) the owner's mark is registered under the TMA; (2) the defendant uses a mark substantially identical with or deceptively similar to it; and (3) the defendant uses it on goods or services the same as or closely related to those it is registered for. Whether a mark is deceptively similar asks whether it nearly resembles the registered mark and is likely to mislead or cause confusion (s 44; Torpedoes v Thorpedo (2003), where 'THORPEDO' was deceptively similar to the registered 'TORPEDOES' for clothing). Protection is limited to the registered classes, unless the mark is well known in Australia (Eastman v John Griffiths (1898); Virgin Enterprises v Klapsas (2001)).
A defendant may avoid liability if the mark is their own name or place of business; describes the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose or another characteristic of their products; is used in comparative advertising; is used with the owner's implied consent; or has been used since before the owner registered or began using the mark.
APPLICATION
(1) Registered: [P] registered ['MARK'] in [year] for [goods/services], so this element is satisfied. (If unregistered → go to passing off.)
(2) Deceptively similar: ['D MARK'] is not identical, but it [differs by one letter / sounds the same / uses the same image and colours]. Similar to Torpedoes v Thorpedo, where a one-letter change was not enough, […]. Evidence of actual confusion — [customers asking if it is a new branch] — shows it is likely to mislead or cause confusion. Against: [D] may argue [the different word/overall look]…
(3) Same/closely related goods: [D] uses the mark for [the same coffee/café services], which are within [P]'s registered class[es].
Defences: ['D MARK'] is not [D]'s own name and does not describe the product; there was no consent, and no use before [P]'s registration, so no defence applies.
CONCLUSION
Therefore, [D] has likely infringed [P]'s registered trade mark.
REMEDIES
Under the TMA, [P] may obtain an injunction to prevent further infringement, and either damages or an account of profits.

C · PASSING OFF (always run it with B, and alone if the mark/get-up is unregistered)

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of passing off?
LAW
If a trade mark (or get-up) is unregistered, the owner may still prevent its misuse through the tort of passing off, or under s 18 of the ACL. A defendant commits passing off if they misrepresent themselves or their product as having some kind of connection with the plaintiff. The plaintiff must show: (1) the defendant made a misrepresentation, express or implied, that its goods or services are connected with the plaintiff or have its endorsement or approval; (2) the misrepresentation was made in the course of trade; and (3) the misrepresentation was intended to deceive potential purchasers. In Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989), a shoe advertisement parodying the knife scene from Crocodile Dundee was passing off, because viewers would believe Paul Hogan had approved or was connected with the product.
APPLICATION
(1) Misrepresentation of connection: By using [a similar name / the same colours / the same logo], [D] impliedly represented that its [café] is connected with [P]. Like Pacific Dunlop v Hogan, where an implied connection was enough, here [customers believed…]. Against: [D] will argue some customers were merely confused, and confusion alone is not enough (compare McWilliam's Wines v McDonald's (1980), TW9).
(2) In trade: [D] used the get-up when selling […] to the public, which is clearly in the course of trade.
(3) Intention to deceive: [D] knew of [P]'s business because [she previously worked there / it is nearby], and copied several features at once, so an intention to deceive can be inferred. Against: [D] says [innocent reason]; the facts don't confirm this, so I assume…
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [D] has committed the tort of passing off. [P] may seek damages (to restore it to its position had the tort not occurred) and an injunction to stop the continuing conduct.

D · PATENT (short-form ILAC)

ISSUE
Is [X]'s [invention] patentable under the Patents Act 1990 (Cth), and has [D] infringed it?
LAW
A patent grants the creator of a new technology the exclusive right to exploit it for a limited period, and it must be registered with IP Australia. A standard patent lasts 20 years and must be inventive; an innovation patent lasts 8 years and need only be innovative. A technology is patentable only if it is: (1) a manner of manufacture — a product, composition or process that has been invented, not an abstract idea or something occurring naturally (NV Philips v Mirabella (1995); Grant v Commissioner of Patents (2006)); (2) novel compared with the prior art base, so it cannot already have been published or publicly used (Windsurfing v Petit (1983)); (3) inventive (not obvious to a person skilled in the field) or innovative (a non-trivial difference); and (4) useful, with a specific, substantial, credible and practical use. A patent is infringed if the technology is copied, used or exploited without consent, although the court may refuse damages or an account of profits if the defendant was unaware of the patent.
APPLICATION
Manner of manufacture: [it is a physical device/process]. Novelty: [was it shown at the expo / sold before filing?] — if so, like Windsurfing v Petit, it is not novel…

F · BREACH OF CONFIDENCE (tort + contract)

ISSUE
Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of breach of confidence?
LAW
Breach of confidence is a tort committed when a person uses or discloses information disclosed to them on a confidential basis without the owner's consent. It requires: (1) the information is confidential in nature, not trivial or public knowledge; (2) it was given in circumstances where an obligation of confidence was expressly made known and accepted, or implied from the context, such as an employer/employee relationship (NP Generations v Feneley (2001)); and (3) there has been an unauthorised use or threatened use. The information need not be in material form: in Fraser v Thames Television (1984), an idea communicated verbally was protected. Further, a confidentiality agreement or a reasonable restraint of trade clause in an employment contract may give a separate contractual claim.
APPLICATION
[The recipe/customer list] is not public knowledge and is valuable to [P]… [D] received it as an employee, so an obligation of confidence is implied, as in NP Generations… [D] has used it to […] without consent.
CONCLUSION
Therefore [D] has likely committed breach of confidence; [P] may seek an injunction and damages, plus a contract claim if [a confidentiality / restraint clause] was breached.

4 · Full model answers

Model 1 — 2021 S1 Q2: Alex and Travis (copyright)

Question text (15 marks in 2021; write it as a full 25-mark answer)

Alex is a law student at the University of Brisbane (UB). One day Alex wakes up late and realises he cannot make it to his 8am lecture. Alex phones his classmate, Travis, and asks Travis to secretly record the lecture with a digital voice recorder so that Alex can listen to it later. UB has a policy that they do not record any lectures.

The lecture goes for two hours and is divided into two sessions. During the first hour, Alex's usual lecturer, Professor Butler, presents a lecture on the topic of Contract Law. The lecture is given by reading out a set of comprehensive teaching notes which Professor Butler prepared prior to class.

During the second hour, Professor Butler invites another academic at UB named Charley to talk to the students. Charley is also a corporate lawyer who gives an unscripted presentation on real-world Contract Law issues whilst answering various questions from students about his legal experiences.

The day after the lecture, Travis sends Alex the recording. Alex listens to it, and then also sells the recording to other students who also could not attend the lecture.

Advise Alex and Travis whether they have committed an intellectual property breach. Explore all aspects of the law including copyright protection, ownership and whether there are any relevant defences.

Answer plan (5 lines)
  1. Issue 1 Protection — split the lecture: Butler's hour (written notes = literary work, original, material form ✅) vs Charley's hour (unscripted → material form is the battleground; s 10 + Donoghue; two sides).
  2. Issue 2 Ownership — Butler = UB employee → s 35(6) UB owns (but uni IP policy may let academics keep it). Charley = UB academic, but is a guest talk within his duties? Argue both; state the assumption.
  3. Issue 3 Infringement — s 31 rights: Travis reproduced it (recording); Alex procured it, then published/communicated it by selling. Substantial part (whole lecture — Hawkes, EMI); objective similarity (exact copy — vs Zeccola); causal connection (direct, deliberate — Bright Tunes).
  4. Issue 4 Defences — fair dealing for research/study: arguable for Alex's private listening; fails for Travis's secret recording against policy (arguably) and clearly fails for the sales.
  5. Remedies s 115 — injunction; damages or account of profits (Alex's sales). One-line overall conclusion.

Whether Alex and Travis have committed an intellectual property breach depends on whether copyright protects the lecture, who owns that copyright, whether Alex and Travis infringed it, and whether any defence applies.

Issue 1: Protection

Issue
Is the lecture given by Professor Butler and Charley protected by copyright under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth)?

Law
Intellectual property is an intangible product of intellectual effort, and IP law gives its owner the right to prevent others from copying, using or exploiting it without permission. Copyright is the right to prevent unauthorised copying of the expression of an idea; it does not protect the idea itself. Copyright is regulated by the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) ("CA") and arises automatically, without registration. A creation is protected if: (1) it is a 'work' or 'subject matter other than works'; (2) it is original; and (3) it is expressed in a material form.

Work. Section 32 protects original literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works. A literary work is the expression of an idea in text, such as a book, article, manual or written information (Mirror Newspapers v Queensland Newspapers [1982]). The CA also separately protects 'subject matter other than works', including sound recordings (s 89), so several layers of copyright may exist in the one product.

Original. A work is original if it originates from the author through independent intellectual effort rather than being copied. It need not be new or unique, and the threshold is low (s 32).

Material form. Copyright protects the expression of an idea, not ideas or facts themselves (John Fairfax & Sons v Australian Consolidated Press (1960)). 'Material form' means 'any form (whether visible or not) of storage' (s 10). In Donoghue v Allied Newspapers (1938), a jockey told his stories to a journalist, who wrote them up. The court held that the journalist, who created the particular expression, was the author; the jockey had no copyright merely because he supplied the ideas orally.

Case analysis in the Law section — facts + held — so you can compare in Application. This is what pushes a 5 to a 6/7 (Tutorial 4 feedback).

Application
Professor Butler's session. The lecture was delivered by reading out comprehensive teaching notes that Professor Butler prepared before class. Those notes are written text expressing his ideas on Contract Law, so they are a literary work under s 32. They are original, because Professor Butler prepared them himself using his own skill and effort, which easily meets the low threshold. They were also in material form, being written and stored before the lecture (s 10). Copyright therefore subsists in the notes, and reading them aloud is a reproduction of that literary work.

Charley's session. Charley's presentation was unscripted, and he answered students' questions spontaneously. His spoken words are likely original, because they come from his own legal experience and are not copied. Material form, however, is arguable. On one view, the talk was never stored in any form by Charley, so, like an idea that is merely communicated, it is not protected. On the other view, Travis's recording stored the words in a material form (s 10), so a literary work then existed. Applying Donoghue, authorship depends on who created the particular expression. Here that was Charley, who chose the words, not Travis, who merely pressed record and contributed no expression. This is the reverse of Donoghue, where the person who only supplied ideas orally was not the author. On balance, it is likely that copyright subsists in Charley's words as a literary work once recorded, with Charley as their author.

The recording itself. Travis's recording is a sound recording, which is 'subject matter other than works' (s 89). That does not give Travis any right to use the lecture content recorded in it, which is a separate layer of copyright.

Conclusion
Copyright likely subsists in Professor Butler's notes as a literary work, and probably also in Charley's presentation once it was recorded, although this is less certain.

Issue 2: Ownership

Issue
Who owns the copyright in Professor Butler's and Charley's material?

Law
Ownership of copyright is separate from ownership of a physical object. The author is generally the first owner (s 35(2)). However, where a work is created by an employee in pursuance of the terms of their employment, the employer generally owns the copyright (s 35(6)). The creator must be an employee rather than an independent contractor, and the work must be made under their employment duties (Redrock Holdings v Hinkley (2001)). These default rules can be changed by agreement (s 35(3)), and university IP policies may allow academics to keep copyright in certain scholarly works.

Application
Butler. Professor Butler is UB's "usual lecturer", which indicates he is a UB employee. Preparing and delivering teaching notes is a central part of a lecturer's duties, so the notes were created in pursuance of his employment, and under s 35(6) UB likely owns the copyright. However, the facts do not say whether UB has an IP policy or agreement letting academics keep copyright in teaching materials (s 35(3)). If it does, Professor Butler would own it. Either way, the owner is not Alex or Travis.

Charley. Charley is "another academic at UB", so he is also an employee. Speaking to students at a colleague's invitation is arguably within an academic's teaching duties, in which case UB owns the copyright under s 35(6). Against this, the talk drew on his separate work as a corporate lawyer and was a one-off guest appearance, so it may not have been "in pursuance of" his UB employment, in which case Charley owns it under s 35(2). Since the facts are silent, I assume it was part of his UB duties.

Conclusion
UB is likely the owner of the copyright in both sessions, unless an IP policy or agreement provides otherwise, or Charley's talk fell outside his duties, in which case he owns his part.

Issue 3: Infringement

Issue
Have Travis and Alex infringed the copyright in the lecture?

Law
Under s 31 of the CA, the owner has the exclusive rights to reproduce the work in a material form, publish it, perform it in public, communicate it to the public and make an adaptation. Infringement occurs when a person exercises these rights without permission, and requires: (1) a substantial part was copied; (2) objective similarity; and (3) a causal connection.

Substantial part is a qualitative test of the importance of what was copied, not its amount. In Hawkes & Son v Paramount Film Service (1934), a short but distinctive part of a musical work in a film was substantial. In EMI Songs Australia v Larrikin Music (2011), only two bars of "Kookaburra" in "Down Under" were still substantial.

Objective similarity compares the particular expression, not the general idea. In Zeccola v Universal Studios (1982), "Great White" copied the characters, situations and sequence of events of "Jaws" and so infringed.

Causal connection requires that the copy be derived from the original rather than created independently. It may be inferred from access, and need not be deliberate: in Bright Tunes v Harrisongs Music (1976), George Harrison subconsciously copied "He's So Fine", which he had heard before.

Application
Exclusive rights. By recording the lecture on a digital voice recorder, Travis reproduced Professor Butler's and Charley's works in a material form, without the permission of UB, which has a policy against recording. Alex asked Travis to make the recording, and then sold copies to other students. By doing so he published the work, or communicated it to the public, and so exercised the owner's exclusive rights himself.

Substantial part. The whole two-hour lecture was recorded. If two bars were substantial in EMI v Larrikin and a short extract in Hawkes, then the entire lecture, including all of its key content, clearly is.

Objective similarity. A sound recording is an exact reproduction of the words spoken. Unlike Zeccola, where the court had to compare plot and characters to find similarity, here the copy is identical.

Causal connection. Travis recorded the lecture directly as it was given, and Alex's copies were made from Travis's recording, so the copies were plainly derived from the original. The copying was deliberate and secret, which is a stronger case than Bright Tunes, where even subconscious copying sufficed.

Conclusion
Travis has likely infringed copyright by reproducing the lecture, and Alex by procuring the recording and then selling it to other students, unless a defence applies.

Issue 4: Defences

Issue
Can Travis or Alex rely on the defence of fair dealing?

Law
A defendant may argue that no substantial part was copied, or rely on an exception under the CA. The fair dealing provisions (ss 40–43, 103A–104) permit use of copyright material without permission where the dealing is fair and for a recognised purpose, including research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, reporting news, or judicial proceedings or professional advice. Whether a dealing is fair depends on the circumstances, including the purpose and extent of the use.

Application
The "no substantial part" defence fails, because the whole lecture was copied. As for fair dealing, Alex may argue that the recording was made so he could study the lecture he missed, which is a purpose of research or study. In favour of fairness, he is an enrolled student and missed a lecture he was entitled to attend. Against this, the entire lecture was copied, it was done "secretly" in breach of UB's policy against recording, and Travis made it for someone else rather than for his own study. These factors suggest the dealing was not fair. In any event, Alex's sale of the recording to other students is a commercial purpose, not research or study, and so falls outside fair dealing altogether.

Conclusion
Fair dealing might arguably cover Alex's private listening, but it will not protect Travis's secret recording, and it clearly cannot excuse Alex's sales.

Remedies

UB (or Charley, for his part, if he is the owner) may seek an injunction to stop further copying and sales (s 115(2)). It may also seek either damages for any loss caused (s 115(2)–(3)) or an account of the profits Alex made from selling the recording (s 115(2)–(3)). Travis and Alex should stop distributing the recording and delete all copies.

Overall: On the balance of probabilities, both Travis and Alex have infringed copyright in the lecture, which is most likely owned by UB, and Alex's sales attract the greatest liability.

Model 2 — Practice question (25 marks): trade mark + passing off

MoonBean Pty Ltd runs a popular chain of three coffee shops in Brisbane. In 2019 it registered the word mark "MOONBEAN" and a logo of a gold crescent moon on a navy circle as trade marks for coffee and café services. It also serves every drink in a distinctive navy cup with a gold band, which is not registered.

In 2026 Ruby, who worked as a barista at a MoonBean shop for two years until 2025, opens a café 500 m from one of MoonBean's shops called "MoonBeam Coffee". Her sign shows a gold crescent moon on a navy background, and she serves coffee in navy cups with a gold band. When asked, Ruby says she "just likes the moon". In its first month, several customers post online: "Didn't know MoonBean opened a new store!" and one says "pretty sure it's not the same owners, but the cups look identical."

Advise MoonBean whether it has any action against Ruby under trade mark law and the tort of passing off. (Do not consider the ACL.)

Answer plan
  1. Issue 1 TM infringement: registered ✅ → deceptively similar (one letter; same moon device; Torpedoes v Thorpedo; evidence of confusion) → same services ✅ → defences (not her own name, not descriptive, no prior use).
  2. Remedies under the TMA.
  3. Issue 2 Passing off (the cup is unregistered, so it needs passing off): misrepresentation of connection (Pacific Dunlop v Hogan; counter: McWilliam's — confusion ≠ enough) → in trade → intention to deceive (former employee, several features copied).
  4. Remedies: damages + injunction.
  5. Conclusion per issue.

MoonBean's claims against Ruby depend on two issues: first, whether Ruby has infringed MoonBean's registered trade marks; and second, whether Ruby has committed the tort of passing off, which is particularly relevant to MoonBean's unregistered cup design.

Issue 1: Trade mark infringement

Issue
Can MoonBean bring an action against Ruby for infringement of its registered trade marks under the Trade Marks Act 1995 (Cth)?

Law
Intellectual property law gives the owner of IP the right to prevent others from copying or exploiting it without permission. A trade mark is a distinctive word, phrase or symbol used in commercial dealings to show a connection between a business and its product (s 17 TMA). It may consist of letters, a word, a name, a device, packaging, a shape or a colour. For example, in Coca-Cola v All-Fect (1999), Coca-Cola's registered contour-bottle shape allowed it to stop All-Fect selling lollies in a bottle of the same shape. Trade marks must be registered to be protected under the TMA, and protection extends to the classes of goods and services for which the mark is registered.

A registered trade mark is infringed if: (1) the owner's trade mark is registered under the TMA; (2) the defendant uses a mark that is substantially identical with or deceptively similar to it; and (3) the defendant uses it in relation to goods or services the same as or closely related to those for which it is registered. A mark is deceptively similar if it nearly resembles the registered mark and is likely to mislead or cause confusion (s 44). In Torpedoes v Thorpedo (2003), the mark "THORPEDO" was held deceptively similar to the registered mark "TORPEDOES" for clothing, despite the different spelling and its link to Ian Thorpe's name.

A defendant may avoid liability if the mark is their own name or place of business; describes the kind, quality or another characteristic of their products; is used in comparative advertising; is used with the owner's implied consent; or has been used since before the owner registered or used it.

Application
Registered. MoonBean registered both the word "MOONBEAN" and its gold-crescent-on-navy logo in 2019 for coffee and café services, so the first element is satisfied.

Deceptively similar. "MoonBeam" differs from "MOONBEAN" by a single letter, and when spoken the two are almost indistinguishable. Similar to Torpedoes v Thorpedo, where a minor spelling change did not prevent deceptive similarity, the small change here does not alter the overall impression. Ruby's logo also uses the same device (a gold crescent moon) on the same navy background as MoonBean's registered logo. There is also evidence of actual confusion: several customers believed that "MoonBean opened a new store", which shows the mark is likely to mislead or cause confusion. Ruby may argue that "beam" and "bean" are different words with different meanings, and that a crescent moon is a common image. However, the combination of a near-identical name and the same logo, colours and layout makes this argument weak.

Same services. Ruby uses the mark for a coffee shop, which is exactly the coffee and café services for which MoonBean's marks are registered. Because the services are the same, MoonBean does not need to show its mark is well known in Australia.

Defences. "MoonBeam" is not Ruby's own name or place of business, and it does not describe the kind or quality of her coffee. MoonBean has not consented. Ruby has only used the mark since 2026, well after MoonBean's 2019 registration, so the prior-use defence does not apply. Her explanation that she "just likes the moon" is not a recognised defence.

Conclusion
Ruby has likely infringed MoonBean's registered word mark and logo.

Remedies
Under the TMA, MoonBean may obtain an injunction stopping Ruby from using the name and logo, and either damages for its lost business or an account of the profits Ruby made using the mark.

Defences are worth marks in IP. Run through the list quickly and knock out each one with a fact.

Issue 2: Passing off

Issue
Can MoonBean bring an action against Ruby for the tort of passing off?

Law
Where a trade mark or get-up is unregistered, the owner may still prevent its misuse through the tort of passing off. A defendant commits passing off if they misrepresent themselves or their product as having some kind of connection with the plaintiff, for example by copying another's trade mark. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant made a misrepresentation, express or implied, that its goods or services are connected with the plaintiff or have the plaintiff's endorsement or approval; (2) the misrepresentation was made in the course of trade; and (3) the misrepresentation was intended to deceive potential purchasers.

In Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989), a shoe advertisement parodied the famous knife scene from Crocodile Dundee. Paul Hogan had not agreed to it, but viewers would assume he had endorsed or was connected with the shoes, so it was held to be passing off. A connection need not be stated expressly; it may be implied from the overall impression created. However, mere confusion, without a belief in a connection, may be insufficient, as shown by the overlapping s 18 case of McWilliam's Wines v McDonald's (1980).

Application
Misrepresentation. Ruby never expressly claimed to be part of MoonBean. However, by using a near-identical name, the same moon logo, and the same distinctive navy cups with a gold band, she impliedly represented that her café is connected with MoonBean, for example as a new branch. As in Pacific Dunlop v Hogan, where viewers inferred an endorsement that was never stated, customers here actually inferred a connection, posting that MoonBean had "opened a new store". Ruby may argue, relying on McWilliam's, that some customers were merely confused. One customer was "pretty sure it's not the same owners". But the test does not require every customer to be deceived, and several customers positively believed there was a connection. This is more than confusion.

In the course of trade. The name, logo and cups are used in selling coffee to the public, so this element is clearly satisfied.

Intention to deceive. Ruby worked at MoonBean for two years, so she knew its name, logo and cup design well. She then opened 500 m from a MoonBean shop and adopted not one but three of its features: name, logo and cups. It is difficult to accept that this was coincidence, and an intention to deceive potential purchasers can be inferred. Against this, Ruby says she "just likes the moon". However, that explains neither the identical cup design nor her choice of a name one letter away from her former employer's. Although the facts do not reveal her private motive, the circumstances strongly indicate an intention to attract MoonBean's customers.

Conclusion
On the balance of probabilities, Ruby has committed the tort of passing off. This is particularly important for the unregistered cup design, which trade mark law does not protect.

Remedies
MoonBean may seek damages to compensate for lost sales and place it in the position it would have been in had the tort not been committed, and an injunction restraining Ruby from continuing to use the name, logo and cup get-up.

Overall: MoonBean is likely to succeed against Ruby both for registered trade mark infringement (name and logo) and in passing off (name, logo and cups).

≈1,250 words. If the exam allowed the ACL, you'd add a third issue under s 18 (Taco Bell tests) — see T6.

5 · Traps

Copyright needs no registration. Trade marks, patents and designs all need registration. Don't mix this up.
An idea is never protected by copyright. The battleground is always material form (spoken, unscripted, a pitch). Use Donoghue + s 10, and consider breach of confidence (Fraser v Thames) for ideas told in confidence.
"Substantial part" is qualitative. Never say "only 5% was copied, so no infringement". Cite Hawkes / EMI v Larrikin.
Fair dealing must be BOTH for a listed purpose AND fair. Selling copies for profit is not "study".
Ownership ≠ authorship: employees' work belongs to the employer (s 35(6)), but only if it was made in pursuance of their duties. Mention university IP policies for academics (the slides do).
TM remedies = injunction + damages OR account of profits (not both). Copyright: s 115 lists damages, injunction and account of profits.
Unregistered mark/get-up → TM infringement fails at element 1. Go to passing off (and ACL s 18 only if the question allows the ACL).
Passing off element 3 in this course = "intended to deceive". Address intention with facts (prior knowledge, multiple features copied).
Defences ARE examinable in IP (Tutorial 2 feedback: "Negligence and IP are two of the areas … that may require [defences]"). Always include a defences paragraph.

COPYRIGHT — writing order
  1. I1 Protection: work (s 32 — literary/musical/dramatic/artistic; or ss 89–92 other subject matter) · original (indep. intellectual effort, low threshold) · material form (s 10 "any form of storage")
  2. Ownership: author s 35(2) · employee → employer s 35(6) (Redrock v Hinkley) · commissioned s 35(5) · agreement s 35(3) · uni IP policy
  3. I2 Infringement: s 31 right exercised (reproduce/publish/perform/communicate/adapt) · substantial part (qualitative) · objective similarity · causal connection
  4. Defences: no substantial part · fair dealing ss 40–43, 103A–104 (research/study, criticism/review, parody/satire, news, judicial/prof. advice) — purpose AND fair?
  5. Remedies s 115: injunction; damages; account of profits
Law opener: "Copyright is the legal right to prevent unauthorised copying of the expression of an idea; it does not protect the idea itself. It is regulated by the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) and is automatic. A creation is protected if (1) it is a work or subject matter other than works; (2) it is original; and (3) it is expressed in material form."
Infringement opener: "Under s 31 the owner has exclusive rights to reproduce, publish, perform in public, communicate to the public and adapt the work. Infringement requires (1) a substantial part copied; (2) objective similarity; (3) a causal connection."
TRADE MARK infringement (s 17 definition): (1) registered under TMA · (2) D's mark substantially identical / deceptively similar (s 44; Torpedoes v Thorpedo) · (3) same / closely related goods/services (class) — unless well known (Eastman v John Griffiths; Virgin v Klapsas).
Defences: own name/place · descriptive · comparative ad · implied consent · prior use.
Remedies: injunction + damages OR account of profits. Unregistered → passing off / ACL s 18.
Registrable? no prescribed sign · graphic · distinctive (Beecham v Colgate) · not scandalous · not deceptive · s 44. Process: search → apply in class → examine → Official Journal → 3-month opposition → register; renew every 10 yrs.
PASSING OFF (tort; TW4 + TW8): (1) misrepresentation (express/implied) of connection/endorsement · (2) in course of trade · (3) intended to deceive potential purchasers. Pacific Dunlop v Hogan. Counter: confusion alone ≠ enough (McWilliam's v McDonald's, s 18). Remedies: damages, injunction.
PATENT: register · manner of manufacture (NV Philips v Mirabella; Grant) · novel vs prior art (Windsurfing v Petit) · inventive (standard, 20 yrs) / innovative (8 yrs) · useful. Infringed: copied/used/exploited without consent; damages may be refused if D was unaware of the patent.
DESIGN (s 5 — overall appearance; register): new + distinctive vs prior art. Infringed: make/import/sell/hire/offer a product identical or substantially similar in overall impression. 5 + 5 yrs.
BREACH OF CONFIDENCE (tort): (1) confidential (not trivial/public) · (2) obligation of confidence, express or implied, e.g. employee (NP Generations v Feneley) · (3) unauthorised (threatened) use. No material form needed (Fraser v Thames). Contract: confidentiality agreement; restraint of trade (must be reasonable).

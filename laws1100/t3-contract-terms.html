① Learn it in 20 minutes

A. Formation (Contract Law 1, Week 5)

A contract is a legally binding promise or agreement. It can be simple (oral, written or implied from conduct, e.g. buying lunch) or formal (must be in writing, e.g. land, deeds). A simple contract has 3 essential elements: (1) Agreement, (2) Intention to create legal relations, (3) Consideration. (Capacity and legality are added in Week 6.)

1 · Agreement = Offer + Acceptance

  • Offer: a proposal by one party to another to immediately enter a legally binding contract. It can be written, spoken or implied from conduct, and made to one person, many people or "the world at large". Objective test: would a reasonable person in the recipient's shoes see it as an offer? Ask whether it was firm, certain, communicated, and made with an intention to be bound.
  • Invitation to treat: an invitation to start dealing, not an offer. Examples: advertisements, goods displayed in shops (Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots: medicines on self-serve shelves were an invitation to treat. The customer makes the offer at the till), auctions, tenders, price lists. Asking for information is not an offer either.
  • An advertisement can be an offer to the world: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893]. The ad promised £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball 3 times a day for 2 weeks and still caught influenza, and said £1,000 was deposited in the bank "showing our sincerity". Mrs Carlill used it and caught the flu. Held: the ad was an offer to the world, not an invitation to treat. Acceptance happened by performing the conditions (communication was waived), and using the ball was consideration. The "sincerity" deposit showed it was not a mere puff.
  • Termination of an offer: revocation is allowed at any time before acceptance, even if the offeror promised to keep the offer open (Dickinson v Dodds (1876)). But if the offeree paid to keep it open (an option or deposit), it cannot be revoked until that time ends (Goldsborough Mort v Quinn (1910)). Other ways an offer ends: rejection, counter-offer, lapse of time, non-occurrence of an event, death.
  • Acceptance: a final and unqualified assent to all the terms of the offer, judged objectively by words or conduct (not by thoughts). Only the offeree can accept. It must be unqualified: a qualified "acceptance" is a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer and makes a new one. It must be made within a reasonable time (Ramsgate v Montefiore (1866): Montefiore offered to buy shares and the company accepted 5 months later. The offer had lapsed, so there was no contract).
  • Acceptance must be communicated, with 3 exceptions: an ongoing commercial relationship (Boyd v Holmes (1878)); unilateral contracts, where performance is the acceptance (Carlill); and the postal rule, where acceptance is complete when the letter is posted (Henthorn v Fraser [1892]).
  • Silence is not acceptance: Felthouse v Bindley (1862). An uncle offered to buy his nephew's horse, saying "if I hear no more … I consider the horse mine". The nephew said nothing. No contract. The offeror cannot impose silence as acceptance.

2 · Intention to create legal relations

  • The parties must intend legal consequences. The onus is on the party alleging the contract. Consideration is usually evidence of intention, but intention must be established separately.
  • Historical view (two presumptions): commercial agreements were presumed to be intended to be binding, and social/domestic ones were presumed not to be. Either presumption could be rebutted. Wakeling v Ripley (1951): Ripley, a wealthy elderly man in Sydney, persuaded his sister and her husband (the Wakelings) to sell up and leave their life in England and move to Sydney to care for him. In return he promised they could live in his house and would inherit it. After a falling-out he evicted them. Held: even though they were family, the promises were serious and the Wakelings gave up so much in reliance on them that the presumption was rebutted. There was a binding contract. (The markers expected analysis of this case in Tutorial 5.)
  • The current approach: Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community of SA Inc (2002). There are no presumptions any more. Look at the agreement on its own merits and consider all the circumstances: the parties' conduct, the context, the language used, the value of the consideration, and the nature of the relationship (social, domestic or commercial). This is very similar to Wakeling v Ripley; the only difference is that you don't start with a presumption.
  • Puffery ("mere puff"): a promise that is clearly not intended to be taken seriously, often found in advertising (e.g. "Red Bull gives you wings"). It creates no contractual obligation. Carlill was argued to be a puff, and that failed because of the £1,000 deposit.

3 · Consideration

  • Each party must pay, or promise to pay, a price for the other's promise. The promisee must give consideration for the promise they want to enforce. Consideration can be money, goods, services, taking on an onerous obligation, refraining from doing something, or a promise to do any of these (Carlill: using the smoke ball as directed was consideration).
  • It need not be adequate (fair), only of some legal value: Thomas v Thomas (1842) (a widow was allowed to stay in the house for £1 a year rent, which was enough).
  • It must be sufficient, which means it cannot be:
    • a vague promise: White v Bluett (1853) (a son's promise to stop complaining was not consideration);
    • past consideration: Roscorla v Thomas (1842). After a horse had been sold and paid for, the seller promised it was "sound and free from vice". It turned out to be vicious. Held: the promise was unenforceable because the buyer's only "consideration" (the price) had already been given before the promise. Past consideration is no consideration. (The markers expected analysis of this case in Tutorial 5.)
    • performance of an existing legal duty: Stilk v Myrick [1809] (sailors promised extra wages for doing the work they were already contracted to do got nothing). Exception: where the promisor gets something extra, a practical benefit, or avoids a detriment, the promise can be enforced: Musumeci v Winadell (1994) (a landlord's rent reduction for a struggling tenant was enforceable because the landlord benefited from keeping the tenant).

B. Promissory estoppel and capacity (Week 6)

Promissory estoppel (PE) is equity's answer when there is no consideration or no completed contract but it would be unjust to let the promisor go back on a promise. Lord Denning in Moorgate v Twitchings (1976): a person who leads another to believe in a state of affairs won't be allowed to go back on it "when it would be unjust or inequitable".

Waltons Stores v Maher (1988): Waltons negotiated to lease the Mahers' premises and required them to demolish the old building and build a new one by January 1984. Waltons secretly told its solicitors to "go slow" and never signed the formal lease. The Mahers started building to Waltons' approved plans. On 19 January, Waltons said it would not proceed. Held (HC): Waltons was estopped from denying a binding agreement to lease, even though no formal contract was ever concluded.

Elements: (1) a legal relationship exists or is anticipated; (2) a clear and unambiguous promise; (3) the promisee relies on it by changing their circumstances; (4) the promisee will suffer material detriment if the promise isn't kept; (5) it would be unconscionable for the promisor to go back on it. Remedies: specific performance, or damages for the detriment suffered (Giumelli v Giumelli (1999), where damages were awarded).

The lecturer warned that PE applies only in a narrow range of situations and is "often applied incorrectly by students". Only raise it where there is a clear promise, real reliance and detriment, and no consideration or no completed contract. If a Contract Law 1-only question excludes it, don't raise it.

Capacity:

  • Minors (under 18: Law Reform Act 1995 (Qld) s 17). Their contracts are enforceable by the minor but not against them, except for: necessaries (goods suited to the minor's condition in life and actual needs, such as food, clothing, education, shelter and medical treatment: Bojczuk v Gregorcewicz (1961)), and beneficial service or employment contracts that are not unfair, oppressive or harsh (Fawcett v Smethurst (1914)). The court weighs the benefit against the detriment to the minor (Hamilton v Lethbridge (1912)).
  • Mental incapacity: the contract is voidable if (1) the person was incapable of understanding the nature of the contract and (2) the other party knew, or ought to have known. The person withdrawing must prove both (Gibbons v Wright (1954)).
  • Intoxication: the same two requirements apply (Blomley v Ryan (1954)).
  • Illegality (an illegal purpose or illegal conduct, e.g. committing a crime or tort, corruption, tax evasion, obstructing justice). Statutory illegality depends on the wording of the statute. Bankrupts have restricted capacity (Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth)). Privity is NOT assessable.

C. Terms of the contract (Week 6): "what exactly did they agree to?"

Term or representation?

A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect, so breaching it is a breach of contract. A representation is a statement made in connection with the contract (true or false) that is not part of it and has no promissory effect. The classification decides your rights and remedies.

Test: what would a reasonable person believe the parties intended about the contractual nature of the statement? Weigh 5 factors:

  1. Language: how strong was the statement? "I assure you…", "I guarantee…" point to a term.
  2. Context: the circumstances, not just the words.
  3. Timing: a statement made immediately before the contract is more likely to be a term.
  4. Maker: relative knowledge and expertise. An expert's statement is more likely to be a term.
  5. Importance: reliance. Did one party obviously rely on it or care particularly about it?

Express terms

Express terms are explicitly agreed, either orally or in writing, at or immediately before the time the contract is made. An unread term still binds a party if it is (a) in a written contract they signed: L'Estrange v Graucob (1934) (Miss L'Estrange signed an order form for a cigarette vending machine without reading the small print excluding all warranties. The machine didn't work and she was still bound. Exceptions: cooling-off periods and non est factum); or (b) brought to their attention by reasonable notice before the contract was made: Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking (1971) (see disclaimers below). Terms raised after formation are not part of the contract.

Online: a clickwrap contract ("I accept" button) is generally binding even if unread. The more active steps required (scrolling, typing), the stronger the incorporation. Browsewrap (terms only behind a hyperlink, no active step) has no clear Australian authority and arguably involves no acceptance and no reasonable notice.

Conditions vs warranties

  • Condition: a major term, fundamental to the contract. Breach allows the innocent party to terminate (rescind) and sue for damages. Poussard v Spiers (1876): a lead singer missed the opening performances through illness. This was a breach of condition, so the producers could terminate and replace her.
  • Warranty: a minor term. Breach gives damages only. Bettini v Gye (1876): a singer missed some rehearsal days. This was a breach of warranty, so damages only and no termination.
  • Test of essentiality: was the promise so important to the innocent party that it would not have entered the contract without it? Associated Newspapers v Bancks (1951): the contract said the cartoonist's comic would appear on the front page of the comic section. The paper moved it to page 3. That was a condition, so Bancks could terminate.
  • Intermediate (innominate) terms, Koompahtoo v Sanpine (2007), are NOT assessable. Don't spend time on them.

Implied terms

Implied terms are hard to establish. A court implies a term (Codelfa v State Rail (1982)) only if it is: (1) reasonable and equitable; (2) necessary to give business efficacy; (3) so obvious it goes without saying; (4) capable of clear expression; (5) not contradictory to any express term. Terms can also be implied by custom or trade usage (Hutton v Warren (1836), a local farming custom implied into a lease), by prior dealings between the parties (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson (1906)), and by statute (ACL guarantees, see T6).

Parol evidence rule (PER)

Where a contract is reduced to writing and appears to be entire, the writing is presumed to contain all the terms. Evidence of earlier or contemporaneous oral agreements cannot be used to add to, vary or contradict it (Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor (1891)). If an oral promise is inconsistent with a written term, the written term generally prevails. Two exceptions:

  1. The contract was not intended to be wholly in writing: it was partly written and partly oral, so oral terms can be proved. A written document existing doesn't mean every term is in it.
  2. Collateral contract: the oral promise is a separate, small, pre-existing contract that induced entry into the main contract. De Lassalle v Guildford (1901): before handing over the signed lease, the tenant was orally assured by the landlord that the drains were in good order. They weren't, and the lease said nothing about drains. The assurance was enforced as a collateral contract. Elements: (1) an oral statement plus a main written contract; (2) the statement was intended as a promise, made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) it is not inconsistent with the main contract; (4) entering the main contract is the consideration. Remedy: damages only. The main contract cannot be terminated, because it is the collateral contract that was breached.

D. Disclaimers / exclusion clauses (Week 6, taught in the W7 workshop)

A disclaimer (also called an exclusion, exemption or limitation of liability clause) is a term stating that a party will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of its contractual obligations. Two issues: (1) Is it a term of the contract? (2) Does it, properly interpreted, apply to this breach?

Issue 1: is it a term? Three routes in

  • Signed written contract → bound even if unread: L'Estrange v Graucob.
  • Reasonable notice before the contract was formed (unsigned documents, signs, tickets). Olley v Marlborough Court (1949): the Olleys booked in at the hotel reception, and the disclaimer was on a notice in their bedroom. It was seen only after the contract was made, so it was not a term, and the hotel was liable for furs stolen from the room. Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking (1971): the ticket from the car-park entry machine referred to conditions displayed inside. The contract was formed at the machine, so the conditions came too late and were not a term. Ask: is this a document a reasonable person would expect to contain terms, and did the business do what was reasonable to give notice?
  • Prior dealings → a previous course of dealing means the customer is taken to know the clause: Balmain New Ferry v Robertson (1906). The workshop exemplar summarises it like this: the notice ("a fare of one penny must be paid on entering or leaving the wharf…") was only visible after payment, but Robertson was bound because he had travelled on the ferry on many previous occasions.

Issue 2: does it cover this breach?

  • In a business-to-business contract, the clause is read neutrally (equal bargaining power is presumed).
  • Contra proferentem (business v consumer): if the clause is ambiguous, read it against the party relying on it, choosing the meaning under which it does not apply: White v John Warwick (1953). White hired a tricycle and the seat tipped forward, injuring him. The clause said "nothing in this agreement shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries to the riders". Read against the owners, it covered only their contractual liability and not their negligence, so they were still liable.
  • Scope: a disclaimer covers only acts within the scope of the contract, not acts outside it: Sydney City Council v West (1965). A car-park attendant negligently let someone take a car (the workshop says "the car keys") without producing the proper ticket. The broad clause ("does not accept any responsibility for … loss … however … caused") did not protect the council, because that act was outside the scope of the contract.

E. Remedies for breach (Week 7 slides, apply to Contract 1–2 answers)

  • Damages: put the innocent party in the position it would have been in had the contract been properly performed. Available for any breach of contract and for breach of a collateral contract. Types: nominal, exemplary, liquidated, unliquidated. The plaintiff must mitigate its loss: Payzu Ltd v Saunders [1919].
  • Rescission / termination: for complete non-performance or breach of a condition (repudiation). Breach of a warranty gives damages only. Rescission is lost through affirmation, delay, third-party rights, impossibility of restitution, or a completed contract.
  • Equitable remedies (where damages are inadequate): specific performance or an injunction. Statutory: ACL remedies if an implied guarantee is breached.
  • Promissory estoppel: specific performance or damages (Giumelli).

Script D · Disclaimer question (mirror of the W7 "High Quality" exemplar)

Whether a disclaimer will effectively protect [Business] from liability for breach of contract depends upon two separate issues: (1) whether the disclaimer is in fact a term of the contract; and (2) whether the disclaimer will be interpreted as applying to the breach in question.

Issue 1

ISSUE
Is the disclaimer a term of the contract between [Customer] and [Business]?
LAW
A disclaimer is a term of a contract stating that one of the parties will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of their contractual obligations. Disclaimers are also known as exclusion, exemption or limitation of liability clauses. A disclaimer will be a term of the contract if it is: (a) incorporated into a written contract signed by the parties; (b) brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract is formed; or (c) implied into the contract as a result of prior dealings between the parties.
Signed documents. A party who signs a written contract is bound by its terms whether or not they have read them: L'Estrange v Graucob, where Miss L'Estrange was bound by an exclusion clause in small print in an order form she had signed without reading it.
Reasonable notice. With an unsigned document, an exclusion clause will be binding only if it was brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was entered into: Olley v Marlborough Court, where a disclaimer on a notice in a hotel bedroom was not a term because the contract had already been formed at the reception desk. Similarly, in Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking conditions referred to on a car-park ticket came too late, because the contract was formed when the customer drove up to the ticket machine. The court asks whether the document is one a reasonable person would expect to contain contractual terms, and whether the business did what was reasonable to bring the terms to the customer's notice.
Prior dealings. If the parties have entered into similar contracts in the past that contained a particular disclaimer, that disclaimer can be implied into the present contract: Balmain New Ferry Co v Robertson. In that case the disclaimer was only visible to the customer after he had paid for the ferry, but the court found it was still a term because Robertson had travelled on the ferry on many previous occasions.
APPLICATION Go through (a) → (b) → (c), in the same order as the Law.
(a) [Customer] [is / is not] bound under a signed written contract because [the disclaimer was / was not in the document she signed]. Similar to L'Estrange v Graucob, [Customer]'s failure to read the terms does not matter …
(b) The disclaimer [was / was not] brought to [Customer]'s attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed, because the first time [she] could see it was [in the change room / on the ticket / after paying]. Like the bedroom notice in Olley, this came only after the contract was made at [the front counter].
(c) However, [Business] can argue the disclaimer is a term because of prior dealings. Similar to Balmain New Ferry, where Robertson had travelled on the ferry on many previous occasions, [Customer] has [been there 20 times in the past year] and so had plenty of opportunity to read the disclaimer and ought to have known of it. [Against: one or two visits / the clause changed / she never saw it].
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, the stronger argument is that the disclaimer [is / is not] a term of the contract, due to [prior dealings / the signed contract / lack of reasonable notice].

Issue 2

ISSUE
Will the disclaimer protect [Business] from liability for [the loss of Customer's laptop]?
LAW
Where a disclaimer is contained in a contract between two businesses, the court presumes equal bargaining power and interprets it neutrally. However, if the disclaimer is uncertain or ambiguous and is contained in a contract between a business and a consumer, the court will interpret it contra proferentem, that is, against the interests of the party that included it or seeks to rely upon it. If there is more than one way of interpreting the disclaimer, the court will choose the interpretation that favours the consumer and does not apply to the breach: White v John Warwick. There, a clause stating that "nothing in this agreement shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries" to hirers of tricycles was read as excluding only contractual liability, not liability for the owners' negligence.
Disclaimers will also not exclude liability for acts occurring outside the scope of the contract: Sydney City Council v West, where the negligent act of a car-park attendant in allowing a person to take a car without producing proof of a ticket was not covered by a widely drafted disclaimer, because the attendant's actions were outside the scope of the contract.
APPLICATION
Contra proferentem. This is a contract between a business, [Business], and a consumer, [Customer]. The disclaimer is ambiguous because it is uncertain whether "[any loss or damage …]" means loss caused by customers themselves or by unavoidable and unforeseen circumstances, or instead loss caused by [Business]'s own careless or negligent actions. As there is more than one interpretation, the court will choose the one that goes against [Business], so it will remain liable for loss caused by its own fault, such as [leaving the key unattended]. Similar to White v John Warwick, general words do not clearly cover negligence.
Scope. [Customer] would argue that the very purpose of the contract was [to store her belongings securely]. This is very similar to Sydney City Council v West, where the purpose of the car park was that only the driver could retrieve the car on producing the ticket. Here, [leaving the keys unattended on a desk so anyone could take them] is a negligent act outside the scope of what a reasonable person would contemplate as occurring within this contract. [Business] may argue that the words "however … caused" are wide enough, but the same was true of the clause in West.
CONCLUSION
The stronger argument is that although the disclaimer [is] a term of the contract, it does not protect [Business] from its own negligent actions. Therefore [Customer] can sue [Business] for [the loss].

Script T · Terms question ("advise under Contract Law 2")

Whether [Claimant] can enforce the statements made by [A] and [B] depends upon: (1) whether each statement is a term of the contract or a mere representation; (2) whether the parol evidence rule excludes it, or a collateral contract arises; and (3) what remedies are available.

T1 · Term or representation

ISSUE
Is [A]'s assurance that […] a term of the contract between [Claimant] and [Defendant], enforceable in contract?
LAW
Contract Law 2 is concerned with identifying the content of the bargain. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect, so its breach is a breach of contract. A representation is a statement made in connection with the contract, which may be true or false, but which does not form part of it and has no promissory effect. To distinguish them, the court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended about the contractual nature of the statement, considering: (1) the language used, since a strong statement such as "I assure you" or "I guarantee" is more likely to be a term; (2) the context in which it was made; (3) the timing, since a statement made immediately before the contract is more likely to be a term; (4) the maker of the statement and their relative knowledge and expertise; and (5) the importance of the statement and whether the other party obviously relied on it.
APPLICATION One short paragraph per factor, with the parties' names.
Language: [A] "assured" [Claimant] that […], a specific, measurable and strong statement, like the lecture example "this machine can process 500 units per hour", which is more likely a term.
Context: […]. Timing: the statement was made [one week before] the contract was signed, i.e. close to formation, which points to a term. Maker: [A] is [head of technology] with expert knowledge, while [Claimant] has none, so [Claimant] was entitled to rely on it. Importance: [Claimant] clearly relied on it; it "provided […] with confidence" and was essential to its business needs.
Against: [Defendant] may argue the statement was only an estimate based on [similar projects], and that the parties chose not to put it in the written contract, suggesting it was not intended to be contractual.
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [A]'s assurance is more likely to be a term than a mere representation, subject to the parol evidence rule.

T2–T3 · Parol evidence rule and collateral contract

ISSUE
Does the parol evidence rule prevent [Claimant] from relying on [A]'s oral assurance, and if so, can [Claimant] enforce it as a collateral contract?
LAW
Under the parol evidence rule, where a contract is reduced to writing and appears to be entire, it is presumed that the writing contains all the terms, and evidence of any previous or contemporaneous oral agreement will not be admitted to add to or vary it: Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor. The rule applies only where the written contract appears to be entire. There are two main exceptions. First, where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, oral evidence may establish further oral terms. Second, the oral statement may form a collateral contract. A collateral contract requires: (1) an oral statement and a main written contract; (2) that the statement was intended as a promise, made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) that it is not inconsistent with the main written contract; and (4) that entry into the main contract is the consideration for the promise: De Lassalle v Guildford. In that case, a landlord's oral assurance that the drains were in good order, given before the tenant handed over the signed lease, was enforced as a collateral contract even though the lease said nothing about drains. Because it is the collateral contract that is breached, the innocent party cannot terminate the main contract but is entitled to damages.
APPLICATION
PER: the written contract [does not mention …], so [Defendant] will argue the PER excludes evidence of [A]'s statement. Exception 1: however, the contract ["lacks detail and is very poorly written"; there is no entire-agreement clause], so it does not "appear to be entire". After [six months of negotiations], the parties likely intended their agreement to be partly oral.
Exception 2, collateral contract: (1) there is an oral statement by [A] and a main written contract; (2) the assurance was a promise made to induce [Claimant] to sign; (3) it does not contradict the written clause "[…]" and is consistent with it; (4) [Claimant] gave consideration by entering the main contract. Similar to De Lassalle v Guildford, where the drains assurance induced the tenant to sign a lease that was silent on drains, here […].
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, the parol evidence rule will not prevent [Claimant] relying on the assurance, which is enforceable either as a term (the contract was not wholly in writing) or, in the alternative, as a collateral contract.

T4 · Puffery (sales talk)

Law: A "mere puff" is a statement clearly not intended to be taken seriously by the receiver, often found in advertising (e.g. "Red Bull gives you wings"), and it does not create contractual obligations. In Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball the advertisement was held to be more than a puff because of the £1,000 deposited to show sincerity. Vague statements of opinion such as "this machine should be perfect for your business" or "I think you'll be very happy with it" are representations, not terms.
Application: [B]'s statements that [the software was "the best on the market"] are exaggerated, unquantifiable sales talk. Unlike Carlill, nothing was done to show sincerity [no research or statistics]. The factors also point away from a term: the language was tentative ("likely"), it was said [at the beginning of negotiations, six months before signing], and [B] was a [junior sales representative] without special expertise.
Conclusion: [B]'s statements are mere puffery and are not enforceable terms.

T5 · The express written term (vague clause) and condition or warranty

Law: Express terms are those explicitly agreed by the parties, orally or in writing. A party who signs a written contract is bound by its terms: L'Estrange v Graucob. ISSUE

LAW
A contract is a legally binding promise or agreement. A simple contract may be made verbally, in writing or by conduct, and requires three elements: (1) agreement; (2) an intention to create legal relations; and (3) consideration.
Agreement. Agreement consists of an offer plus an acceptance. An offer is a proposal by one party to another to immediately enter into a legally binding contract. Whether a statement is an offer is assessed objectively, by asking whether a reasonable person in the recipient's position would believe it to be an offer. An offer must be distinguished from an invitation to treat, which merely invites others to deal; advertisements and price lists are usually invitations to treat, although an advertisement can be an offer to the world at large where it shows a willingness to be bound immediately: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. Acceptance is a final and unqualified assent to all the terms of the offer, made by words or conduct and judged objectively. If acceptance is qualified, it is a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer and replaces it with a new offer. Acceptance must generally be communicated, and silence is not acceptance: Felthouse v Bindley.
Intention. The parties must intend to create legal relations, and the onus is on the party alleging the contract. Traditionally, courts presumed that commercial agreements were intended to be binding and that social or domestic agreements were not, though either presumption could be rebutted: Wakeling v Ripley. In that case, Ripley persuaded his sister and brother-in-law to give up their life in England and move to Sydney to care for him, promising they could live in his house and inherit it. Despite the family relationship, the court found an intention to be bound because of the seriousness of the promises and the sacrifices made in reliance on them. The current approach is to consider the agreement on its own merits, without presumptions, by assessing objectively all the circumstances: the parties' conduct, the context, the language used, the value of any consideration and the nature of the relationship: Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community of SA.
Consideration. For an agreement to be binding, the promisee must provide consideration for the promise received: the price paid, or promised, for the other party's promise. Issue 1: Alex's assurance about 10,000 concurrent users

Issue

Is Alex's assurance that the software could handle at least 10,000 concurrent users an enforceable term of the contract between GlobalCorp and Technovate?

Law

Terms and representations. Once a contract is formed, Contract Law 2 identifies the content of the bargain. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect, so its breach is a breach of contract. A representation is a statement made in connection with the contract, which may be true or false, but which does not form part of the contract and has no promissory effect. To decide which a statement is, the court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended about its contractual nature, having regard to: (1) the language used, since strong statements such as "I assure you" are more likely to be terms; (2) the context in which it was made; (3) the time it was made, since a statement immediately preceding the contract is more likely to be a term; (4) the maker of the statement and their relative knowledge and expertise; and (5) the importance of the statement, including whether the other party obviously relied on it. The lecture examples show that a precise, measurable claim, such as "this machine can process 500 units per hour", is more likely to be a term.

Parol evidence rule. Where a contract has been reduced to writing and appears to be entire, it is presumed that the writing contains all of its terms, and evidence of any previous or contemporaneous oral agreement will not be admitted to add to or vary it: Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor. The rule only applies where the written contract appears to be entire. There are two main exceptions. First, where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, oral evidence may be used to establish oral terms. Second, the oral statement may form a collateral contract.

Collateral contract. A collateral contract is a separate, pre-existing contract formed by a promise that induces the other party to enter into the main contract. The elements are: (1) an oral statement and a main written contract; (2) the oral statement was intended as a promise, made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) the collateral contract is not inconsistent with the main written contract; and (4) entry into the main contract is the consideration for the promise: De Lassalle v Guildford. In that case, before the tenant handed over the signed lease, the landlord orally assured him that the drains were in good order. The lease said nothing about the drains, which proved defective. The assurance was enforced as a collateral contract. Because it is the collateral contract that is breached, the innocent party cannot terminate the main contract but may claim damages.

Application

Term or representation. Language: Alex "assured" GlobalCorp that the software would handle "at least 10,000 concurrent users". This is a strong, specific and measurable statement, very like the lecture example "this machine can process 500 units per hour", and unlike vague opinions such as "this machine should be perfect for your business". GlobalCorp obviously relied on it.

Conclusion

Issue

Are Damon's statements that the software would "likely enhance productivity", was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise GlobalCorp's business operations" enforceable terms of the contract?

Issue 3: the written "industry-standard" clause

Law

Express terms are those explicitly agreed by the parties, and a party is bound by the terms of a written contract it has signed: L'Estrange v Graucob. Once a term is identified, the court determines its importance. A condition is fundamental to the contract, and its breach entitles the innocent party to terminate and sue for damages: Poussard v Spiers, where a singer's absence from the opening performances allowed the producers to terminate. A warranty is a minor term whose breach gives damages only: Bettini v Gye, where missing rehearsals gave damages only. The test of essentiality asks whether the promise was so important to the innocent party that it would not have entered the contract without it: Associated Newspapers v Bancks, where a promise to publish a cartoonist's work on the front page was a condition.

Application

The clause is an express written term binding on Technovate. However, it is vague. Like the front-page placement in Bancks and the opening performances in Poussard, it goes to the heart of the bargain, because software that cannot handle GlobalCorp's users is "not suitable" for its business. GlobalCorp would probably not have entered the contract without it, so it is likely a condition rather than a minor warranty as in Bettini v Gye.

The written clause is a term, but proving its breach is uncertain. The capacity promise, if it is a term, is likely a condition.

Remedies

Damages aim to place GlobalCorp in the position it would have been in had the contract been properly performed, and they are available for any breach of contract and for breach of a collateral contract. If Alex's assurance is a term and a condition, GlobalCorp may terminate the contract and claim damages, including its $250,000 of losses. If the assurance is enforced only as a collateral contract, GlobalCorp cannot terminate the main contract but may recover damages for the $250,000 loss caused by the software's inability to handle 10,000 users. GlobalCorp must take reasonable steps to mitigate its loss, for example by seeking an upgrade or a replacement system promptly: Payzu Ltd v Saunders. No remedy is available for Damon's statements. Issue 1

Issue

Is the disclaimer a term of the cloakroom contract between Melissa and the BCC?

Law

A disclaimer is a term of a contract stating that one of the parties will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of its contractual obligations. Disclaimers are also known as exclusion, exemption or limitation of liability clauses. A disclaimer will be a term of the contract if it is: (a) incorporated into a written contract that has been signed; (b) brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract is formed; or (c) implied into the contract as a result of prior dealings.

Signed contract. A party who signs a written contract is bound by its terms whether or not they have read them: L'Estrange v Graucob. In that case, Miss L'Estrange signed an order form for a vending machine without reading the small print, which excluded all warranties. When the machine failed, she was bound by the exclusion clause.

Reasonable notice. With an unsigned document, an exclusion clause is binding only if it was brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was entered into: Olley v Marlborough Court. There, a hotel's disclaimer was on a notice in the guests' bedroom, which they saw only after the contract had been made at the reception desk, so it was not a term. Similarly, in Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking, conditions referred to on a car-park ticket were not a term because the contract had already been formed at the ticket machine. The court asks whether the document is of a kind a reasonable person would expect to contain contractual terms, and whether the business did what was reasonable to bring the terms to the customer's attention.

Application

Signed contract. Melissa signed the cloakroom contract. The disclaimer appears in a separate piece of paper containing the terms and conditions of that contract, which Melissa did not sign. The BCC will argue that, because the terms and conditions document formed part of the cloakroom contract and was handed over as part of the signing process, Melissa is bound by it as part of the signed contract. Similar to L'Estrange v Graucob, Melissa's decision not to read it ("I don't need to read this") does not help her. Melissa may argue that the disclaimer was not in the document she actually signed. The facts do not say whether the signed contract referred to the separate terms. I assume it did, since the document is described as containing "the terms and conditions of the cloakroom contract".

Reasonable notice. Even if the separate page is treated as an unsigned document, the disclaimer was brought to Melissa's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed. The attendant handed her the terms before she signed and before she paid the $10, and the disclaimer was "clearly visible" in that document. A document headed as the terms and conditions of a contract is exactly the kind of document a reasonable person would expect to contain contractual terms. This is unlike Olley and Thornton, where the terms were only seen after the contract had been formed. Here, the BCC did everything reasonable to give notice, and Melissa chose not to read it.

Prior dealings. There is no evidence that Melissa had used the cloakroom before, so prior dealings (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson) do not need to be relied upon.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the disclaimer is a term of the cloakroom contract, because it was part of the contract Melissa signed or, alternatively, was brought to her attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed.

Issue 2

Issue

Will the disclaimer protect the BCC from liability for the theft of Melissa's fur coat?

Law

Where a disclaimer is contained in a contract between two businesses, the courts presume equal bargaining power and interpret it neutrally. However, where the disclaimer is uncertain or ambiguous and is contained in a contract between a business and a consumer, the court will interpret it contra proferentem, that is, against the interests of the party seeking to rely on it. If there is more than one way of interpreting the disclaimer, the court will choose the interpretation that makes it less likely that the disclaimer applies to the breach: White v John Warwick. In that case, a clause in a tricycle hire agreement that "nothing in this agreement shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries" was read narrowly, as excluding only contractual liability and not liability for the owners' negligence when the seat came loose and injured the hirer.

Further, a disclaimer will not exclude liability for acts occurring outside the scope of the contract: Sydney City Council v West. There, a car-park attendant negligently allowed a person to take a car without producing proof of a ticket. Despite a widely drafted clause excluding responsibility for loss "however … caused", the council was liable, because the attendant's act was outside the scope of the contract.

Application

Contra proferentem. The cloakroom contract is between a business, the BCC, and a consumer, Melissa, so the disclaimer will be read against the BCC. The disclaimer is ambiguous. It is unclear whether "any loss or damage to the property of any of its patrons … however that loss or damage may arise" was intended to cover loss caused by patrons themselves or by unavoidable and unforeseen events, or whether it extends to loss caused by the BCC's own careless or negligent conduct. The clause does not expressly mention negligence. As there is more than one interpretation, the court will choose the one that goes against the BCC. Similar to White v John Warwick, where general words did not exclude the owners' negligence, the BCC will remain liable for loss caused by its own fault: its attendant leaving the cloakroom unattended with the key to Locker 5 lying in view on the front desk.

Scope. Melissa will argue that the whole purpose of the cloakroom contract was the secure storage of her belongings. The cloakroom is described as "a secure place … in which people safely store their belongings", and she paid $10 for that service. This is almost identical to Sydney City Council v West. Just as the purpose of the car park was that only the customer could retrieve the car on producing a ticket, the purpose of the cloakroom was that only Melissa could retrieve her coat from a locked locker. Leaving the cloakroom unattended with the locker key on display, so that a thief could simply pick it up and open Locker 5, is a negligent act that is the opposite of what the contract required. It falls outside the scope of what a reasonable person would contemplate occurring within this contract. The BCC may argue that "however that loss or damage may arise" is broad enough to cover theft, but the clause in West was equally broad and still did not protect the council.

Conclusion

The stronger argument is that, although the disclaimer is a term of the contract, it does not protect the BCC from liability for its own negligent conduct, which was outside the scope of the contract. Therefore the BCC is liable for the loss of Melissa's coat.

Remedies

Melissa may claim damages for breach of contract, which aim to put her in the position she would have been in had the contract been performed, i.e. had her coat been returned safely. She can therefore recover the value of the fur coat. The facts do not state its value, so evidence of its market value or replacement cost will be needed. Melissa must take reasonable steps to mitigate her loss (Payzu Ltd v Saunders), for example by cooperating with the police in case the coat is recovered.

Issue 1

Issue

Was a contract formed between Jack and Mary for the sale of the painting?

Law

A contract is a legally binding promise or agreement. A simple contract may be made verbally, in writing or by conduct, and it requires three elements: (1) agreement; (2) intention to create legal relations; and (3) consideration.

Element 1: Agreement. Agreement consists of an offer plus an acceptance. An offer is a proposal by one party to another to immediately enter into a legally binding contract, assessed objectively by asking whether a reasonable person in the recipient's position would regard it as an offer. An offer must be distinguished from an invitation to treat, such as most advertisements and price lists, which merely invites others to make offers. However, an advertisement may be an offer to the world where it shows a willingness to be bound immediately: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. Acceptance occurs when the offeree indicates, by words or conduct, a final and unqualified assent to all the terms of the offer. Acceptance must be unqualified, otherwise it will be considered a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer and puts forward a new offer on different terms. A counter-offer differs from a mere rejection, which simply refuses the offer. Acceptance is judged objectively and may be made by conduct, but silence alone is not acceptance: Felthouse v Bindley.

Element 2: Intention. The parties must intend to create legal relations, and the onus is on the party alleging the contract to prove it. Traditionally, courts presumed that social or domestic agreements were not intended to be binding, but this presumption could be rebutted: Wakeling v Ripley. In that case, Ripley, an elderly man living in Sydney, persuaded his sister and her husband to leave England and move to Sydney to look after him, promising they could live in his house and would inherit it. When he later evicted them, the court held that, despite the family relationship, there was an intention to be legally bound, given the seriousness of the promises and the significant sacrifices made in reliance on them. Today this is an objective test, and the correct approach is to consider the agreement on its own merits without presumptions. All of the relevant circumstances and the parties' conduct are considered, including the conduct of the parties, the context in which the agreement was made, the language used, the value of any consideration and the nature of the relationship: Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community of SA Inc.

Element 3: Consideration. The common law requires that, for an agreement to be binding, the promisee must provide consideration for the promise received. Consideration is the price paid, or promised, by each party for the other's promise, and it may take the form of money, goods or services, or a promise to provide them: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. It need not be adequate, only of some legal value: Thomas v Thomas.

Application

To determine whether a contract was formed between Jack and Mary, it is necessary to apply the three elements.

Element 1: Agreement. Jack's question, "Would you be willing to sell it to me?", was a mere inquiry, not an offer. Mary's eBay listing at $150,000 was an advertisement and, unlike the advertisement in Carlill, merely an invitation to treat. The offer was made when Mary said, "you can buy it for $140,000": a firm, certain proposal, communicated to Jack, to sell immediately at a stated price. A counter-offer was made when Jack replied, "How about $120,000?" This was not a rejection but a counter-offer, because it rejected the $140,000 offer while proposing a new price, and Mary's original offer was then no longer open for acceptance. A further counter-offer occurred when Mary responded, "Yes. That's a deal. I will deliver it to your address by next week. Is that okay?" Although Mary said "yes", she added a new term about delivery and asked for Jack's agreement, so her response was qualified. Acceptance occurred when Jack nodded and handed Mary a cheque for $120,000. This was acceptance by conduct, objectively showing his final and unqualified assent to the sale at $120,000 with delivery by next week. It was not silence, as in Felthouse v Bindley, but positive acts. Alternatively, if Mary's "Yes. That's a deal" is treated as an acceptance of Jack's $120,000 offer, agreement was reached at that point. Either way, there was agreement.

Element 2: Intention. The correct approach is to consider the agreement between Jack and Mary on its own merits, viewing all the conduct objectively. Mary may argue that, as siblings chatting over coffee at her home, they were in a social or domestic setting and did not intend legal consequences. However, the nature of the relationship is no longer decisive (Ermogenous). As was the case in Wakeling v Ripley, where the court found that family members intended to be bound because the arrangement was serious and significant, the parties here clearly intended to be legally bound. Various facts support this. (1) The value of the consideration was very large, $120,000. (2) The parties engaged in genuine price negotiation from $140,000 down to $120,000, typical of a commercial bargain. (3) Mary used commercial language: "That's a deal." (4) Jack immediately wrote a cheque for the full price, a formal step. (5) The parties arranged delivery to Jack's address by a set time. (6) The painting was already listed for sale on the open market on eBay, showing Mary was dealing with it commercially. (7) Jack is an art collector who buys expensive paintings, so this was the type of transaction he enters into in a business-like way. Just as the Wakelings' sacrifices showed seriousness in Wakeling, Jack's payment of $120,000 shows the seriousness of this arrangement.

Element 3: Consideration. Jack provided consideration in the form of the $120,000 cheque. Mary provided consideration in the form of her promise to transfer the painting and deliver it to Jack's address. This is sufficient consideration. Even though the price was below the eBay listing, consideration need not be adequate (Thomas v Thomas).

Conclusion

A court would likely decide that a contract was formed between Jack and Mary for the sale of the painting for $120,000, including delivery by the following week.

Issue 2

Issue

Is Mary's promise to re-frame the painting enforceable by Jack?

Law

A promise is only enforceable as part of a contract if the promisee has provided consideration for it. Consideration must be sufficient, and past consideration is not good consideration. An act or payment completed before a promise is made cannot be the price of that promise: Roscorla v Thomas. In that case, after the sale of a horse had been concluded, the seller promised the buyer that the horse was sound and free from vice. The horse proved vicious, but the buyer could not enforce the promise, because the only consideration he had given, the purchase price, had been provided before the promise was made.

Application

Mary's promise to re-frame the painting is separate from her promise to sell and deliver it. The contract of sale was complete when Jack accepted and gave Mary the cheque at her home. Mary's promise to re-frame was made later, when Jack phoned her after arriving home. The payment by Jack for the purchase of the painting was made before Mary's promise. Therefore that payment is past consideration and cannot support the new promise. This is very similar to Roscorla v Thomas. Just as the seller's promise about the horse's soundness was made only after the sale had been concluded and the price paid, Mary's promise to put a new frame on the painting was made only after the sale had been agreed and the $120,000 handed over. Jack did not offer any additional payment or anything else in return for the new frame. Ultimately, no consideration was provided by Jack for Mary's promise to re-frame the painting.

Conclusion

Mary's promise to re-frame the painting cannot be enforced, as past consideration is not sufficient consideration.

Advice to Mary

Mary formed a binding contract to sell the painting to Jack for $120,000 and has performed it by delivering the painting by the following week. Jack is not entitled to his money back, as there has been no breach of the contract of sale, and Mary is not legally obliged to re-frame the painting.

The question limited the answer to Contract Law 1, so promissory estoppel (Contract Law 2) is deliberately NOT discussed. Even if it were allowed, Jack suffered no real detriment in reliance. D · DISCLAIMER: writing order

Opener: "Whether a disclaimer will effectively protect [B] from liability for breach of contract depends upon two separate issues: (1) whether the disclaimer is in fact a term of the contract; and (2) whether it will be interpreted as applying to the breach in question."
I1: Is the disclaimer a term of the contract between [C] and [B]?
L1: Disclaimer = a term stating a party won't be in breach despite failing to perform its obligations (exclusion / exemption / limitation clause). It is a term if (a) in a signed written contract: bound even if unread (L'Estrange v Graucob, signed order form with small print, bound); (b) brought to attention by reasonable notice before formation (Olley v Marlborough: bedroom notice seen after contract made at reception, not a term; Thornton v Shoe Lane: ticket terms came after the contract at the machine, not a term. Is it a document expected to contain terms? Did B take reasonable steps?); (c) prior dealings (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson: visible only after payment, but bound because he had travelled many times before).
A1: (a) signed? → (b) when was the contract formed, and where was the sign or ticket? → (c) how many previous visits? "Similar to Balmain, where…"
C1: "On the balance of probabilities, the stronger argument is that the disclaimer is / is not a term, due to …"
I2: Will the disclaimer protect [B] from liability for [loss]?
L2: B2B contract → read neutrally. B2C and ambiguous → contra proferentem, read against the party relying on it. If there is more than one meaning, choose the one under which it does NOT apply (White v John Warwick: "nothing … shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries" to tricycle hirers covered contract only, not negligence, so liable). Scope: no protection for acts outside the scope of the contract (Sydney City Council v West: attendant let a person take the car without a proper ticket; "however caused" clause did not apply).
A2: "The disclaimer is ambiguous because it is uncertain whether '…' means loss caused by customers or unforeseen events, or by [B]'s own negligence…" → "The purpose of the contract was secure [storage]… very similar to West… leaving keys unattended is outside the scope."
C2: "Although the disclaimer is a term, it does not protect [B] from its own negligent actions; [C] can sue for [loss]."

T · TERMS: writing order

T1 Term or representation? Term = a promise, part of the contract, promissory effect → breach. Representation = a statement made in connection with the contract, not part of it. Test: what would a reasonable person think the parties intended? 5 factors: (1) language (strength: "I assure / guarantee"); (2) context; (3) timing (just before contract → term); (4) maker (expertise / relative knowledge); (5) importance / reliance.
Lecture table: "500 units per hour", "guarantee 40,000 km", "rent includes electricity" → term. "One previous owner" → either. "Most popular", "lovely atmosphere", "should be perfect for your business", "you'll be very happy" → representation.
T2 Parol evidence rule: where a contract is reduced to writing and appears entire, the writing is presumed to contain all terms; no earlier or contemporaneous oral evidence may add to or vary it (Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor). If inconsistent, the writing prevails. Exceptions: (1) not intended to be wholly in writing (partly oral; poorly drafted, no entire-agreement clause); (2) collateral contract.
T3 Collateral contract (De Lassalle v Guildford: landlord's oral assurance that the drains were good, given before the tenant handed over the signed lease, which was silent on drains → enforced). Elements: (1) oral statement plus main written contract; (2) intended as a promise to induce entry; (3) not inconsistent with the written contract; (4) entry into the main contract = consideration. Remedy: damages only; the main contract can't be terminated.
T4 Puffery: a statement clearly not meant to be taken seriously (advertising, "Red Bull gives you wings") → no obligation. Carlill: not a puff because of the £1,000 "sincerity" deposit. Signs of a puff: vague, unmeasurable, "best", "revolutionise", "likely", junior staff, early in negotiations, no data.
T5 Express terms: agreed orally or in writing at or just before formation. Bound if signed (L'Estrange) or given reasonable notice before formation (Thornton). Online: clickwrap binds (more active steps = stronger); browsewrap has no Australian case and arguably no acceptance or notice.
Condition (essential) → terminate plus damages (Poussard v Spiers: lead singer missed opening nights). Warranty (minor) → damages only (Bettini v Gye: missed rehearsals). Test of essentiality: would they have contracted without it? (Associated Newspapers v Bancks: front-page placement = condition). Innominate (Koompahtoo) is NOT assessable.
Implied terms (hard to establish), Codelfa: (1) reasonable and equitable; (2) necessary for business efficacy; (3) so obvious it goes without saying; (4) capable of clear expression; (5) not contradicting an express term. Also implied by custom / trade usage (Hutton v Warren), prior dealings (Balmain), and statute (ACL).

F · FORMATION (Contract Law 1): writing order

I: Was a contract formed between [A] and [B] for […]? L: 3 elements: agreement, intention, consideration.
Agreement = offer + acceptance. Offer: a proposal to immediately enter a binding contract; objective test (firm, certain, communicated, intention). Invitation to treat: ads, shop displays (Pharmaceutical Society v Boots), auctions, tenders, price lists, inquiries. An ad can be an offer to the world (Carlill). Revocation any time before acceptance (Dickinson v Dodds) unless the option was paid for (Goldsborough Mort v Quinn). Offer also ends by rejection, counter-offer, lapse, death. Acceptance: final, unqualified assent by words or conduct; qualified = counter-offer (kills the original offer); within reasonable time (Ramsgate v Montefiore: 5 months, lapsed); must be communicated, except ongoing dealings (Boyd v Holmes), unilateral (Carlill), postal rule (Henthorn v Fraser). Silence ≠ acceptance (Felthouse v Bindley: uncle and horse).
Intention: onus on the party alleging the contract. Old presumptions: commercial = yes, social/domestic = no, rebuttable (Wakeling v Ripley: sister and husband left England to care for Ripley on his promise of his house; family, but bound because the promise was serious and they sacrificed a lot). Now: no presumptions; all circumstances: conduct, context, language, value of consideration, relationship (Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community). List 5 or more facts.
Consideration: the promisee must pay a price for the promise (money, goods, services, forbearance, promise) (Carlill). Need not be adequate (Thomas v Thomas: £1 rent). Not sufficient if: vague (White v Bluett); past (Roscorla v Thomas: soundness promise about a horse made after the sale, unenforceable); an existing duty (Stilk v Myrick), unless extra or a practical benefit (Musumeci v Winadell).
A-starters: "The offer was made when… A counter-offer was made when… A further counter-offer occurred when… Acceptance occurred when [conduct]…" / "As was the case in Wakeling v Ripley, where…, it is clear the parties intended to be bound: (1)… (2)… (3)…" / "[A] provided consideration in the form of…; [B] in the form of…" / "The payment was made before [B]'s promise; it is therefore past consideration. Similar to Roscorla v Thomas…"
Promissory estoppel (narrow!): Denning in Moorgate v Twitchings; Waltons Stores v Maher (Waltons told solicitors to "go slow" while the Mahers demolished and rebuilt to its plans, then withdrew; estopped). Elements: (1) relationship exists or anticipated; (2) clear, unambiguous promise; (3) reliance by changing position; (4) material detriment; (5) unconscionable to resile. Remedy: specific performance or damages (Giumelli).
Capacity: minors under 18 (Law Reform Act 1995 (Qld) s 17) are not bound, except for necessaries (Bojczuk) and beneficial employment (Fawcett v Smethurst; Hamilton v Lethbridge). Mental incapacity or intoxication → voidable if incapable of understanding and the other party knew or ought to have known (Gibbons v Wright; Blomley v Ryan). Illegality; bankrupts.

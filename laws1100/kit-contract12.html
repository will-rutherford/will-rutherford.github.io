ILAC A · Formation: was a contract formed?

Issue

Was a legally binding contract formed between [A] and [B] for [the sale of the painting]?

Law

A contract is a legally binding promise or agreement. A simple contract can be made verbally, in writing or by conduct, and requires three elements: (1) agreement; (2) intention to create legal relations; and (3) consideration.
Agreement = offer + acceptance. An offer is a proposal by one party to another to immediately enter into a legally binding contract, judged objectively by asking whether a reasonable person in the recipient's shoes would believe it to be an offer. It must be distinguished from an invitation to treat, an invitation to deal such as most advertisements, goods displayed in shops (Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots), auctions, tenders and price lists. However, an advertisement can be an offer to the world at large: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, where an ad promising £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball as directed and still caught influenza, backed by £1,000 deposited "showing our sincerity", was an offer that Mrs Carlill accepted by using the ball. Acceptance is a final and unqualified assent to all the terms of the offer, judged objectively; a qualified acceptance is a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer. Acceptance must be communicated, and silence is not acceptance: Felthouse v Bindley.
Intention. The parties must intend to create legal relations. Traditionally courts presumed commercial agreements were binding and social/domestic agreements were not (Wakeling v Ripley, where the court found family members intended to be bound because Ripley's sister and brother-in-law gave up their life in England to move to Sydney to care for him in return for a home and inheritance). The current approach considers the agreement on its own merits, with no presumption, looking at the parties' conduct, the context, the language used, the value of any consideration and the nature of the relationship: Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community of SA.
Consideration. The promisee must provide consideration, the price paid or promised for the other's promise (money, goods, services, refraining from something, or a promise to do any of these): Carlill. It need not be adequate, only of some legal value: Thomas v Thomas.

= invitation to treat: "come and make me an offer". Offeror = the one making the offer; offeree = the one receiving it.

ILAC B · Later promise: is it supported by consideration?

Issue

Is [B]'s later promise to [re-frame the painting] enforceable by [A]?

Law

A promise is only enforceable if the promisee has provided consideration for it, and consideration must be sufficient. It cannot be a vague promise (White v Bluett, a son's promise to stop complaining), past consideration, or the performance of an existing contractual obligation. Past consideration is no consideration: Roscorla v Thomas, where, after a horse had been sold and paid for, the seller promised it was sound and free from vice; the buyer could not enforce that promise because the price had been paid before it was made. Performing an existing duty is not consideration: Stilk v Myrick, where sailors promised extra wages for the work they were already bound to do got nothing. However, where the promisor receives something extra, a benefit, or avoids a detriment, the promise may be enforced: Musumeci v Winadell.

ILAC C · Promissory estoppel

Issue

Is [Promisor] estopped from going back on its promise to [Promisee] that […], even though no contract was concluded?

Law

A promise must normally be contained in a contract and supported by consideration to be enforceable. However, equity developed promissory estoppel: when a person, by words or conduct, has led another to believe in a particular state of affairs, they will not be allowed to go back on it where it would be unjust or inequitable to do so: Moorgate v Twitchings. Promissory estoppel allows a promise to be enforced even though the promisee provided no consideration: Walton Stores v Maher, where the Mahers demolished their building and began building a new one to Waltons' specifications for a lease, while Waltons secretly told its solicitors to "go slow" and then pulled out; the High Court held Waltons could not deny a binding agreement. The elements are: (1) a legal relationship exists or is anticipated; (2) the promisor makes a clear and unambiguous promise; (3) the promisee relies on it by changing their circumstances; (4) the promisee will suffer material detriment if the promise is not kept; and (5) it would be unconscionable for the promisor not to keep it. The court may order specific performance or damages: Giumelli v Giumelli.

= estopped: "not allowed to go back on it". Unconscionable: against good conscience, very unfair.

ILAC D · Capacity

Issue

Did [Party] have the legal capacity to enter the contract with [Other], and is the contract enforceable against [him/her]?

Law

Persons under 18 generally lack contractual capacity: Law Reform Act 1995 (Qld) s 17. A minor's contract is enforceable by the minor but not against the minor, except contracts for necessaries, being goods suitable to the minor's condition in life and actual requirements such as food, clothing, education, shelter and medical treatment (Bojczuk v Gregorcewicz), and contracts for beneficial services, such as employment or training that is not unfair, oppressive or harsh (Fawcett v Smethurst), where the court weighs the benefit against the detriment to the minor (Hamilton v Lethbridge). A contract with a person with an intellectual disability or mental illness is voidable if (1) they were incapable of understanding the nature of the contract and (2) the other party knew or ought to have known; the person withdrawing must prove both: Gibbons v Wright. The same two requirements apply to intoxicated persons: Blomley v Ryan.

= voidable: valid until the weaker party chooses to set it aside.

ILAC E · Term or representation (incl. puffery)

Issue

Is [Maker]'s statement that ["…"] a term of the contract between [Claimant] and [Defendant], or a mere representation?

Law

Contract Law 2 is concerned with identifying the content of the bargain. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect, so its breach is a breach of contract. A representation is a statement made in connection with the contract, which may be true or false, but which does not form part of the contract and has no promissory effect. The court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended about the contractual nature of the statement, considering: (1) the language used, as a strong statement ("I assure you", "I guarantee") is more likely a term; (2) the context in which it was made; (3) the time it was made, as a statement immediately before the contract is more likely a term; (4) the maker's relative knowledge and expertise; and (5) the importance of the statement and whether the other party obviously relied on it. In the lecture examples, "this machine can process 500 units per hour" is more likely a term, while "this machine should be perfect for your business" is a representation. A mere puff, such as "Red Bull gives you wings", is not intended to be taken seriously and is neither: unlike the advertisement in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, which was more than a puff because £1,000 was deposited to show sincerity.

ILAC F · Parol evidence rule & collateral contract

Issue

Does the parol evidence rule prevent [Claimant] from relying on [Maker]'s oral statement that […], and if so, is it enforceable as a collateral contract?

Law

Under the parol evidence rule, where a contract is reduced to writing and appears to be entire, it is presumed that the writing contains all its terms, and evidence of any previous or contemporaneous oral agreement will not be admitted to add to or vary it: Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor. If an oral promise is inconsistent with a written term, the written term generally prevails. There are two exceptions. First, where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, oral evidence may establish oral terms. Second, the statement may form a collateral contract, which requires: (1) an oral statement and a main written contract; (2) the statement was intended as a promise, made to induce entry into the main contract; (3) it is not inconsistent with the main written contract; and (4) entry into the main contract is the consideration for the promise: De Lassalle v Guildford, where a landlord's oral assurance that the drains were in good order, given before the tenant handed over the signed lease, was enforced as a collateral contract although the lease said nothing about drains. As it is the collateral contract that is breached, the innocent party cannot terminate the main contract but is entitled to damages.

= parol: oral. Collateral contract: a small side-contract "my promise, in return for you signing the main one".

ILAC G · Express term: condition or warranty?

Issue

Is the term that […] a condition or a warranty, and can [Claimant] terminate the contract with [Defendant] for its breach?

Law

Express terms are those explicitly agreed by the parties, orally or in writing. A term is binding even if not read if it is in a written contract signed by the party (L'Estrange v Graucob) or brought to the party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract is made (Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking). Terms are either conditions or warranties. A condition is a major term, fundamental to the contract; its breach entitles the innocent party to terminate and sue for damages: Poussard v Spiers, where a lead singer missed the opening performances, so the producers could terminate. A warranty is a minor term; its breach gives damages only: Bettini v Gye, where a singer missed some rehearsals. The test of essentiality asks whether the promise was so important to the innocent party that it would not have entered the contract without it: Associated Newspapers v Bancks, where moving a cartoonist's comic from the front page breached a condition.

ILAC H · Implied term

Issue

Is a term that […] implied into the contract between [A] and [B]?

Law

Implied terms are terms not discussed by the parties but included in the contract by legal rules, and are difficult to establish. A court will imply a term only if it is: (1) reasonable, equitable and fair; (2) necessary to give business efficacy to the contract; (3) so obvious that it goes without saying; (4) capable of clear expression; and (5) not contradictory of any express term: Codelfa v State. Terms may also be implied by custom or trade usage, the way things are usually done in a trade or region (Hutton v Warren, where a local farming custom was implied into a lease), or by prior dealings between the parties (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson).

ILAC I · Disclaimer, Issue 1: is it a term of the contract?

Whether the disclaimer protects [Business] from liability for breach of contract depends on two issues: (1) whether the disclaimer is in fact a term of the contract; and (2) whether it will be interpreted as applying to the breach in question.

Issue

Is the disclaimer a term of the contract between [Customer] and [Business]?

Law

A disclaimer (also known as an exclusion, exemption or limitation of liability clause) is a term stating that one party will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of its contractual obligations. A disclaimer will be a term if it is: (a) expressly set out in a written contract signed by the parties (L'Estrange v Graucob, where L'Estrange was bound by small-print exclusions in an order form she signed without reading); (b) brought to the other party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed; or (c) implied as a result of prior dealings. With an unsigned document, a disclaimer binds only if it was brought to the party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was entered into: Olley v Marlborough, where a notice in a hotel bedroom came too late because the contract was made at reception. For tickets, the court asks whether the document is one that would reasonably be expected to contain terms, and whether the business did what was reasonable to bring them to notice: Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking. Prior dealings: Balmain New Ferry v Robertson, where the disclaimer was only visible after payment, but it was still a term because Robertson had travelled on the ferry on many previous occasions.

ILAC J · Disclaimer, Issue 2: does it cover the breach?

Issue

Will the disclaimer protect [Business] from liability for [the loss of Customer's coat]?

Law

Where a disclaimer is in a contract between two businesses, the court presumes equal bargaining power and interprets it neutrally. However, if the disclaimer is uncertain or ambiguous and is in a contract between a business and a consumer, the court interprets it contra proferentem, against the party relying on it; if there is more than one interpretation, the court chooses the one under which the disclaimer does not apply to the breach: White v John Warwick, where a clause excluding liability for "personal injuries" to hirers of tricycles was read as not covering the owners' negligence. A disclaimer also does not exclude liability for acts outside the scope of the contract: Sydney City Council v West, where a car-park attendant negligently let a person take a car without a ticket, and a widely drafted disclaimer did not cover it because the act was outside the scope of the contract.

Filled-in example · 2024 Semester 2 Q2 (GlobalCorp v Technovate)

Question (25 marks). After six months of negotiations, Technovate entered into a written contract with GlobalCorp to create customised software. One week before signing, Alex, head of Technovate's technology department, assured GlobalCorp that the software would handle at least 10,000 concurrent users, based on similar projects for other clients; this gave GlobalCorp confidence in its scalability. At the beginning of negotiations, Damon, a junior sales representative, said the software would "likely enhance productivity" (with no research or statistics), was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise GlobalCorp's business operations". The software handles only 5,000 users, which is unsuitable; GlobalCorp lost $250,000, and productivity did not improve. The written contract lacks detail, is very poorly written, and contains a vague clause promising "industry-standard performance capabilities", but does not address Technovate's statements. Advise GlobalCorp under Contract Law 2. Use ILAC.

Issue 1: Alex's assurance

Issue. Is Alex's assurance that the software would handle at least 10,000 concurrent users an enforceable term of the contract between GlobalCorp and Technovate, despite the parol evidence rule?

Law. A term is a promise that forms part of the contract and has promissory effect; a representation is a statement made in connection with the contract that does not form part of it. The court asks what a reasonable person would believe the parties intended, considering the language used, the context, the timing, the maker's relative expertise and the importance of the statement. Under the parol evidence rule, where a contract is reduced to writing and appears to be entire, oral evidence will not be admitted to add to or vary it: Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor. The rule does not apply where the contract was not intended to be wholly in writing, or where a collateral contract arises. A collateral contract requires an oral statement and a main written contract, the statement being a promise made to induce entry into the main contract, consistency with the written contract, and entry into the main contract as consideration: De Lassalle v Guildford, where a landlord's oral assurance that the drains were sound was enforced although the lease said nothing about drains.

Application. Language: Alex "assured" GlobalCorp of "at least 10,000 concurrent users", a strong, specific and measurable statement, like the lecture example "this machine can process 500 units per hour". Timing: it was made one week before signing, close to formation. Maker: Alex is head of technology with expertise GlobalCorp lacked. Importance: the assurance "provided GlobalCorp with confidence", and 5,000 users was "not suitable" for its operations. On the other hand, Technovate may argue Alex gave only an estimate and the statement was left out of the contract; however, the strength of the words and GlobalCorp's obvious reliance outweigh this.

Technovate will argue the parol evidence rule excludes the assurance. However, the contract "lacks detail and is very poorly written" and does not address any of Technovate's statements, so it does not appear to be entire; the parties likely intended the agreement to be partly oral. Alternatively, a collateral contract arises: there is an oral statement and a main written contract; the assurance was a promise inducing GlobalCorp to sign; it is consistent with the "industry-standard performance" clause; and GlobalCorp gave consideration by entering the main contract. As was the case in De Lassalle v Guildford, where the drains assurance induced the tenant to sign a lease silent on drains, here Alex's assurance induced GlobalCorp to sign a contract silent on user capacity.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Alex's assurance is a term or, alternatively, a collateral contract, and the parol evidence rule does not exclude it. Therefore, GlobalCorp would be entitled to damages of $250,000 to put it in the position it would have been in had the software handled 10,000 users, subject to its duty to mitigate (Payzu Ltd v Saunders); if enforced as a collateral contract it cannot terminate the main contract.

Issue 2: Damon's statements

Issue. Are Damon's statements that the software would "likely enhance productivity", was "the best on the market" and would "revolutionise" GlobalCorp's operations enforceable terms of the contract?

Law. Tentative opinions such as "this machine should be perfect for your business" are representations, not terms. A mere puff, such as "Red Bull gives you wings", is a statement not intended to be taken seriously and creates no contractual obligation, unlike the advertisement in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, where £1,000 was deposited to show sincerity.

Application. "The best on the market" and "revolutionise" are exaggerated, unquantifiable sales talk. Unlike Carlill, nothing showed sincerity, as Damon gave no research or statistics. "Likely enhance productivity" is hedged opinion. The other factors also point away from a term: it was said at the beginning of six months of negotiations, and Damon was a junior sales representative without special expertise.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Damon's statements are mere puffery or representations, not terms. Therefore, GlobalCorp would not be entitled to any contractual remedy for Damon's statements.

Issue 3: the written clause

Issue. Has Technovate breached the express term requiring "industry-standard performance capabilities", and is it a condition?

Law. A signed written term binds the parties: L'Estrange v Graucob. A condition is fundamental and its breach allows termination and damages (Poussard v Spiers, lead singer missing opening night); a warranty gives damages only (Bettini v Gye, missed rehearsals). The test of essentiality asks whether the innocent party would have contracted without the promise: Associated Newspapers v Bancks.

Application. The clause is an express term of the signed contract. There is insufficient information as to the industry standard; assuming comparable software handles 10,000 users, a 5,000-user limit is a breach. Like opening night in Poussard, capacity goes to the heart of the bargain, since GlobalCorp could not operate without it, so the term is likely a condition, unlike the rehearsals in Bettini.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find the clause is a condition that may have been breached. Therefore, GlobalCorp would be entitled to terminate the contract and claim damages of $250,000.

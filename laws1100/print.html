Tort of Negligence: exam sheet

Order: Opening line → ISSUE "Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of negligence?" → Law intro (3 elements; CLA 2003 (Qld)) → 1 Duty → 2 Breach (s 9(1)(a)(b)(c) + s 9(2)(a)–(d) + Bolton) → 3 Causation (s 11(1)(a) but-for / material contribution; s 11(1)(b) scope) PER HEAD OF DAMAGE → 4 Defences (CN; VAR only if relevant) → 5 Remedies ($ calc, − CN %) → Conclusion "On the balance of probabilities…"
Law intro. A tort is a civil wrong allowing the injured person to sue for damages. Negligence = carelessly causing harm to another. P must prove on the balance of probabilities: (1) D owed a duty of care; (2) D breached it (reasonable person standard); (3) the breach caused P's loss, within the scope of D's liability. Then defences and remedies. Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) ('CLA') + common law.
1 DUTY. Donoghue v Stevenson [1932]: snail in ginger beer; manufacturer owed a duty to the consumer despite no contract. Neighbour principle: take reasonable care to avoid acts/omissions you can reasonably foresee would injure persons so closely and directly affected that you ought to have them in contemplation.
Established categories: motorist→road users; doctor→patient; accountant/banker→client; manufacturer→consumer; occupier→entrants (Australian Safeway Stores v Zaluzna (1987)); trespassers (Hackshaw v Shaw (1984)); employer→employee.
Public authority: not automatic. Was there a hidden risk needing a warning/prevention, or was it obvious? Hidden → duty: Nagle v Rottnest (submerged rock, "Swimming area" sign); Swain v Waverley (sandbar between the flags, patrolled). Obvious → none: Romeo v Conservation Commission (cliff top); Vairy v Wyong (natural rock platform, unpatrolled).
No category: (1) reasonable foreseeability; (2) salient features: relationship, control, relative knowledge, experience, vulnerability and reliance, personal responsibility.
Negligent misstatement: (1) business/serious advice; (2) knew/should know P would rely; (3) reliance reasonable (easier if D claims special skill). Hedley Byrne v Heller (careless reference; saved by a disclaimer; duty in obiter); Rentokill v Channon (bad pest report, house demolished: duty + breach). 3rd party: Esanda v Peat Marwick, no duty unless D knew the advice would be passed on, would lead to a transaction, and would cause loss.
2 BREACH, s 9(1) CLA: (a) risk foreseeable (knew/ought to have known); (b) not insignificant; (c) a reasonable person in D's position would have taken precautions. Reasonable person = normal intelligence + the perception/knowledge such a person would have.
s 9(2): (a) probability: Bolton v Stone; (b) seriousness: Paris v Stepney (one-eyed worker, no goggles → breach); (c) burden: Bolton v Stone; (d) social utility: Watt v Hertfordshire (fire rescue, unsecured equipment → no breach).
Bolton v Stone (1951): cricket ball hit out of the ground struck a woman on the road; it happened very rarely in many years; low probability + high burden → no breach. Distinguish: "Unlike Bolton, the risk here was known/reported/likely and cheap to prevent."
3 CAUSATION, s 11(1) CLA (apply BOTH to EACH head of damage):
(a) Factual: breach a necessary condition: "but for" D's carelessness, would P still be harmed? Yates v Jones [1990]: car crash → friend suggested heroin in hospital → addiction caused by the friend, not the crash (break in the chain).
Several causes → material contribution: D's conduct increased the risk and the risk eventuated; need not be the sole cause. Chappel v Hart (1998) (McHugh J); Cook v ACT Racing Club [2001] (overheated sauna → dehydration → heart attack; one cause only, still liable).
(b) Scope of liability: type of harm reasonably foreseeable, not remote: Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961] (oil → fire at a wharf: not foreseeable). Schneider v Smith & Anor (2016): motorcycle crash; illegal drugs taken without medical advice = deliberate voluntary act, not prescribed → no causation for drugs; medical + psych treatment = "ordinary and natural consequences" → recoverable; resignation had other work causes → not recoverable.
Injury types: physical, nervous shock/psychiatric, economic loss, property.
4 DEFENCES. Contributory negligence (partial, apportioned): Ingram v Britten [1994] (tractor at speed; frame unsecured); Manley v Alexander (12 stubbies, slept on the road, run over) = intoxication. Voluntary assumption of risk (complete; very hard): full knowledge + appreciation of the precise risk, freely accepted: Agar v Hyde (2000) (rugby scrum). CLA ss 13–14 obvious risk → presumed aware unless P proves otherwise; s 15 no duty to warn of obvious risk; ss 17–19 dangerous recreational activity. Only if relevant!
Vicarious liability: employer liable for employee acts in the course of authorised work/employment: Century v Northern Ireland (1942); Deatons v Flew (1949).
5 REMEDIES (TW4). Damages = compensation, not punishment; restore P, so far as money can, to the pre-tort position. Ordinary damages assessed once and for all → estimate future losses. Nominal (no loss); aggravated (humiliation/distress); exemplary (deliberate, malicious disregard). Injunction = ongoing torts only.
Heads: medical/hospital · rehab · past lost income (calc) · future earning capacity · pain and suffering → total → × (100 − CN%).
Calcs: 6 mo of $95k = $47,500 · 12 mo of $60k = $60,000 · 25% = ÷4 · 30% = 3×10%.

Write in this order: Opener → (Consumer? s 3) → s 18 → s 29 → Guarantees ss 54/55/56 (+ s 64) → [s 21–22 unconscionable] → [ss 23–27 unfair terms] → [ss 35/36/40] → REMEDIES → [Defences s 207/208]. Opener

Whether [P] has claims against [D] under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL), a Schedule to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), depends upon [four] issues: whether [P] is a "consumer"; misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18); false or misleading representations (s 29); and the consumer guarantees (ss 54–56). Remedies are then considered.

Preliminary — Consumer (s 3)

I: Is [P] a "consumer" who can rely on the consumer guarantees?
L: s 3: consumer if (a) price ≤ $100,000; OR (b) goods of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use; OR (c) goods vehicle/trailer. Wide enough to include businesses not buying for resale — restaurant owner buying cutlery/furniture/food for the menu is a consumer: Star Kingdom Investments v Camatic [2024] FCA 329.
A: [P] paid [$X] < $100,000 → consumer on price limb alone, even though for business. Not resale (uses it in business). [Counter: re-supply? — but…]
Note: needed for ss 54–56 only. ss 18/29 → anyone incl. competitors (Gillette v Energiser (2002)).

s 18 — Misleading or deceptive conduct

I: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 of the ACL?
L — rule: s 18(1) "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". Intention/fault irrelevant. Fine print/disclaimers won't necessarily cure: ACCC v Telstra (2007). 4 requirements:
1 Person — broad; individuals + corporations.
2 In trade or commerce — s 2(1) — within Aust/overseas; includes any business or professional activity, whether or not for profit: Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-op Building Society (1978).
3 Engage in conduct — s 2(2) — doing/refusing to do an act; statement, claim, promise. Silence = conduct if it creates/maintains a misleading impression: Henjo Investments v Collins (1988). Future matters s 4: misleading if no reasonable grounds: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010).
4 Misleading or deceptive — mislead = lead into error; deceive = cause to believe something false: Weitmann v Katies (1977). Objective; need not prove actual deception ("likely to").
Taco Bell — Taco Co of Australia v Taco Bell (1982): Sydney restaurant already "Taco Bell"; US Taco Bell opened in Sydney under same name → customers would think connected → misleading.
Test 1: identify the relevant section of the public (target audience).
Test 2: effect on all within it, "including the astute and the gullible, the intelligent and the not so intelligent, the well educated as well as the poorly educated".
Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979): person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed, but perhaps of somewhat less than average intelligence and background knowledge" — not someone "quite unusually stupid".
Small % enough: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010). Don't use your own view / "reasonable person".

s 29 — False or misleading representations

I: Has [D] made false or misleading representations in contravention of s 29?
L: must not, in trade or commerce, in connection with supply/possible supply/promotion of goods or services, make false or misleading representations: (a) goods of a particular standard, quality, value, grade, composition, style or model, or particular history/previous use (ACCC v Cadbury (2004)) · (b) services of a particular standard/quality/value/grade · (c) goods are new · (e) testimonial · (g) sponsorship, approval, performance characteristics, accessories, uses or benefits (Hartnell v Sharp (1975)) · (i) price · (k) place of origin. No meaningful difference between "false or misleading" and "misleading or deceptive": ACCC v Coles Supermarkets [2014] FCA 634 at [40] per Allsop CJ; Optus v Telstra [2018]. No need to classify fraudulent/negligent/innocent.
Egg Case — ACCC v C.I. & Co Pty Ltd [2010] FCA 1511: cage eggs labelled "free range", later "Fresh Range – Omega 3" with free-range-looking packaging; consumers had no practical way to tell once in carton → misleading + falsely represented quality and characteristics; North J: "high level of dishonesty", "cruel deception" of animal-welfare buyers; penalty to send clear message to industry even though short period.
A: "'[…]' represents the goods are of a particular [quality/composition/history] (s 29(1)(a)) / have [approval/performance characteristics] (s 29(1)(g)); false because… Similar to C.I. & Co, [P] could not detect… As under s 18 (ACCC v Coles)…"

Consumer guarantees — ss 51–64

List: s 51 title · 52 undisturbed possession · 53 no undisclosed securities · 54 acceptable quality · 55 fit for disclosed purpose · 56 description · 57 sample/demo model · 58 repairs & spare parts · 59 express warranties · 60 services due care & skill · 61 services fit for purpose/result · 62 services reasonable time · 64 can't exclude/restrict/modify — term void.
s 54: supply to consumer in trade or commerce (not auction) → acceptable quality = fit for all purposes commonly supplied; acceptable appearance & finish; free from defects; safe; durable — as a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with state and condition (incl. hidden defects) would regard as acceptable. Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas [2015] VCAT 2044; Mitchell v Oz Design Furniture [2016] VCAT 108.
s 55(1): reasonably fit for any disclosed purpose + any purpose supplier represents. (2) disclosed = particular purpose (common or not) made known expressly or by implication to (a) supplier or person who conducted prior negotiations (e.g. agent/salesperson) or (b) manufacturer. Not apply if consumer didn't rely or it was unreasonable to rely on supplier's skill/judgment. (s 61 = services.)
s 56: goods supplied by description must correspond with the description.
Manufacturer (Pt 3-5): s 7 "manufacturer" broad · s 138 strict liability for safety defect · s 9 safety defect = not as safe as consumer expects · s 142 defences. (Injury cases only.)

Unconscionable conduct — ss 20, 21, 22

I: Has [D] engaged in unconscionable conduct towards [P] in contravention of s 21?
L: = unfairly taking advantage of superior bargaining position or another's weakness. s 20 — unconscionable "within the meaning of the unwritten law" (Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio); doesn't apply if s 21 applies. s 21 — in trade or commerce, in connection with supply/acquisition of goods/services, conduct that is "in all the circumstances, unconscionable". Elements: (1) engaged in conduct (2) trade or commerce (3) in connection with supply/acquisition (4) other party not a listed public company (5) unconscionable. s 21(4)(c): may consider terms + manner/extent contract carried out (not just formation). ACCC v Coles [2014] FCA — misused bargaining power over suppliers; "serious, deliberate and repeated".
s 22 factors — no single factor determinative. Procedural (1)(a),(c),(d): (a) relative bargaining strength · (c) could customer understand documents · (d) undue influence, pressure, unfair tactics (age, infirmity, illiteracy). Substantive (1)(b),(j),(k): (b) conditions not reasonably necessary for supplier's legitimate interests · (j) terms of contract / negotiation / performance · (k) unilateral right to vary. (Amadio = procedural.)
A: elderly / grieving / depressed / poor eyesight / limited English / "sign now" / "once-in-a-lifetime" / adviser absent / one-sided exit fee.

Unfair contract terms — ss 23, 24, 25, 27

I: Is clause [X] an unfair term and therefore void under s 23?
L — s 23: void if (1) consumer contract or small business contract, (2) standard form contract, (3) term unfair. s 23(3) consumer contract = goods/services/land to an individual, wholly or predominantly personal/domestic/household — narrower than s 3. s 27 standard form = not individually negotiated; same terms for all; s 27(2) matters (who had bargaining power, prepared in advance, take-it-or-leave-it, opportunity to negotiate). s 24(1) unfair = (a) significant imbalance in rights/obligations (not trivial); (b) not reasonably necessary to protect legitimate interests of advantaged party; (c) detriment (financial or otherwise — inconvenience, delay, distress) if relied on. s 24(2)–(3) must consider transparency (plain language, legible, presented clearly, readily available) + contract as a whole ("possible defence"). s 25 examples (one party but not the other can): avoid/limit performance · terminate (Director v AAPT (2006)) · penalise for breach · vary terms (Director v Trainstation (2006)) · renew/not renew · decide breach/meaning · limit right to sue · limit vicarious liability for agents.
A: "D can terminate any time without penalty but P must pay $[X] exit fee → significant imbalance, s 25 termination/penalty example, as in Director v AAPT; not reasonably necessary because…; detriment $[X]; buried in clause [..] of [35] pages → not transparent."

Opener + general law

Whether [X] can have the [contract/transfer/will] set aside depends on whether [his] consent was vitiated by [A], [B] and [C]. A contract is only enforceable against a party who entered it willingly, with free and voluntary consent. Vitiating factors (misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence, unconscionable conduct) affect genuine consent; the contract may be void (no effect) or voidable (valid until set aside at the innocent party's option).
UNCONSCIONABLE CONDUCT — Law
Unfair, unjust, against good conscience. Harshness alone not enough; equity intervenes where no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). Kitto J, Blomley v Ryan: "special disadvantage… because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage". CBA v Amadio: (1) special disability/disadvantage, no real equality; (2) stronger party knew/ought to know; (3) took unfair advantage — unconscientious to procure in the circumstances.
Amadio: elderly Italian migrants, limited written English, signed unlimited guarantee/mortgage for son's company (son said $50k/6 months); manager brought it to their home, knew of English & company's troubles, didn't explain → set aside.
ACL ss 20–22 adopt this (s 20 = "unwritten law") — only if ACL allowed.
UNDUE INFLUENCE — Law
Controlling influence & power dominating another's will — "where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". Voidable where: (1) pre-existing relationship; (2) controlling influence; (3) stronger takes advantage for own benefit; (4) weaker not exercising independent judgement.
Presumed: parent/child, guardian/ward, trustee/beneficiary, doctor/patient, lawyer/client, religious adviser/disciple (Allcard v Skinner).
Other — prove dominance: spouses, principal/agent, bank/client, accountant/client, employer/employee, carer/patient (Johnson v Buttress). Then onus on stronger party: "pure voluntary and well-understood act".
Johnson v Buttress: elderly illiterate widower, limited intelligence, dependent on Mrs Johnson after wife died, transferred his home to her, no proper independent advice → set aside.
MISREPRESENTATION — Law
False statement of fact inducing contract → voidable. (1) false statement of fact by representor to representee; (2) existing fact/past event, not future intention; (3) before/at formation; (4) intended to & did induce. NOT: opinion, puff, promise, future intention, mere silence (unless half-truth, previously true now false, not corrected, duty to disclose).
Fraudulent — knowingly, without belief, recklessly; induces; damage (Derry v Peek: directors honestly believed tram-rights statement → not fraud). Rescind + damages.
Negligent — special relationship/duty; serious/business matter; knows recipient will act; reasonable reliance; damage. Rescind + damages.
Innocent — no intent to deceive (Oscar Chess v Williams: car honestly said 1948, really 1939). Rescind only, NO damages.
MISTAKE — Law
General rule: no escape (caveat emptor); void only exceptionally. Must be before/at formation, fundamental, a mistake not a misrep.
Common — same mistake by both; conditional on shared belief, belief wrong (Leaf v Int'l Galleries: both thought painting a Constable; 5-yr delay → no rescission).
Mutual — different beliefs (Raffles v Wichelhaus: two ships "Peerless").
Unilateral — serious, fundamental, other knew/ought to know, sought to take advantage (Taylor v Johnson: $15k total vs per acre; buyer exploited → set aside).
Non est factum — encouraged to sign unread; good reason (blind, illiterate, no English) + mistake as to fundamental nature of document → void.
DURESS — Law
Pressure to coerce contract → voidable. (1) express/implied threat of harm; (2) contributed to decision.
Person: Barton v Armstrong (death threats; need only be a reason). Goods: Hawker Pacific v Helicopter (helicopter withheld until paid more). Economic — must be unlawful: North Ocean Shipping v Hyundai (+10% or no tanker; affirmed by delay).
FRUSTRATION — Law
Event not either's fault makes performance impossible/radically different (not just harder/dearer). (1) supervening event; (2) neither caused; (3) contract didn't provide for it; (4) unjust to compel. Taylor v Caldwell (music hall burnt), Krell v Henry (coronation cancelled), Esposito v Bowden (law change), Codelfa v State Rail (conditions change).
REMEDIES — Law
Rescission (equitable): court terminates contract, restores parties to original positions — for misrep, mistake, duress, UI, UC. Bars: (1) restitution impossible; (2) affirmed; (3) delay; (4) third party rights; (5) executed; (6) unconscientious conduct by claimant.
Damages: position as if no breach/misrep; fraud & negligent misrep only — not innocent. Mitigate: Payzu v Saunders.
Equitable: specific performance, injunction.

Agency & Partnership — exam sheet

Writing order: Opener → I1 Partnership? (s 5(1) PA, 4 reqs) → I2 Authority for transaction 1 (express → implied → apparent / s 8) → I3 Authority for transaction 2 → I4 Agent personally liable? Opener

Whether [3P] can recover [$X] depends upon three issues: first, whether [A, B and C] are in partnership; second, whether [A] had authority to bind [the firm] when [purchasing …]; and third, if not, whether [A] is personally liable to [3P].
I1 — Partnership
Issue: Were [A, B, C] carrying on business in partnership under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld), such that each was the agent of the others?
Law: “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit” — s 5(1) PA. No formalities; agreement may be implied from conduct; exists regardless of intention. Each partner = principal + agent of others; unlimited personal liability — Kendall v Hamilton (1879).
  1. Persons ≥2, ≤20 (s 115 CA; prof. exceptions reg 2A.1.01).
  2. Carrying on a business — trade/occupation/profession (s 3); continuity; one-off ≠ partnership Smith v Anderson (1880); starts when they begin working together Khan v Miah [2000].
  3. In common — each acts for the others; employer/employee ≠ partners Degiorgio v Dunn (2004); silent partners still liable.
  4. View of profit — even if loss Minter v Minter (2000).
Facts ✔: joint account · equal profit split · majority vote · shared office · “part of the team” · promo materials · years of trading.
Facts ✘: fixed salary only, no profit share/no say · single project · freelance on the side (weak).
I2/I3 — Authority
Issue: Is [P / the firm] bound by the contract [A] made with [3P] for [item], on the basis that [A] had authority under the law of agency?
Law opener: Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. If A acts within authority, the contract is between P and 3P and A incurs no personal liability. Authority: actual (express / implied) or apparent.
Actual = consensual agreement between P and A alone — Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park [1964].
Express — writing (deed/power of attorney) or oral.
Implied — all things necessarily incidental to express instructions / business efficacy; not found in Peterson v Moloney (1951). Partners: usual things to carry on the business — sell firm property, buy goods of the kind usually used, hire staff, borrow, incur/pay debts; overridden by agreement/express contrary instruction — Construction v Hexyl (1985).
Company officers: director/Chair alone → none; MD/CEO → ordinary course; Secretary → administrative — Giltrap City v Commerce Commission [2004].
Apparent — general (3 elements)
P leads 3P to reasonably believe A has authority → P bound even if A had none / was told not to.
  1. Lack of knowledge — 3P didn’t know A lacked authority;
  2. Holding out by P — appointment to position / past dealings;
  3. Reasonable reliance on P’s representation.
Panorama v Fidelis (1971) — co. secretary hired cars (some personal use); within usual → apparent authority; company bound.
Essington v Regency (2004) — agent varied contract; P did nothing to represent authority → none. A can’t create own authority.
Withdrawal: apparent authority survives until 3P told — Summers v Solomon (1857); Tooth v Laws (1888); P has duty to inform 3P.
Apparent — partners (s 8 PA, 4 reqs)
Every partner is agent of the firm; acts “for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm” bind the firm unless no authority AND 3P knew / didn’t believe them a partner.
  1. Business of the kind carried on by firm — what is the firm’s actual business + what deals such a firm usually does — Mercantile v Garrod (1962);
  2. Done in the usual way — otherwise 3P should suspect — Goldberg v Jenkins (1889);
  3. 3P knew/believed they were a partner;
  4. 3P didn’t know/suspect lack of authority — good faith, no notice — Crouch v IPG (2013).
Partnership agreement binds partners only, not 3Ps.

I4 — Agent personally liable (no authority)

Law: A liable if (1) A claimed to contract on behalf of P; (2) A had no authority; (3) 3P relied and wouldn’t have contracted otherwise — Oliana Foods v Culinary Co [2020]. Also possibly deceit, negligence, s 18 ACL.
A: signed “on behalf of [firm]” / used firm card → (1); Issue 2 → (2); [3P] wouldn’t have [built $180k stage] for [A] personally → (3). Against: A disclosed he needed approval → no reliance.
Agency of necessity (only if emergency): A has P’s property · property under threat · can’t contact P · good faith. Springer v GWR [1921] — failed: could have contacted P.

Capacity: P needs full capacity; A needn’t (even a minor).
Outcome: privity between P and 3P if agency disclosed + A within actual/apparent authority; A not party, not liable.
P liable for A’s torts? Employee → likely vicarious (within instructions); independent contractor → unlikely.
Fiduciary duties — agent → principal
Law: Agents owe fiduciary duties to P from the relationship of trust and confidence; often also contract terms.
  • Follow lawful instructions — Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830)
  • Due care, skill, diligence (else negligence) — Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991)
  • Act in person (no sub-agent) — John McCann v Pow (1975) (agent appointed another agent to sell → breach)
  • Act in P’s interests; no secret profit/commission/bribe — Hewson v Sydney (1968)
  • Communicate relevant info — John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002)
  • Confidentiality · keep accounts, keep P’s money separate
P’s duties to A: pay only if agreed · indemnify/reimburse expenses within instructions · A has lien if unpaid.
Fiduciary duties — partners ↔ partners
  • No personal profit from position/info — Chan v Zaccharia (1984)
  • No undisclosed conflict
  • Full disclosure — Law v Law (1905); s 31 PA
  • Account for private profits — Birtchnell v Equity (1929); s 32 PA
  • Don’t compete with firm — s 33 PA

D · DISCLAIMER: writing order

Opener: "Whether a disclaimer will effectively protect [B] from liability for breach of contract depends upon two separate issues: (1) whether the disclaimer is in fact a term of the contract; and (2) whether it will be interpreted as applying to the breach in question."
I1: Is the disclaimer a term of the contract between [C] and [B]?
L1: Disclaimer = a term stating a party won't be in breach despite failing to perform its obligations (exclusion / exemption / limitation clause). It is a term if (a) in a signed written contract: bound even if unread (L'Estrange v Graucob, signed order form with small print, bound); (b) brought to attention by reasonable notice before formation (Olley v Marlborough: bedroom notice seen after contract made at reception, not a term; Thornton v Shoe Lane: ticket terms came after the contract at the machine, not a term. Is it a document expected to contain terms? Did B take reasonable steps?); (c) prior dealings (Balmain New Ferry v Robertson: visible only after payment, but bound because he had travelled many times before).
A1: (a) signed? → (b) when was the contract formed, and where was the sign or ticket? → (c) how many previous visits? "Similar to Balmain, where…"
C1: "On the balance of probabilities, the stronger argument is that the disclaimer is / is not a term, due to …"
I2: Will the disclaimer protect [B] from liability for [loss]?
L2: B2B contract → read neutrally. B2C and ambiguous → contra proferentem, read against the party relying on it. If there is more than one meaning, choose the one under which it does NOT apply (White v John Warwick: "nothing … shall render the owners liable for any personal injuries" to tricycle hirers covered contract only, not negligence, so liable). Scope: no protection for acts outside the scope of the contract (Sydney City Council v West: attendant let a person take the car without a proper ticket; "however caused" clause did not apply).
A2: "The disclaimer is ambiguous because it is uncertain whether '…' means loss caused by customers or unforeseen events, or by [B]'s own negligence…" → "The purpose of the contract was secure [storage]… very similar to West… leaving keys unattended is outside the scope."
C2: "Although the disclaimer is a term, it does not protect [B] from its own negligent actions; [C] can sue for [loss]."

T · TERMS: writing order

T1 Term or representation? Term = a promise, part of the contract, promissory effect → breach. Representation = a statement made in connection with the contract, not part of it. Test: what would a reasonable person think the parties intended? 5 factors: (1) language (strength: "I assure / guarantee"); (2) context; (3) timing (just before contract → term); (4) maker (expertise / relative knowledge); (5) importance / reliance.
Lecture table: "500 units per hour", "guarantee 40,000 km", "rent includes electricity" → term. "One previous owner" → either. "Most popular", "lovely atmosphere", "should be perfect for your business", "you'll be very happy" → representation.
T2 Parol evidence rule: where a contract is reduced to writing and appears entire, the writing is presumed to contain all terms; no earlier or contemporaneous oral evidence may add to or vary it (Mercantile Bank of Sydney v Taylor). If inconsistent, the writing prevails. Exceptions: (1) not intended to be wholly in writing (partly oral; poorly drafted, no entire-agreement clause); (2) collateral contract.
T3 Collateral contract (De Lassalle v Guildford: landlord's oral assurance that the drains were good, given before the tenant handed over the signed lease, which was silent on drains → enforced). Elements: (1) oral statement plus main written contract; (2) intended as a promise to induce entry; (3) not inconsistent with the written contract; (4) entry into the main contract = consideration. Remedy: damages only; the main contract can't be terminated.
T4 Puffery: a statement clearly not meant to be taken seriously (advertising, "Red Bull gives you wings") → no obligation. Carlill: not a puff because of the £1,000 "sincerity" deposit. Signs of a puff: vague, unmeasurable, "best", "revolutionise", "likely", junior staff, early in negotiations, no data.
T5 Express terms: agreed orally or in writing at or just before formation. Bound if signed (L'Estrange) or given reasonable notice before formation (Thornton). Online: clickwrap binds (more active steps = stronger); browsewrap has no Australian case and arguably no acceptance or notice.
Condition (essential) → terminate plus damages (Poussard v Spiers: lead singer missed opening nights). Warranty (minor) → damages only (Bettini v Gye: missed rehearsals). Test of essentiality: would they have contracted without it? (Associated Newspapers v Bancks: front-page placement = condition). Innominate (Koompahtoo) is NOT assessable.
Implied terms (hard to establish), Codelfa: (1) reasonable and equitable; (2) necessary for business efficacy; (3) so obvious it goes without saying; (4) capable of clear expression; (5) not contradicting an express term. Also implied by custom / trade usage (Hutton v Warren), prior dealings (Balmain), and statute (ACL).

F · FORMATION (Contract Law 1): writing order

I: Was a contract formed between [A] and [B] for […]? L: 3 elements: agreement, intention, consideration.
Agreement = offer + acceptance. Offer: a proposal to immediately enter a binding contract; objective test (firm, certain, communicated, intention). Invitation to treat: ads, shop displays (Pharmaceutical Society v Boots), auctions, tenders, price lists, inquiries. An ad can be an offer to the world (Carlill). Revocation any time before acceptance (Dickinson v Dodds) unless the option was paid for (Goldsborough Mort v Quinn). Offer also ends by rejection, counter-offer, lapse, death. Acceptance: final, unqualified assent by words or conduct; qualified = counter-offer (kills the original offer); within reasonable time (Ramsgate v Montefiore: 5 months, lapsed); must be communicated, except ongoing dealings (Boyd v Holmes), unilateral (Carlill), postal rule (Henthorn v Fraser). Silence ≠ acceptance (Felthouse v Bindley: uncle and horse).
Intention: onus on the party alleging the contract. Old presumptions: commercial = yes, social/domestic = no, rebuttable (Wakeling v Ripley: sister and husband left England to care for Ripley on his promise of his house; family, but bound because the promise was serious and they sacrificed a lot). Now: no presumptions; all circumstances: conduct, context, language, value of consideration, relationship (Ermogenous v Greek Orthodox Community). List 5 or more facts.
Consideration: the promisee must pay a price for the promise (money, goods, services, forbearance, promise) (Carlill). Need not be adequate (Thomas v Thomas: £1 rent). Not sufficient if: vague (White v Bluett); past (Roscorla v Thomas: soundness promise about a horse made after the sale, unenforceable); an existing duty (Stilk v Myrick), unless extra or a practical benefit (Musumeci v Winadell).
A-starters: "The offer was made when… A counter-offer was made when… A further counter-offer occurred when… Acceptance occurred when [conduct]…" / "As was the case in Wakeling v Ripley, where…, it is clear the parties intended to be bound: (1)… (2)… (3)…" / "[A] provided consideration in the form of…; [B] in the form of…" / "The payment was made before [B]'s promise; it is therefore past consideration. Similar to Roscorla v Thomas…"
Promissory estoppel (narrow!): Denning in Moorgate v Twitchings; Waltons Stores v Maher (Waltons told solicitors to "go slow" while the Mahers demolished and rebuilt to its plans, then withdrew; estopped). Elements: (1) relationship exists or anticipated; (2) clear, unambiguous promise; (3) reliance by changing position; (4) material detriment; (5) unconscionable to resile. Remedy: specific performance or damages (Giumelli).
Capacity: minors under 18 (Law Reform Act 1995 (Qld) s 17) are not bound, except for necessaries (Bojczuk) and beneficial employment (Fawcett v Smethurst; Hamilton v Lethbridge). Mental incapacity or intoxication → voidable if incapable of understanding and the other party knew or ought to have known (Gibbons v Wright; Blomley v Ryan). Illegality; bankrupts.

Copy-ready Law paragraphs (short form)

Trespass (general): Trespass is an intentional tort committed when D interferes with the person or property of P: to land, goods or the person. It is actionable per se, so P need not prove damage. It requires an interference that is direct, intentional or negligent, and without consent or lawful justification.
Land: D must interfere with P’s exclusive possession of land by entering, remaining on or putting something on it, including airspace. In Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco a sign protruding 4 inches into P’s airspace was a trespass, and an injunction was granted.
Goods: Interference with goods in P’s possession, e.g. taking or using them without permission. P need only have rightful possession, not ownership, and need not prove damage.
Battery: Physical interference with P’s body. “The direct intentional imposition of any unwanted physical contact… constitutes… Battery”; no harm is required (Battiato v Lagana). Contact “generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of everyday life” is excepted (In re F). In Rixon v Star City a hand on a banned patron’s shoulder to get his attention was not battery.
Assault: Causing a reasonable apprehension of direct, imminent, harmful or offensive contact. No contact is needed. Actual fear is immaterial, otherwise it would depend on whether P was courageous or timid (Brady v Schatzel). A conditional threat still counts (Rozsa v Samuels). Advancing with a clenched fist was assault in Stephens v Myers.
False imprisonment: Total restraint with no reasonable avenue of escape. In Symes v Mahon P accompanied police on a mistaken warrant and was falsely imprisoned (no force needed). In Bird v Jones P blocked from one side of a bridge was merely obstructed: escape must be reasonable, without risk of injury or serious inconvenience. In Burton v Davies the only escape was jumping from a moving truck, so P was imprisoned.
Private nuisance: D’s act indirectly interferes with P’s use and enjoyment of private land (unlike trespass, which is direct). P needs an interest in the land, actual harm, intentional or reckless interference, and interference that is sustained and unreasonable. The court balances undisturbed enjoyment against D’s right to undertake activities, considering severity, duration, location, sensitivity, deliberateness or malice, and precautions.
Defamation: Publishing to a third party a statement about P that would damage their reputation. P must prove it was (1) defamatory: esteem diminished (Radio 2UE v Chesterton); in World Hosts an implication that a restaurant owner was bankrupt was enough; (2) identified P (need not be named); (3) published (any form; failure to remove can create liability). Defences: justification, absolute privilege (s 26), fair reporting, honest opinion (s 31) (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld)).
Deceit: A fraudulent misrepresentation. D makes a statement of fact knowing it is false, intending reliance; P relies on it and suffers harm. An opinion honestly and reasonably held is not deceit (Bisset v Wilkinson: the sheep-capacity statement was not deceit).
Passing off: D misrepresents (expressly or impliedly) a connection with or endorsement by P, in the course of trade, intending to deceive purchasers (Pacific Dunlop v Hogan: an implied Crocodile Dundee association was enough). Overlaps s 18 ACL and unregistered trade marks (T5).

Remedies (always finish here)

Damages aim to restore P, so far as money can, to the position had the tort not occurred (compensation, not punishment). Ordinary: assessed once and for all, so estimate future losses. Nominal: right breached, no loss (e.g. trespass to land). Aggravated: humiliation or emotional distress (e.g. defamation, public detention). Exemplary: D deliberately and maliciously disregarded P’s rights. Injunction: order to do or refrain; appropriate for ongoing nuisance, trespass, defamation; breach brings civil or criminal penalties. Interim injunction: restrains until final decision. Passing off (W8): injunction + damages.

Competition law — CCA Part IV — exam sheet

Opener (Law): "Australia's competition laws are primarily contained in Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), enforced by the ACCC. Its objective is to prohibit trade practices that substantially lessen competition. Some conduct is prohibited per se (cartel conduct, misuse of market power, RPM); other conduct only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market — 'the area of close competition … the field of rivalry' (Re QCMA (1976))."
SLC — 4 steps: (1) define market (complainant → narrow; defendant → wide) · (2) did it / is it likely to lessen competition? · (3) substantial? (large, weighty, real, of substance) · (4) if not, was the PURPOSE to SLC?
PER SE: cartel · s 46 · RPM s 48. CONDITIONAL (SLC): secondary boycott · exclusive dealing s 47 · mergers s 50 · s 45 agreements.
ACCC authorisation (on application; public benefit > detriment): all EXCEPT misuse of market power.
CARTEL (s 45AD; civil ss 45AJ/45AK; criminal ss 45AF/45AG): contract/arrangement/understanding between competitors that (a) fixes prices, (b) restricts output, (c) rigs bids, (d) allocates customers/suppliers/territories. TPC v TNT; ACCC v Visy; CDPP v NYK [2017] (freight rates, bid rigging, customer allocation; $25m; first criminal conviction).
Price fixing: (1) CAU with competitor(s); (2) purpose/likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices (ACCC v Alice). Formula, data exchange, output cuts, same discounts all count. Informal is enough. Counter: parallel conduct / price leadership ≠ collusion (TPC v Email).
MISUSE OF MARKET POWER s 46: substantial market power → take advantage → eliminate/substantially damage competitor or prevent entry (ACCC v Ticketek [2011]). Not authorisable. Predatory pricing: Boral (C&M Bricks) — HC: no misuse; no substantial power + no intention to recoup. 2017: legit competition OK even if inefficient rivals are hurt; add the post-2017 "purpose/effect of SLC" line.
RPM s 48 (ss 96–100) — supplier → reseller: (1) minimum price · (2) price understood as a minimum · (3) no advertising below $X · (4) induce not to discount · (5) threaten/refuse supply to force it. TPC v Sony (1990); ACCC v Chaste (2005). Per se; illegal since 1971.
BOYCOTTS (ss 45D, 45DA, 45DB, 45DD): Primary (s 45) A+B refuse C — TPC v JW Bryant; ACCC v AMA (WA) (2001): collective fee negotiation + threat to withdraw doctors' services = price fixing + primary boycott; $285k. Secondary A+B pressure C → C drops D — ACCC v CFMEU (2017–18); harder to prove.
EXCLUSIVE DEALING s 47 (SLC needed): restricts with whom/how much/where. Full-line: supply only if the buyer won't buy from/resupply a competitor or resupply to a place (TPC v Massey). Third-line s 47(6): (1) wanted product + forced product; (2) the forced one is a third party's; (3) can't get one without the other; (4) SLC (ACCC v Black & White Cabs [2010]; travel insurance / Cannon Investments).
MERGERS s 50: effect/likely effect of SLC in a substantial market; s 50(3) factors; benefits (scale, risk, replacing poor performers); go to the ACCC first. s 45(1)(a): competitor deal intended/likely to SLC (ACCC v Visy); a CAU is enough.
PENALTIES: fine s 76 · injunction s 80 · damages s 82 · compensation s 79B · probation/community service · corrective ads s 86C(2)(d) · adverse publicity s 86D · rescission/variation/specific performance s 87 · undertakings/declaration s 87B. Fine factors: nature & extent; loss caused; circumstances; prior conduct. Max (corp) = greatest of $50m / 3× benefit / 30% turnover (if benefit unknown). Individual: $2.5m. Cartels: criminal too.

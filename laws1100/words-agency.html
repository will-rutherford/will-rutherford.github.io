Issue 1: Is there a partnership?

What this means: a partnership is two or more people running a business together to make money. It does NOT need a written agreement or registration — if the 4 requirements are met, it exists, even if they never called it one. Why it matters: in a partnership every partner is the agent of the others, so what one partner signs can bind all of them, and each partner is personally liable for the firm's debts (they can lose their own house/savings = unlimited personal liability).

WRITE — Issue

Were [A, B and C] carrying on [business name] in partnership under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld), such that each was the agent of the others?

WRITE — Law (copy all four requirement sentences)

A partnership is "the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit": Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) ('PA') s 5(1). No registration or formalities are required, and the partnership agreement may be written, oral or wholly implied from the parties' conduct; a partnership exists whenever the four requirements are satisfied, regardless of the parties' intention. Each partner is both a principal and an agent of the other partners, and each has unlimited personal liability for the firm's debts: Kendall v Hamilton (1879).

First, there must be at least two persons, and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115.
Second, they must be carrying on a business, which includes any trade, occupation or profession (PA s 3) and requires some continuity or repetition of activity; a one-off project is usually not a partnership: Smith v Anderson (1880). A partnership begins from the date the parties begin working together to establish an identifiable business: Khan v Miah [2000].
Third, the business must be carried on in common, meaning each person acts on behalf of the others as well as themselves. An employer and employee carry on business together but are not partners, because the employer does not act on behalf of the employee: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Even silent partners count and are liable.
Fourth, the business must be carried on with a view of profit; it remains a partnership even if a loss is expected or made: Minter v Minter (2000).

Issue 2: Did [A] have authority to make the [purchase / contract]?

What this means: authority = permission to make deals that bind the principal. If the agent had authority, the deal is between the principal and the third party (the principal pays; the agent doesn't). There are 3 kinds — always go through ALL THREE in this order: express actual (P actually told A "you may do this", in writing or out loud), implied actual (things that naturally come with the job P gave A), apparent (P made the outsider reasonably believe A had permission, even if A didn't). Two purchases = do this issue twice (usually one is authorised and one isn't).

WRITE — Issue

Is [P / the firm and its partners] bound by the contract [A] made with [3P] for [the item], on the basis that [A] had authority under the law of agency?

WRITE — Law

Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability. Authority may be actual — express or implied — or apparent.

Actual authority "is a legal relationship between principal and agent created by a consensual agreement to which they alone are parties": Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express actual authority is given by the principal in writing (including by deed, such as a power of attorney) or orally. Implied actual authority extends to all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the express instructions, or needed to give them business efficacy; it is not found merely because it would be convenient: Peterson v Moloney (1951).

Apparent authority arises where the principal leads a third party to reasonably believe the agent has authority; the principal is then bound even if the agent had no actual authority, and even if the principal expressly prohibited the act. Three requirements must be met: (1) the third party did not know the agent lacked actual authority; (2) the principal held the agent out as having authority, for example by appointing them to a position or allowing them to act in the past; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on that representation. In Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), a company secretary hired cars in the company's name, partly for personal use; the company was bound because hiring cars fell within a secretary's usual, and therefore apparent, authority. The representation must come from the principal, not the agent: in Essington v Regency (2004) there was no apparent authority to vary a contract because the principal had done nothing to represent that the agent could do so.

Partnership question? Add this sentence to the Law: "Each partner has implied authority to do the usual things necessary to carry on the firm's business, such as buying goods of the kind usually employed in that business, borrowing money and incurring debts; but implied authority can be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985)." Then for apparent authority use the s 8 four requirements in the next heading.

Issue 2 (partners): Apparent authority of a partner — s 8 PA

What this means: when the agent is a partner, apparent authority is tested using the Partnership Act s 8, which has four requirements (use these instead of the general three). Even if the other partners never gave permission, the firm is bound if the deal was the normal kind of deal for that business, done in the normal way, with someone who thought they were dealing with a partner and didn't know about any lack of permission. A secret internal rule (e.g. "needs another partner's OK") only binds the partners, not outsiders — unless the outsider knew about it.

WRITE — Law

Under s 8 PA, every partner is an agent of the firm, and acts done "for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm" bind the firm and the partners, unless the partner had no authority and the third party knew this, or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Accordingly, where a partner lacks actual authority, the firm is still bound if four requirements are met.
First, the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm, judged by what the firm's usual business actually is and what transactions a firm of that kind would usually engage in: Mercantile v Garrod (1962).
Second, the business was carried on in the usual way, because otherwise the third party should suspect a lack of authority: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889).
Third, the third party knew or believed the person was a partner.
Fourth, the third party did not know or suspect the lack of authority, relying in good faith and without notice: Crouch v IPG (2013).
The partnership agreement binds only the partners, not third parties.

Issue 3: Who is liable?

What this means: after authority, say who actually has to pay. If there WAS authority → the principal pays (in a partnership, all partners together, from their own pockets). If there was NO authority → the principal walks away, but the outsider can sue the agent personally for breach of warranty of authority = the agent effectively promised "I'm allowed to do this" and that promise was false.

WRITE — Issue (only for a deal with NO authority)

Can [3P] hold [A] personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

WRITE — Law

Where an agent lacks authority, the agent is personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed to make the contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent did not in fact have authority; and (3) the third party relied on the representation and would not have contracted without it: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]. The agent may also be liable in deceit or negligence, or for misleading conduct under s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law.

Issue 4: Did [A] breach fiduciary duties owed to [P / the other partners]?

What this means: fiduciary = a relationship of trust where one person must put the other's interests first. An agent owes these duties to the principal; partners owe them to each other. Use this heading when the principal is your client (e.g. "advise Cristina") or a partner misbehaved. These duties come from equity (judge-made fairness rules) and are often also terms of the agency contract — so a breach is usually a breach of contract too.

Full mini-answer — 2025 S2 Q2: StageCorp v CT Events (all blanks filled)

Whether StageCorp Pty Ltd ('StageCorp') can recover the remaining $162,000 depends upon three issues: first, whether Callum, Tessa and Robert carry on CT Events in partnership; second, whether Robert had authority to bind CT Events and its partners to the stage contract; and third, if not, whether Robert is personally liable to StageCorp. As CT Events operates in Sydney, NSW legislation would technically apply; this answer applies the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) ('PA'), assuming the provisions are equivalent.

Issue 1: Partnership

Issue: Were Callum, Tessa and Robert carrying on CT Events in partnership under the PA, such that each was the agent of the others?

Law: A partnership is "the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit": PA s 5(1). No formalities are required and the agreement may be implied from conduct. Each partner is principal and agent of the others, with unlimited personal liability: Kendall v Hamilton (1879). First, there must be at least two persons and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115. Second, they must be carrying on a business with continuity; a one-off project is usually not enough: Smith v Anderson (1880); a partnership begins when the parties begin working together in an identifiable business: Khan v Miah [2000]. Third, the business must be carried on in common; an employer and employee are not partners: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Fourth, there must be a view of profit: Minter v Minter (2000).

Application: There are three persons, within the limit of 20. CT Events has operated since mid-2022 with a website, social media presence and expanding client base; unlike Smith v Anderson, this is a continuing trade. Consistent with Khan v Miah, Robert became a partner when he began working in the business in early 2023, despite no formal agreement. The three share an office, a joint account, split profits equally and decide by majority vote, and Robert was introduced to clients "as part of the team" — each acting on behalf of the others. Callum and Tessa may argue that each person's freelance work shows they were not truly in business together; however, unlike the employee in Degiorgio v Dunn, Robert shares profits equally, uses the joint account and has an equal vote. Profits are split equally, so there is a view of profit (Minter v Minter).

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Callum, Tessa and Robert are partners under s 5(1) PA, so Robert is an agent of the firm and of Callum and Tessa.

Issue 2: Robert's authority to buy the stage

Issue: Are CT Events and its partners bound by the $180,000 contract Robert signed with StageCorp, on the basis that Robert had authority under the law of agency?

Law: Agency is a relationship in which an agent is authorised to act for a principal in dealings with a third party; if the agent acts within authority, the contract is between principal and third party. Actual authority arises from a consensual agreement between principal and agent: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Partners have implied authority to do the usual things needed to carry on the firm's business, but this may be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions: Construction v Hexyl (1985); implied authority is not always found: Peterson v Moloney (1951). Under s 8 PA, acts "for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm" bind the firm. The firm is bound if: (1) the business was of the kind carried on by the firm: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) it was done in the usual way: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person was a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of authority: Crouch v IPG (2013). The representation must come from the principals: Essington v Regency (2004).

Application: Express. There is no evidence Callum or Tessa expressly authorised the purchase; they were "outraged", and Robert admitted he still needed to "convince Callum and Tessa". Implied. StageCorp will argue buying event equipment is usual for an events business. However, CT Events deliberately hired rather than bought equipment and decided matters by majority vote; as in Construction v Hexyl, that practice overrides any implied authority, and as in Peterson v Moloney, none is likely to be found. Kind of business. Applying Mercantile v Garrod, CT Events' business is small functions under 100 guests; a custom stage for "large-scale concerts and festivals" is a different kind of business. Usual way. The usual way was to hire, and a $180,000 purchase by one partner is out of proportion; as in Goldberg v Jenkins, StageCorp should have suspected a lack of authority. Believed a partner. Satisfied: Robert signed "on behalf of CT Events", paid from the joint account, and Callum and Tessa held him out as part of the team — unlike Essington v Regency, the holding out came from the principals. No notice. Robert said the purchase was his "long-term goal", "provided he could convince Callum and Tessa", so StageCorp was on notice and did not rely in good faith without notice (Crouch v IPG).

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Robert had no actual or apparent authority, so CT Events, Callum and Tessa are not bound.

Issue 3: Robert's personal liability

Issue: Can StageCorp hold Robert personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

Law: An agent lacking authority is personally liable if: (1) the agent claimed to contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent had no authority; and (3) the third party relied on that representation and would not otherwise have contracted: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020].

Application: First, Robert signed "on behalf of CT Events" and paid from the joint account. Second, he had no authority. Third, StageCorp would not have built a $180,000 custom stage for Robert personally. Robert may argue StageCorp did not rely on any claim of authority because he disclosed he needed his partners' support; however, by signing a binding contract in the firm's name and paying a deposit from its account, he represented that the firm was committed.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Robert is personally liable to StageCorp for breach of warranty of authority.

Remedies

StageCorp's best course is to claim damages from Robert personally, up to the $162,000 balance less what it recovers by taking back and reselling the stage. In the alternative, StageCorp may argue Robert had apparent authority under s 8 PA; if accepted, Callum, Tessa and Robert would be jointly and personally liable for $162,000 (Kendall v Hamilton), as a partnership is not a separate legal entity — but given Robert's statement, this is weaker. Separately, Callum and Tessa may pursue Robert for breach of his duty to disclose matters affecting the firm (s 31 PA) to recover the $18,000 deposit.

What this topic is, in one breath: a contract (or gift/transfer) is only binding if the person agreed freely and genuinely. If their consent was spoiled — by a lie, a fundamental mistake, a threat, a dominating relationship, or someone exploiting their weakness — the law lets the innocent party set it aside (rescission). The exam question almost always gives you a vulnerable person (old, sick, grieving, poor English, dependent) and a stronger party who gets them to sign. Your job: run one ILAC per vitiating factor, strongest first, then Remedies.

① Learn it in 20 minutes

0. The big picture — genuine consent, void vs voidable

Contract law balances two policies: caveat emptor ("let the buyer beware") but freedom of contract — free and voluntary consent. A contract is only enforceable against a party if that party entered it willingly. Factors that affect genuine consent are called vitiating factors — they "vitiate" (spoil) the contract and allow the innocent party to set it aside. There are five: misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence, unconscionability.

  • Void = of no contractual effect at all (never a contract). Typical for: fundamental mistake.
  • Voidable = valid and binding until the innocent party chooses to set it aside (or confirm it). Typical for: misrepresentation, duress, undue influence.

Slides 3–6. Note: the slides say a contract "will be void" for unconscionable conduct — strictly it is set aside at the weaker party's election. Safe exam wording: "the contract is liable to be set aside".

1. Misrepresentation

Definition: a false statement of fact made by one party to another. If the other party was induced to enter the contract by it, the contract is voidable.

Elements (slide 7)

  1. A false statement/representation of fact made by the representor to the representee;
  2. About an existing fact or past event — not a statement of future intention;
  3. Made before or at the time the contract is concluded;
  4. Intended to induce, and in fact does induce, the other party to enter the contract.

NOT a misrepresentation (slide 8)

  • Honest statements of opinion; puffs/exaggerated statements; promises; statements of future intention.
  • Mere silence — UNLESS: a previously true statement becomes untrue; the representor doesn't correct a statement after discovering it is untrue; non-disclosure turns a statement into a half-truth; or there is a legal duty of full disclosure.

Three types

NegligentHonest but careless(1) relationship/special position → duty of care; (2) serious or business subject matter; (3) adviser realises recipient will act on it; (4) reliance was reasonable; (5) damage (usually money)Rescind + damages

Cases:

  • Derry v Peek (1889) — company directors' prospectus said the company had the right to run steam trams; in fact it needed Board of Trade consent, which was later refused. The directors honestly believed the statement, so it was not fraud. Sets the test: fraud = false statement made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly (careless whether true or false).
  • Oscar Chess Ltd v Williams [1957] — Williams traded in a car to a dealer saying it was a 1948 model, relying on its (falsified) log book; it was really a 1939 model. He honestly believed it → an innocent misrepresentation, not a contractual term, so no damages.
ACL link (slide 14): if a party was tricked or deceived, they can usually sue under the ACL (misleading or deceptive conduct s 18, false representations s 29) — the remedies are far greater, so parties usually prefer the ACL. But if the question says "Contract Law 3 only", stay in common law/equity and at most add one line noting the ACL. See T6 ACL.

2. Mistake

General rule: a mistake by one party does not entitle them to escape the contract (caveat emptor). Only in exceptional circumstances is a contract void for mistake. A mistake is where one or more parties misunderstand a fact — arguably no "meeting of the minds".

Always check: (1) the mistake existed prior to or at the time of contracting; (2) it is fundamental (not minor); (3) it is a mistake, not a misrepresentation (if the other side caused it by a false statement, run misrep instead).

MutualBoth think they agreed, but each was thinking of a different thingAgreement conditional on a belief held by each party, and the beliefs are differentRaffles v Wichelhaus (1864)

  • Leaf v International Galleries (1950) — buyer and seller both believed a painting was by Constable; it wasn't. The mistake went to the quality of the thing, and the buyer waited about five years before trying to rescind — he failed (lapse of time). Use it to show the "both share the same false belief" scenario and that delay kills rescission.
  • Raffles v Wichelhaus (1864) — contract for cotton "ex Peerless" from Bombay; there were two ships called Peerless, sailing months apart, and each party meant a different one. No meeting of minds → no binding contract.
  • Taylor v Johnson (1983) — Mrs Johnson agreed to sell land for $15,000 in total, believing it was $15,000 per acre. Taylor knew (or suspected) she was mistaken and deliberately kept her from finding out. High Court set the contract aside.

Non est factum ("it is not my deed") — slide 21

A form of unilateral mistake where one party is encouraged to sign a written contract without reading/understanding it (limited). The court may find it void if the person who made the mistake: (1) had a good reason for not reading it (e.g. blind, illiterate, cannot read English); and (2) the mistake is about the fundamental nature of the document they were signing (e.g. thought it was a quote, not a contract). No case named on the slide — just cite the principle.

3. Duress

Pressure exerted by one party to coerce or compel another to contract on particular terms. Contract is voidable, not void.

Elements: (1) one party expressly or impliedly threatens the other with harm; (2) the threat contributed to the other's decision to enter the contract.

TypeCaseFacts → held
Duress to the person (physical, mental, psychological — to them or loved ones)Barton v Armstrong (1973)Armstrong threatened to have Barton killed unless he signed a deal to buy Armstrong's shares. The deal was set aside — the threat only had to be a reason for signing, not the only reason.
Duress to goods/propertyHawker Pacific v Helicopter (1991)Hawker Pacific refused to release a helicopter it had worked on unless the owner signed an agreement to pay more money. The owner needed it urgently and signed → duress to goods; the agreement was voidable.
Economic duressNorth Ocean Shipping v Hyundai (1979)Shipbuilder demanded 10% more than the agreed price, threatening not to deliver a tanker. Economic duress — but only where the pressure is in some way unlawful. The buyer then waited months without protesting, so it was treated as having affirmed the contract and could not rescind.
"Sign now, it's a limited-time offer!" is sales pressure, not duress. There is no threat of harm. Mention duress in one line and dismiss it, or skip it.

4. Undue influence

Arises where a party has a degree of controlling influence and power and dominates the will of another — "every case where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". Unlikely in ordinary commercial deals between equals with no history.

Voidable at the election of the weaker party when (slide 26):

  1. The parties are in a pre-existing relationship;
  2. One party has a controlling influence over the other;
  3. The stronger party takes advantage of that influence to act for their own benefit;
  4. The weaker party is not exercising independent judgement → no genuine free and voluntary consent.

Two routes

Route A — Presumed (recognised relationships)
Automatic presumption of undue influence:
  • Parent and child
  • Guardian and ward
  • Trustee and beneficiary
  • Doctor and patient
  • Lawyer and client
  • Religious adviser and disciple
Allcard v Skinner (1887)
Route B — Other relationships (must prove dominance)
Party alleging must show the other exercised a dominance/controlling influence so they did not freely and voluntarily contract. Courts have found this can arise between:
  • Spouse and spouse
  • Principal and agent
  • Bank and client
  • Accountant and client
  • Employer and employee
  • Carer and patient ← exam favourite
Johnson v Buttress (1936)

Onus shift (slides 27, 29): once the presumption arises (route A) or dominance is proven (route B), the stronger party must prove the transaction was the "pure voluntary and well-understood act" of the weaker party and that they did not exert their influence.

  • Allcard v Skinner (1887) — Miss Allcard joined a religious sisterhood and gave away her property to it under its rules (religious adviser/disciple → presumed undue influence). But she waited years after leaving before suing, so her claim was barred by delay. Use: recognised relationship + delay as a bar.
  • Johnson v Buttress (1936) — Mr Buttress, an elderly, illiterate widower of limited intelligence, became dependent on Mrs Johnson after his wife's death and transferred his only real asset — his home — to her, without proper independent advice. High Court: a relationship of influence and dependence existed; Mrs Johnson couldn't show it was his free and well-understood act → transfer set aside. The model for every "elderly person gives house to carer" exam question.

5. Unconscionable conduct (equity)

Conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. At common law, a clause just being harsh isn't enough to set aside a contract. Equity stepped in (from the 1970s) based on inequality of bargaining power — "there runs a single thread: the inequality of bargaining power" (Lord Denning).

The victim must be in such a condition that there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger).

Kitto J, Blomley v Ryan (1956): "Whenever one party to a transaction is at a special disadvantage in dealing with the other party because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands."

Elements — Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio

  1. The weaker party was under a special disability/weakness/disadvantage vis-à-vis the stronger party, so there was no real equality between them;
  2. The stronger party knew or should have known about that special disadvantage;
  3. The stronger party took unfair advantage of it — it was "unconscientious" to procure the agreement in the circumstances in which it was procured.

Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio — Mr and Mrs Amadio, elderly Italian migrants with limited written English, signed a guarantee and mortgage over their property for their son's company's debts to the bank. Their son told them it was limited ($50,000, six months); it was actually unlimited. The bank manager brought the document to their home, knew of their poor English and that the company was in serious financial trouble, but did not explain it or suggest advice. High Court: special disadvantage + bank knew + unconscientious → guarantee set aside.

Undue influence vs unconscionable (they often overlap — run both): undue influence looks at the quality of the weaker party's consent (was their will dominated by a relationship?). Unconscionable conduct looks at the stronger party's conduct (did they exploit a disadvantage they knew about?). Unconscionable conduct does not need a pre-existing relationship — perfect for strangers like door-to-door salespeople.
ACL link (slide 34): statute has adopted these principles — ACL ss 20–22. s 20 = unconscionable "within the meaning of the unwritten law" (i.e. Amadio); s 21 = in connection with goods/services; s 22 = factors. See T6 ACL. Only use if the question allows the ACL.

6. Remedies

Possible remedies: rescission; damages; equitable remedies; statutory remedies.

Rescission (the main remedy for vitiating factors)

An order of the court declaring the contract terminated/unenforceable in the event of complete non-performance, breach of a condition, misrepresentation or mistake, duress, undue influence or unconscionability. The court will, as far as possible, restore the parties to their original positions (e.g. deposit refunded, property transferred back). Rescission is an equitable remedy.

Bars to rescission — the right is lost if:

  1. substantial restitution is impossible (can't put things back);
  2. the contract is affirmed (North Ocean Shipping v Hyundai);
  3. there is delay / lapse of time (Leaf v International Galleries, Allcard v Skinner);
  4. third party rights have intervened (e.g. property sold to an innocent buyer);
  5. the contract is executed/completed;
  6. the party seeking relief has acted unconscientiously ("clean hands").

Damages

Aim: put the innocent party in the position they would have been in had the breach/misrepresentation not occurred. Available for breach of contract, breach of a collateral contract, and fraudulent or negligent misrepresentation — not innocent misrepresentation. Types: nominal, exemplary, liquidated, unliquidated. Plaintiff must mitigate their loss: Payzu Ltd v Saunders [1919].

Equitable & statutory

If damages are inadequate: specific performance (order to perform) or injunction (order forbidding conduct — more likely). Statutory remedies where ACL/Sale of Goods implied terms are breached.

7. Discharge & frustration (also in the Contract Law 3 lecture)

Discharge = a valid contract comes to an end, releasing the parties from further obligations — by consent, operation of law (e.g. frustration), breach/repudiation, performance, or lapse of time. Breach of a condition → may terminate (repudiation); breach of a warranty → damages only.

Frustration: something happens, not the fault of either party, that makes performance impossible or only possible in a way neither anticipated. Must be substantially different, not merely more difficult or expensive. Terminated by frustration if:

  1. a supervening event has made performance completely impossible, or impossible in the original way envisaged;
  2. neither party caused it (beyond their control);
  3. the contract did not provide for the event, expressly or impliedly; and
  4. it would be unjust to compel either party to proceed.

Examples: natural disasters (flood, fire); subject matter destroyed — Taylor v Caldwell (1863) (music hall hired for concerts burnt down → frustrated); death/incapacity in a personal-services contract; change in law makes it illegal — Esposito v Bowden (1857); the anticipated event doesn't happen — Krell v Henry (1903) (room hired to watch the coronation procession, which was cancelled); conditions of performance change dramatically — Codelfa v State Rail (1982).

Frustration is not a vitiating factor (consent was fine at the start) — but it IS Contract Law 3, and the 2025 Juan question plants a bushfire. If a disaster happens after signing, add a separate Frustration ILAC.

④ How to structure a multi-factor answer

  1. Plan (in the 10 min): underline every vulnerability fact and every "pressure/advantage" fact. Run the decision table. Rank the factors by strength.
  2. Opener (1 line): "Whether [X] can have the [contract/transfer] set aside depends on whether any vitiating factors affected [X]'s genuine consent, namely [A], [B] and [C]."
  3. Short general Law intro (2–3 lines, once only): vitiating factors, voidable/void.
  4. Issue 1 = strongest factor — full I-L-A-C with sub-headings for each element and proper case analysis (facts of the case + compare).
  5. Issue 2, 3… = next strongest — full ILAC each, but you can refer back ("As discussed in Issue 1, Juan was at a special disadvantage…") instead of repeating facts.
  6. Weak factors — one or two sentences inside the relevant issue or after the strong ones: "Duress is unlikely to apply as Jake made no threat of harm…". Don't write a full ILAC on something that obviously fails (feedback penalises irrelevant areas).
  7. Remedies (always last) — rescission, restoration of positions, check EACH bar against the facts, damages only if fraud/negligent misrep.
  8. Overall conclusion (1–2 lines) answering the question asked ("Advise Juan…").
Time budget (25 marks ≈ 55 min): Plan 5 · Issue 1 15 · Issue 2 12 · Issue 3 8 · Issue 4 6 · Remedies 6 · Conclusion 1.
Why strongest first: if you run out of time, the marks are already banked where the marker expects the most. It also lets later issues say "for the same reasons as Issue 1…".
Don't write "Did Melania exert undue influence?" as your Issue. Name the client and the outcome they want: "Can Christine have Harris' will set aside on the basis of undue influence?"

⑤ ILAC SCRIPT — the exact sentences to write

Write in this order. Copy the Law paragraphs verbatim; fill the [blanks] in Application.

0. Opener + general law (write once, at the top)

Whether [Client] can have the [contract with X / transfer of the property / will] set aside depends on whether [his/her] consent was vitiated by [unconscionable conduct, undue influence, misrepresentation and/or mistake]. Each is considered in turn, followed by remedies.
A contract will only be enforceable against a party if that party entered into it willingly, with free and voluntary consent. Factors that affect genuine consent are called vitiating factors, and include misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence and unconscionable conduct. Where a vitiating factor is established, the contract may be void (of no contractual effect) or voidable (valid until set aside at the option of the innocent party).

A. Unconscionable conduct

ISSUE
Can [Client] have the [contract/transfer] with [Stronger party] set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?
LAW
Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. While mere harshness of a term is not enough at common law, equity will intervene where there is an inequality of bargaining power such that there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). As Kitto J stated in Blomley v Ryan, equity intervenes "whenever one party to a transaction is at a special disadvantage in dealing with the other party because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands."
Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, a contract will be set aside for unconscionable conduct where: (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage in relation to the stronger party, so that there was no real equality between them; (2) the stronger party knew or ought to have known of that special disadvantage; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it, so that it was unconscientious to procure the agreement in the circumstances in which it was procured.
In Amadio, Mr and Mrs Amadio were elderly Italian migrants with a limited understanding of written English who signed a guarantee and mortgage over their property securing their son's company's debts. Their son had told them the guarantee was limited to $50,000 and six months, when in fact it was unlimited. The bank manager, who brought the document to their home for signing, knew of their limited English and of the company's financial difficulties, but did not explain the document. The High Court held that the Amadios were under a special disadvantage, the bank knew of it, and it was unconscionable for the bank to accept the guarantee, which was set aside.
APPLICATION

(1) Special disadvantage

[Client] was under a special disadvantage because [he/she] was [age], [had limited English / poor eyesight / suffered from depression / ADD / was grieving the recent death of his wife / was a disability pensioner / was dependent on X]. These are the kinds of "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties" referred to in Blomley v Ryan, which affected [his] ability to conserve [his] own interests.
Similar to the Amadios, who were elderly migrants with limited English, [Client] is [an 85-year-old immigrant with difficulty understanding English]. Further, unlike the Amadios, [Client] also [had poor eyesight / was suffering depression], which makes [his] disadvantage even greater.
Against: [Stronger party] may argue that [Client] was an adult capable of refusing to sign and [had a solicitor present / had family support]. However, [the solicitor was chosen by X and not independent / the stepson was away], so this carries little weight.

(2) Knowledge

[Stronger party] knew, or at least ought to have known, of the disadvantage because [Client told him directly that… / she lived with him and managed his personal and financial affairs]. Like the bank manager in Amadio, who knew of the Amadios' limited English, [X] was fully aware of [Client]'s [weakness].

(3) Unfair advantage / unconscientious

It was unconscientious for [X] to procure the agreement in these circumstances because [he pressured Client to sign immediately / did not explain the document / did not suggest independent advice / chose the solicitor / the transaction was entirely for X's benefit / the terms were one-sided]. As in Amadio, where the manager did not explain the document or suggest advice, [X] […].
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [Client] can have the [contract/transfer] set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct, as all three elements in Amadio are satisfied.

B. Undue influence

ISSUE
Can [Client] have the [transfer/will/contract] in favour of [X] set aside on the basis of undue influence?
LAW
Undue influence occurs where a party to a transaction has a degree of controlling influence and power over another person and dominates that person's will — "every case where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". A transaction is voidable at the election of the weaker party where: (1) the parties are in a pre-existing relationship; (2) one party has a controlling influence over the other; (3) the stronger party takes advantage of that influence to act for their own benefit; and (4) the weaker party is not exercising independent judgement, so there is no genuine free and voluntary consent.
Certain recognised relationships automatically raise a presumption of undue influence, including parent and child, guardian and ward, trustee and beneficiary, doctor and patient, lawyer and client, and religious adviser and disciple (Allcard v Skinner). In other relationships, the party alleging undue influence must demonstrate that the other party exercised a dominance or controlling influence over them such that they did not freely and voluntarily enter the transaction. The courts have recognised that such influence may arise between spouses, principal and agent, bank and client, accountant and client, employer and employee, and carer and patient (Johnson v Buttress). Once this is shown, the onus shifts to the stronger party to prove that the transaction was the "pure voluntary and well-understood act" of the weaker party and that they did not exert their influence.
In Johnson v Buttress, Mr Buttress, an elderly widower who was illiterate and of limited intelligence, became dependent on Mrs Johnson after the death of his wife and transferred his only significant asset, his home, to her without proper independent advice. The High Court held that there was a relationship of influence and dependence, that Mrs Johnson could not show the transfer was Mr Buttress' free and well-understood act, and the transfer was set aside.
APPLICATION

(1) Pre-existing relationship

[X] and [Client] were in a pre-existing relationship of [carer and patient], as [X] [had lived with and cared for him for over a year]. This is not one of the automatically recognised relationships in Allcard v Skinner, but carer and patient is a relationship in which the courts have found controlling influence may arise (Johnson v Buttress). [Client's family] must therefore show dominance.

(2) Controlling influence / dominance

[X] exercised a controlling influence because [she managed his personal and financial affairs / he was emotionally dependent on her / he relied on her for daily care / she "convinced" him / he wanted to "appease" her]. Issue 1: Unconscionable conduct

Issue

Can Juan have the contract with ES set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Although mere harshness of a term is not enough at common law, equity will intervene where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). As Kitto J stated in Blomley v Ryan, this arises whenever one party "is at a special disadvantage in dealing with the other party because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands."

Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, a contract will be set aside where: (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage in relation to the stronger party; (2) the stronger party knew or ought to have known of that disadvantage; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it, so that it was unconscientious to procure the agreement in the circumstances. In Amadio, an elderly Italian migrant couple with limited written English signed a guarantee and mortgage over their property for their son's company's debts, believing it was limited to $50,000 and six months when it was unlimited. The bank manager brought the document to their home, knew of their limited English and the company's financial difficulties, and did not explain the document. The High Court held the bank had acted unconscionably and set the guarantee aside.

He lives on a remote property and relies on his stepson, who was away, for all major financial decisions. These are exactly the "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties" described in Blomley v Ryan. On the balance of probabilities, Juan can have the contract set aside for unconscionable conduct, as all three elements in Amadio are satisfied.

A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact made by one party to another which induces that party to enter the contract, making the contract voidable. Issue 1: Undue influence

Issue

Can Jeffrey and Max have David's transfer of the apartment to Eveline set aside on the basis of undue influence?

Law

Undue influence occurs where a party has a degree of controlling influence and power over another person and dominates that person's will — "every case where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". A transaction is voidable at the election of the weaker party where: (1) the parties are in a pre-existing relationship; (2) one party has a controlling influence over the other; (3) the stronger party takes advantage of that influence for their own benefit; and (4) the weaker party is not exercising independent judgement, so there is no genuine free and voluntary consent.

Certain recognised relationships automatically raise a presumption of undue influence, such as parent and child, doctor and patient, lawyer and client, and religious adviser and disciple (Allcard v Skinner). In other relationships, the party alleging undue influence must show that the other party exercised a dominance or controlling influence over them so that they did not act freely and voluntarily. Courts have recognised that such influence may arise in relationships including spouses, employer and employee, and carer and patient (Johnson v Buttress). Once this is shown, the onus shifts to the stronger party to prove that the transaction was the "pure voluntary and well-understood act" of the weaker party.

In Johnson v Buttress, Mr Buttress, an elderly widower who was illiterate and of limited intelligence, became dependent on Mrs Johnson after his wife's death and transferred his only significant asset, his home, to her without proper independent advice. The High Court found a relationship of influence and dependence, held that Mrs Johnson had not shown the transfer was his free and well-understood act, and set the transfer aside.

Application

Pre-existing relationship. David and Eveline were in a pre-existing relationship of carer and patient: David lives with Eveline, who is his carer, and she was also a friend of his deceased wife. This is not one of the automatically presumed relationships in Allcard v Skinner, so Jeffrey and Max must show dominance. However, carer and patient is a relationship in which the courts have recognised controlling influence may arise, as in Johnson v Buttress.

Controlling influence. Eveline had a controlling influence over David. David is 89, a widowed disability pensioner with a limited ability to read, write and speak English, who suffers from depression and is "dependent upon Eveline to look after him". Eveline manages some of his personal and financial affairs. Very similar to Mr Buttress, who became dependent on Mrs Johnson after his wife died, David became reliant on Eveline, a friend of his late wife, after he was widowed. Eveline also "convinces" David to revoke his will and transfer the apartment, showing her influence over his decisions.

Advantage for Eveline's benefit. Eveline used her influence for her own benefit. It was Eveline who asked for the apartment, and she persuaded David not only to leave it to her but to transfer it to her "immediately". The apartment was a significant, income-producing asset (it is rented out) that David intended his two sons to inherit. As in Johnson v Buttress, where the elderly man gave away his home, a gift of this size to a carer, at the expense of David's own children, is hard to explain by ordinary motives.

No independent judgement. David did not exercise independent judgement: we are told he "does not fully understand the effect of his decision". The solicitor was "of [Eveline's] choice" and met with Eveline and David together, merely advising them of the paperwork. David therefore received no independent advice about the effect of giving away his apartment, just as Mr Buttress lacked proper independent advice. Eveline may argue that David had developed a genuine friendship with her, that he was legally advised, and that he had a month to reconsider before the transfer. However, a solicitor chosen by Eveline who only explained paperwork in her presence is not independent advice, and given David's limited English and lack of understanding, Eveline is unlikely to prove that the transfer was his "pure voluntary and well-understood act".

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the transfer is voidable for undue influence, as Eveline is unlikely to rebut the inference that she dominated David's will.

Issue 2: Unconscionable conduct

Issue

Can Jeffrey and Max have the transfer of the apartment to Eveline set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Equity will intervene where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger), that is, where one party is at a special disadvantage "because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands" (Kitto J, Blomley v Ryan). Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, the transaction will be set aside where: (1) the weaker party was under a special disadvantage in relation to the stronger party; (2) the stronger party knew or ought to have known of it; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it. In Amadio, elderly Italian migrants with limited written English signed an unlimited guarantee and mortgage for their son's company debts, believing it was limited; the bank manager knew of their poor English and the company's difficulties but did not explain the document, and the High Court set the guarantee aside.

Application

Special disadvantage. David was at a special disadvantage: he is 89, a disability pensioner, suffers from depression, has limited ability to read, write and speak English, and depends on Eveline for his care. Similar to the Amadios, who were elderly migrants with limited English, David could not properly understand the documents he signed, and his depression is the kind of "illness… impaired faculties" referred to in Blomley v Ryan.

Knowledge. Eveline knew of David's disadvantage. She lives with him, is his carer, and manages his personal and financial affairs. She is in a far better position to know of his weakness than the bank manager in Amadio, who was still held to know of the Amadios' limited English.

Unfair advantage. Eveline took unfair advantage of David's position. She asked for the apartment herself, convinced him to revoke his will and transfer it immediately, and arranged a solicitor of her own choice. As in Amadio, where the bank did not ensure the Amadios understood the transaction, Eveline did nothing to ensure David understood its effect or obtained independent advice, and the transaction was entirely for her benefit. Eveline may argue that the transfer was a genuine gift arising out of their friendship, but given David did not fully understand his decision, it was unconscientious for Eveline to procure it.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the transfer can also be set aside for unconscionable conduct, as each element in Amadio is satisfied.

Remedies

The primary remedy is rescission, an equitable remedy by which the court sets the transaction aside and, as far as possible, restores the parties to their original positions. The right to rescind is lost if restitution is impossible, the transaction has been affirmed, there has been delay, third party rights have intervened, or the party seeking relief has acted unconscientiously (Allcard v Skinner, where the claim failed due to delay).

None of these bars appear to apply. Jeffrey and Max acted as soon as the will was read, so unlike in Allcard v Skinner there is no delay. David did not affirm the transfer, as he died soon after. Restitution is possible because Eveline still holds the apartment, and it is assumed she has not sold it to a third party; if she has, rescission may be barred by third party rights. Jeffrey and Max have not acted unconscientiously. The court would therefore order that the apartment be transferred back to David's estate, to be dealt with according to law (the effect of the revocation of the will on whether Jeffrey and Max inherit is a matter of succession law outside this question). Damages are not relevant, as there is no misrepresentation.

The note about the revoked will shows you noticed a real complication without wandering into an area outside the course.

Overall conclusion

Jeffrey and Max should be advised that the transfer of the apartment to Eveline is likely to be set aside on the basis of both undue influence and unconscionable conduct, and the apartment returned to David's estate.

A transaction is only binding where a party enters into it willingly, with free and voluntary consent. Vitiating factors, which affect that genuine consent, allow the transaction to be set aside.

Issue 1: Undue influence

Issue

Can Christine have Harris' will in favour of Melania set aside on the basis of undue influence?

Law

Undue influence occurs where one party has a controlling influence and power over another and dominates that person's will — "every case where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". A transaction is voidable where: (1) the parties are in a pre-existing relationship; (2) one party has a controlling influence over the other; (3) the stronger party takes advantage of that influence for their own benefit; and (4) the weaker party is not exercising independent judgement.

Recognised relationships such as parent and child, doctor and patient, and religious adviser and disciple raise an automatic presumption of undue influence (Allcard v Skinner). In other relationships, including carer and patient (Johnson v Buttress), the party alleging undue influence must show the other exercised a dominance or controlling influence, after which the onus shifts to the stronger party to prove the transaction was the "pure voluntary and well-understood act" of the weaker party. In Johnson v Buttress, an elderly, illiterate widower who had become dependent on Mrs Johnson after his wife's death transferred his home to her without proper independent advice; the High Court set the transfer aside for undue influence.

Application

Pre-existing relationship. Melania is Harris' carer and lives with him, and was a friend of his deceased wife. Carer and patient is not an automatically presumed relationship under Allcard v Skinner, but it is one in which courts have recognised a controlling influence may arise (Johnson v Buttress), so Christine must prove dominance.

Controlling influence. Melania had managed Harris' personal and financial affairs for over a year, and over the past six months Harris developed an "emotional dependency" on her following the loss of his wife. This closely mirrors Johnson v Buttress, where Mr Buttress became dependent on Mrs Johnson after his wife's death. Harris' desire to "appease Melania" when signing shows her influence was operating on his decision.

Advantage for Melania's benefit. Melania took advantage of that influence for herself: it was she who asked to receive the $750,000 house in place of Harris' own daughter. Like the transfer of Mr Buttress' home, this was Harris' most significant asset, given to his carer at the expense of his family.

No independent judgement. Harris did not exercise independent judgement. He "immediately" agreed, which is consistent with his ADD and tendency to rash, impulsive decisions rather than considered thought. Melania took him to her own friend, Justine, who helped "Harris and Melania" finalise the paperwork together, so Harris had no independent advice. Overwhelmed by the documents, he "agree[d] to sign everything that is presented to him". Melania might argue that a solicitor was involved and Harris agreed willingly. However, a solicitor who is Melania's friend and acted for both of them is not independent, just as Mr Buttress lacked independent advice, and Melania is unlikely to show the amendment was Harris' "pure voluntary and well-understood act".

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the amendment to Harris' will is liable to be set aside for undue influence.

Issue 2: Unconscionable conduct

Issue

Can Christine have Harris' will set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct by Melania?

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience, arising where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). Kitto J in Blomley v Ryan described it as where one party is at a special disadvantage "because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage". Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio: (1) the weaker party must be under a special disadvantage; (2) the stronger party must know or ought to know of it; and (3) the stronger party must take unfair advantage of it. In Amadio, a bank manager who knew an elderly migrant couple had limited English and did not explain an unlimited guarantee they signed for their son's company acted unconscionably, and the guarantee was set aside.

Application

Special disadvantage. Harris is 93, a disability pensioner, recently bereaved, emotionally dependent on Melania, and suffers from attention-deficit disorder that makes him impulsive and prone to rash decisions. Unlike the Amadios, his disadvantage is not one of language but of "impaired faculties" and emotional vulnerability, which Blomley v Ryan expressly recognises. His disadvantage is arguably greater than in Amadio, given his age and condition.

Knowledge. Melania knew of Harris' disadvantage: she manages his affairs precisely "for these reasons" — his temperament, rash decision-making and ADD. Her knowledge is more direct than that of the bank manager in Amadio.

Unfair advantage. Melania took unfair advantage by asking Harris for the house when she knew he made impulsive decisions, then taking him straight to her own friend to finalise the amendment while he was overwhelmed. As in Amadio, she did nothing to ensure Harris understood the effect or obtained independent advice, and the benefit was entirely hers. It was unconscientious for her to procure the amendment in these circumstances.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the amendment can also be set aside for unconscionable conduct.

Opener + general law

Whether [X] can have the [contract/transfer/will] set aside depends on whether [his] consent was vitiated by [A], [B] and [C]. A contract is only enforceable against a party who entered it willingly, with free and voluntary consent. Vitiating factors (misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence, unconscionable conduct) affect genuine consent; the contract may be void (no effect) or voidable (valid until set aside at the innocent party's option).
UNCONSCIONABLE CONDUCT — Law
Unfair, unjust, against good conscience. Harshness alone not enough; equity intervenes where no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). Kitto J, Blomley v Ryan: "special disadvantage… because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage". CBA v Amadio: (1) special disability/disadvantage, no real equality; (2) stronger party knew/ought to know; (3) took unfair advantage — unconscientious to procure in the circumstances.
Amadio: elderly Italian migrants, limited written English, signed unlimited guarantee/mortgage for son's company (son said $50k/6 months); manager brought it to their home, knew of English & company's troubles, didn't explain → set aside.
ACL ss 20–22 adopt this (s 20 = "unwritten law") — only if ACL allowed.
UNDUE INFLUENCE — Law
Controlling influence & power dominating another's will — "where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". Voidable where: (1) pre-existing relationship; (2) controlling influence; (3) stronger takes advantage for own benefit; (4) weaker not exercising independent judgement.
Presumed: parent/child, guardian/ward, trustee/beneficiary, doctor/patient, lawyer/client, religious adviser/disciple (Allcard v Skinner).
Other — prove dominance: spouses, principal/agent, bank/client, accountant/client, employer/employee, carer/patient (Johnson v Buttress). Then onus on stronger party: "pure voluntary and well-understood act".
Johnson v Buttress: elderly illiterate widower, limited intelligence, dependent on Mrs Johnson after wife died, transferred his home to her, no proper independent advice → set aside.
MISREPRESENTATION — Law
False statement of fact inducing contract → voidable. (1) false statement of fact by representor to representee; (2) existing fact/past event, not future intention; (3) before/at formation; (4) intended to & did induce. NOT: opinion, puff, promise, future intention, mere silence (unless half-truth, previously true now false, not corrected, duty to disclose).
Fraudulent — knowingly, without belief, recklessly; induces; damage (Derry v Peek: directors honestly believed tram-rights statement → not fraud). Rescind + damages.
Negligent — special relationship/duty; serious/business matter; knows recipient will act; reasonable reliance; damage. Rescind + damages.
Innocent — no intent to deceive (Oscar Chess v Williams: car honestly said 1948, really 1939). Rescind only, NO damages.
MISTAKE — Law
General rule: no escape (caveat emptor); void only exceptionally. Must be before/at formation, fundamental, a mistake not a misrep.
Common — same mistake by both; conditional on shared belief, belief wrong (Leaf v Int'l Galleries: both thought painting a Constable; 5-yr delay → no rescission).
Mutual — different beliefs (Raffles v Wichelhaus: two ships "Peerless").
Unilateral — serious, fundamental, other knew/ought to know, sought to take advantage (Taylor v Johnson: $15k total vs per acre; buyer exploited → set aside).
Non est factum — encouraged to sign unread; good reason (blind, illiterate, no English) + mistake as to fundamental nature of document → void.
DURESS — Law
Pressure to coerce contract → voidable. (1) express/implied threat of harm; (2) contributed to decision.
Person: Barton v Armstrong (death threats; need only be a reason). Goods: Hawker Pacific v Helicopter (helicopter withheld until paid more). Economic — must be unlawful: North Ocean Shipping v Hyundai (+10% or no tanker; affirmed by delay).
FRUSTRATION — Law
Event not either's fault makes performance impossible/radically different (not just harder/dearer). (1) supervening event; (2) neither caused; (3) contract didn't provide for it; (4) unjust to compel. Taylor v Caldwell (music hall burnt), Krell v Henry (coronation cancelled), Esposito v Bowden (law change), Codelfa v State Rail (conditions change).
REMEDIES — Law
Rescission (equitable): court terminates contract, restores parties to original positions — for misrep, mistake, duress, UI, UC. Bars: (1) restitution impossible; (2) affirmed; (3) delay; (4) third party rights; (5) executed; (6) unconscientious conduct by claimant.
Damages: position as if no breach/misrep; fraud & negligent misrep only — not innocent. Mitigate: Payzu v Saunders.
Equitable: specific performance, injunction.

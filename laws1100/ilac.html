The 30-second version

  1. Read the LAST sentence of the question first. It names the area ("under Tort Law", "under the ACL", "Contract Law 3 only", "law of agency"). That tells you which topic page to open (see ②).
  2. For each issue write four headings: Issue (one short question ending "?"), Law (long: define the area, then each element with its case or section, plus a short case analysis), Application (long: go through the same elements in the same order, using the parties' names and facts, compare with the cases, argue both sides), Conclusion (one sentence: "On the balance of probabilities…").
  3. Finish with Remedies every time, and Defences only if the facts raise one.
  4. Only cite cases and sections from the course. Use headings. Never write one big block of text.

How to lay out the 6-page booklet

  1. Write "Question 1" (or 2) clearly on the cover and at the top of page 1.
  2. Optional 1–2 line opener framing the issues (template in ④).
  3. ISSUE 1 – [short name] as an underlined heading, then the sub-headings Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion. Inside Law and Application, add sub-sub-headings for each element (e.g. Duty of care / Breach / Causation, or s 18 / s 29 / s 54).
  4. Leave a blank line between paragraphs, with one element or one case per paragraph. Feedback 5 penalised "large sections of bulky, uninterrupted text".
  5. ISSUE 2 … repeats the same pattern.
  6. Finish with REMEDIES (always) and DEFENCES (only if relevant) as headings. Many students put Defences before Remedies in negligence, which is fine because defences reduce the damages.
  7. Roughly 1,200–1,600 handwritten words fits in 6 pages. If you run out of space, ask for another booklet and label it with the question number.

What to do when a fact is missing

There is insufficient information as to [whether / how / when … e.g. whether the sign was visible before Paula paid]. Assuming that [assumption most consistent with the facts], [result]. However, if [the opposite], then [the opposite result].

This is exactly what the exam instruction asks for. It also earns marks because it shows you know why the fact matters. Use it for things like a person's age or knowledge, whether a warning was seen, whether a contract was signed, whether the buyer is a "consumer", or the exact amounts.

③ ILAC explained, letter by letter

From the W3 slide: Issue = the main legal problem between the parties. Law = the relevant law (case law and legislation) that applies to the facts. Application = applying the law in your Law section to the facts. Conclusion = the answer to your Issue. The W7 workshop's rule of thumb: short Issue and Conclusion, long Law and Application.

ISSUE

What it is: the legal question you're answering, named by parties and cause of action.

Rules (W7 slides 15 and 21; Feedback 2):
  • Short: "no more than 2 to 3 lines of text (usually one sentence)".
  • Always a question, ending in "?".
  • It names both parties and the cause of action: "Can A bring an action against B for the Tort of Negligence?"
  • It asks about the whole cause of action, not just one element. "Students who framed the 'issue' as being whether A owed a duty of care to B were misguided."
  • One Issue per distinct claim. Two separate claims mean two ILACs (Feedback 4: undue influence and unconscionable conduct were best done as two ILACs).
GOOD
"Issue 1: Is the disclaimer a term of the contract between Paula and GCGR?"
"Issue 2: Will the disclaimer protect GCGR from liability?"
"Can Vinny bring an action against Bazza for the tort of negligence?"
"Did Davo have the authority to act on behalf of his principal Cristina, when accepting Patrizia's offer?"
BAD
"Did Bazza owe Vinny a duty of care?" (asks about one element only: "misguided")
"Even if the disclaimer is a term of the contract, is it outside the scope of the contract?" (assumes half the answer; rated "Average")
"Can Paula make a claim… under contract law? Is GCGR negligent against Paula?" (brings in an area the question didn't ask about; rated "Substandard")
LAW

What it is: every legal rule you'll use, each with its authority, and nothing you won't apply.

Rules (W7 slides 16–18, 22–23; Feedback 2–5):
  1. Begin by broadly defining the legal area ("A disclaimer is a statement that…").
  2. List the elements / ways the rule can be satisfied, each with its authority ("See: Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking"; "s 9(1) CLA"; "s 18 ACL").
  3. Explain any sub-tests in detail (e.g. the s 9(2) factors; the two causation tests in s 11(1); the Taco Bell / Annand tests for s 18; the "engaged in conduct" and "in trade or commerce" definitions in s 2).
  4. Case analysis where a case can be "meaningfully compared to or distinguished from" the facts, or where the lecturer flagged it as important (Bolton v Stone, Yates v Jones, Wakeling v Ripley, Roscorla v Thomas, Balmain New Ferry, Sydney City Council v West…). Give the case's facts in one or two lines, then what was held.
  5. Include the law on defences where they're relevant, and only those.
  6. Only use course cases and sections.
GOOD (W7 High Quality)
"A disclaimer is a statement that one of the parties will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of their contractual obligations. Disclaimers can be a term of a contract if they are: incorporated into a written and signed contract; or brought to a party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract is formed. See: Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking; or implied into the contract as a result of prior dealings."

"See: Balmain New Ferry Co v Robertson. Balmain New Ferry was a case where the disclaimer was only visible to the customer after payment for the Ferry had been made. However, the Court found that… the disclaimer was still a term of the contract due to Robertson having travelled… on previous occasions."
BAD
"Agreement = offer plus acceptance. Consideration." (wrong law: formation wasn't in issue; rated "Substandard: Stronger Application than Law")

"Disclaimers: Balmain v New Ferry. Contra Proferentum: White v Warwick." (case names with no rule or facts; rated "Substandard: Stronger Law than Application")

"Contract Law and Tort Law." (no rules at all)
APPLICATION

What it is: where most of the marks are. You take each rule from your Law section and decide it on these facts.

Rules (W7 slides 19–20, 24–25; Feedback 2, 3, 5):
  1. Mirror the Law section: same order, same sub-headings ("Remember to apply the law using a similar structure to how you set out your law section").
  2. Use the parties' names and the specific facts (numbers, quotes, "20 times in the past year", "in the change room").
  3. Compare or distinguish the case: "Similar to what happened in Balmain New Ferry, where…, in this question we are told that…"
  4. Argue both sides where facts are arguable: "GCGR can argue… Paula would argue…". Then say which argument is stronger.
  5. Apply causation separately to EACH head of damage, each under its own heading. Feedback 2: students who didn't "often failed to reach the standard… for a High Distinction (or even a Distinction)".
  6. Separate promises or claims get separate analysis (Feedback 3: the painting-delivery promise and the re-framing promise had to be split).
GOOD (W7 High Quality)
"GCGR can argue that the disclaimer is a term of the contract, and therefore binding, because of previous dealings. Similar to what happened in the case of Balmain New Ferry, where Mr Robertson was found to have travelled on the Ferry to work on many previous occasions, in this question we are told that Paula has been to the track 20 times in the past year. She therefore had plenty of opportunities to read the disclaimer and the disclaimer is therefore implied into the contract as Paula ought to have known about it."
BAD
"There was an offer which was accepted and enforced by consideration. There was a contract and a disclaimer." (no facts, no reasoning)

"GCGR can argue that the disclaimer is binding under a course of previous dealings." (right point but no fact ["20 times"] and no case comparison: "Average")

"…so GCGR is liable because of negligence." (wrong area)
CONCLUSION

What it is: the answer to your Issue, in one sentence.

Rules (W7 slides 21, 26): short; "rarely more than 1 sentence"; "always an answer to the Issue". In a multi-issue answer the final conclusion must cover each issue's outcome. Signal the standard: "On the balance of probabilities…" / "The stronger argument is…".
GOOD
"On the balance of probability, the stronger argument is that the disclaimer is a term of the contract, due to prior dealings."
"The stronger argument is that although the disclaimer is a term of the contract due to prior dealings, it does not protect GCGR from their own negligent actions. Therefore, Paula can sue GCGR for the loss of her laptop."
BAD
"Yes and yes."
"Paula can make the damages claim." (an answer with no reason, and it doesn't follow from any analysis)
"Even though the disclaimer is a term of the contract, it is outside the scope of the contract." (doesn't say what that means for Paula)

The W7 High Quality answer, assembled in full (mirror this layout)
Question (W7 workshop): Paula has driven go-karts at GCGR about 20 times in the past year, paying each time at the front counter. Her ticket includes a locker key. After paying she goes to the change room, where a large sign says: "GCGR is not liable for any loss or damage suffered by customers while on the GCGR premises however that loss or damage may arise or be caused." A thief takes locker keys from an open box on the unattended front counter and steals her laptop. Advise Paula whether she can claim damages under Contract Law?

Whether a disclaimer will effectively protect a business from liability for breach of contract depends upon two separate issues: (1) whether the disclaimer is in fact a term of the contract; and (2) whether the disclaimer will be interpreted as applying to the breach in question.

Opener: names the two issues so the marker can see your plan.

Issue 1 – Is the disclaimer a term?

Issue

Is the disclaimer a term of the contract between Paula and GCGR?

Law

A disclaimer is a statement that one of the parties will not be in breach despite failing to perform one or more of their contractual obligations. Disclaimers can be a term of a contract if they are: (a) incorporated into a written and signed contract (L'Estrange v Graucob); (b) brought to a party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract is formed (Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking; Olley v Marlborough); or (c) implied into the contract as a result of prior dealings.

Prior dealings: if the parties have entered into similar contracts in the past, and those previous contracts contained a particular disclaimer, then that disclaimer can become a term of the present contract.

In Balmain New Ferry Co v Robertson, the disclaimer was only visible to the customer after payment for the ferry had been made. However, the Court found that even though the disclaimer was not written into the contract or brought to the party's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed, it was still a term of the contract because Robertson had travelled on the ferry on previous occasions.

Case analysis = facts + held, in a separate paragraph, ready to be compared below.

Application

Written contract. Paula is not bound by the disclaimer under a written contract because GCGR did not incorporate it into the written contract she entered into.

Reasonable notice. The disclaimer was not brought to Paula's attention by reasonable notice before the contract was formed, because the first time any customer would see it was in the change room, after they had contracted with GCGR at the front desk.

Prior dealings. GCGR can argue that the disclaimer is a term of the contract because of previous dealings. Similar to Balmain New Ferry, where Mr Robertson had travelled on the ferry on many previous occasions, here Paula has been to the track 20 times in the past year. She therefore had plenty of opportunities to read the disclaimer, and it is implied into the contract as Paula ought to have known about it.

Same three points, same order as the Law. Each one uses a fact from the question.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the stronger argument is that the disclaimer is a term of the contract, due to prior dealings.

Issue 2 – Does it cover this breach?

Issue

Will the disclaimer protect GCGR from liability?

Law

If a disclaimer is uncertain or ambiguous and is contained in a contract between a business and a consumer, the court will interpret it contra proferentem, against the interests of the party that included it or seeks to rely upon it. If there is more than one way of interpreting the disclaimer, the Court will choose the way that favours the consumer and does not apply to the breach (White v John Warwick).

Disclaimers also do not exclude liability for acts occurring outside the scope of the contract. In Sydney City Council v West, a car-park attendant negligently allowed a person to take a set of car keys without proof of a ticket. The disclaimer did not cover this because the attendant's actions were outside the scope of the contract.

Application

Contra proferentem. The disclaimer is ambiguous because it is uncertain whether "any loss or damage suffered by customers" means damage customers cause themselves, damage from unavoidable and unforeseen circumstances, or damage from GCGR's own careless or negligent actions. As there is more than one interpretation, the Court will choose the one that goes against GCGR. GCGR will therefore be liable where the loss was caused by its own fault, such as leaving the keys unattended for a thief to steal.

Scope. Paula would argue that the contract's purpose is to let customers go-kart knowing that only they can access their belongings. This is very similar to Sydney v West, where the purpose of the car park was to let drivers leave their keys knowing only they could retrieve them on showing a ticket. Here, leaving keys unattended on a desk so that anyone could open the lockers is a negligent act that is outside the scope of what a reasonable person would contemplate under this contract.

Conclusion

The stronger argument is that although the disclaimer is a term of the contract due to prior dealings, it does not protect GCGR from its own negligent actions. Therefore, Paula can sue GCGR for the loss of her laptop.

For an exam answer, add a Remedies heading here (damages for the value of the laptop; see ⑥). The W13 slides say to always consider remedies.

2. Law

2a. Define the area (always first)

[Area] is [definition from the slides]. For [P] to succeed, [P] must establish [three] elements: (1) […]; (2) […]; and (3) […] ([authority]).

e.g. "A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another person. There are three requirements: the defendant owes the plaintiff a duty of care; the defendant breaches that duty; and the breach causes the plaintiff's loss (causation)."

2b. Each element: rule + authority (one paragraph each, with its own sub-heading)

[Element]: [rule in plain words]. See: [Case] / Section [X] of the [Act] provides that [paraphrase].
In determining [whether …], the court will consider [the factors / tests]: (a) […]; (b) […]; (c) […] ([s 9(2) CLA / Taco Bell; Annand]).

2c. Case analysis (its own paragraph)

In [Case], [one or two lines of facts]. The court held that [outcome] because [reason].

Do this for the 1–2 cases whose facts are closest to the question, or those flagged in lectures (Bolton v Stone, Yates v Jones / Schneider v Smith, Wakeling v Ripley, Roscorla v Thomas, Balmain, Sydney v West, Taco Bell…). Other cases can stay as "See: X".

3. Application (mirror the Law: same sub-headings, same order)

[Element heading]. Here, [element] is [likely / unlikely] to be satisfied because [specific fact with names and numbers].
Similar to [Case], where [case fact], in this question [matching fact]. Therefore, [same result].
Unlike [Case], where [case fact], here [different fact]. [Case] can therefore be distinguished, and [different result].
[P] would argue that […]. Alternatively, [D] may argue that […]; however, [why that's weaker]. The stronger argument is that […].
There is insufficient information as to […]; assuming that […], [result].
[Head of damage, e.g. Medical expenses ($36,000)]. But for [D]'s breach, [P] would not have […], so factual causation is satisfied (s 11(1)(a)). This loss is [a natural and ordinary consequence / too remote] of the breach, so it [is / is not] within the scope of liability (s 11(1)(b)).

Repeat that last block once for each loss: medical, lost wages, rehab/addiction, psychiatric, travel, and so on.

4. Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, [P] [can / is unlikely to] [succeed in …] because [one key reason].
(Final, multi-issue) Therefore, although [Issue 1 outcome], [Issue 2 outcome], and [P] [can / cannot] [overall result].

5. Defences (only if the facts raise one)

[D] may raise the defence of [contributory negligence]. [Rule] ([authority]). Here, [P] [was looking at his phone / had consumed alcohol / ignored warnings]. Similar to [Manley v Alexander], where […], [P]'s damages are likely to be reduced to reflect [his] share of responsibility.
[D] may argue [voluntary assumption of risk]; however, this defence is very difficult to establish because [P] did not have full knowledge of the precise risk that eventuated ([Agar v Hyde] distinguished).

6. Remedies (ALWAYS)

As [D] is likely to be liable, [P] may seek [damages / rescission / an injunction / s 236 damages]. The purpose of [damages] is to [place P in the position they would have been in had the wrong not occurred]. [P] could therefore recover [each head + amount that passed causation], but not [the heads that failed].

⑤ What markers reward vs penalise

REWARDED

  • Clear ILAC headings: "you MUST clearly use headings to represent each of the ILAC stages" (FB3).
  • "Clear, disciplined and effective ILAC structure; excellent signposting and organisation" (FB5).
  • "Concise but meaningful case analysis": facts and decision of a similar case in Law, compared in Application (FB3, FB5). Bands 6–7 need "meaningful case analysis in relation to each issue, rather than simply identifying or summarising the relevant authorities" (FB4).
  • Citing legislation and cases: the CLA, ACL sections, and the cases from lectures (FB2).
  • Explaining the tests inside a rule: s 9(2) factors with s 9(1); both s 11(1) causation tests; Taco Bell / Annand for s 18; s 2 definitions of "engaged in conduct" and "in trade or commerce" (FB2, FB5).
  • Causation applied separately to each head of damage, under headings (FB2).
  • "Persuasive and well-reasoned factual analysis": applying principles to "a novel and unfamiliar factual scenario" (FB3, FB5).
  • Spotting the subtle points: counter-offers, past consideration, literal truth and puffery, s 29(1)(a) alongside s 18 (FB3, FB5).
  • Using the course's ILAC Template layout (FB2).
  • "Structure and clarity are generally more important than flair" (FB4).

PENALISED

  • Not using ILAC: "Answers that do not adopt the ILAC methodology will be subject to significant mark deductions" (FB5).
  • "Large sections of bulky, uninterrupted text", or a "big wall of uninterrupted text" (FB3, FB5).
  • Failing to "clearly distinguish between the relevant legal principles and their application" (FB5).
  • Elements not "presented clearly and separately", and case analysis not in its own paragraph (FB5).
  • Issue framed as one element ("whether A owed a duty of care" was "misguided") (FB2).
  • Irrelevant law: Voluntary Assumption of Risk when it wasn't relevant; novel duty when an established duty existed; ss 54–56 for a competitor (FB2, FB5).
  • Naming s 18 and jumping to a conclusion: "It is not sufficient to simply identify s 18, state that conduct was misleading… and proceed immediately to a conclusion" (FB5).
  • Citing cases not taught: "a strong indication that students have not written their own answers" (FB2).
  • Merging separate promises or claims into one muddled analysis (FB3).
  • Copying out the question, which earns nothing (FB3).
  • Too short: tutorials needed 800+ words. Expect about 1,200+ per 25-mark exam answer (FB1–4).
"Even where a student demonstrates a strong understanding of the relevant law, the effectiveness of their legal analysis can be substantially diminished if their answer is poorly structured or difficult to follow" (FB5). Structure is marks.

⑥ Remedies and defences cheat list (as the course teaches them)

Taken only from the lecture slides (W3, W4, W6, W7, W8, W9, W10, W11, W12). Use these names and authorities exactly.

Tort (negligence and other torts): W3, W4

RemedyAs taught
Damages (the typical tort remedy)Purpose: compensate, placing the injured person "in the position they would have been had the tort not been committed… so far as money can do so". "Notions of punishment generally have no place."
Ordinary damagesRestore the plaintiff's position. Assessed "once and for all", so future losses must be estimated (e.g. future lost earnings).
NominalA right was breached but there's no compensable damage (e.g. trespass to land).
AggravatedCompensates humiliation or emotional distress (e.g. defamation).
ExemplaryThe defendant deliberately and maliciously disregarded the plaintiff's rights, so more than compensation is awarded.
Injunction / interim injunctionOrder to do or refrain from doing something. Suits ongoing torts (nuisance, trespass, defamation). An interim injunction restrains the activity until the final decision.
Negligence defences (W3 slides 51–56): "Two important defences":
  • Contributory negligence: if the plaintiff contributed to their own loss, liability is apportioned. Ingram v Britten (plaintiff drove the tractor at speed; defendant didn't secure the frame). Manley v Alexander (plaintiff drank 12 stubbies and slept in the middle of the road; defendant negligently drove over him). W4 example: driver 70%, pedestrian on her phone 30%.
  • Voluntary assumption of risk: "very difficult to establish". The plaintiff had full knowledge of the precise risk and freely accepted it. A full defence. Agar v Hyde (rugby scrum). CLA ss 13–14 obvious risk; s 15 no duty to warn of an obvious risk; ss 17–19 dangerous recreational activity.
  • Also consider who to sue: vicarious liability of the employer (Century v Northern Ireland; Deatons v Flew).
Only raise VAR if the facts really show knowing acceptance of the risk (FB2).
Other-tort defences (W4):
  • Trespass: accident; consent; necessity; self-defence; defence of property (proportionate).
  • Defamation: justification (substantial truth); absolute privilege (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 26); fair reporting; honest opinion (s 31).

Contract (Contract 1–3): W6, W7

RemedyAs taught
Rescission (equitable)Court order declaring the contract terminated or unenforceable for: complete non-performance, breach of a condition, misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence or unconscionability. Restores the parties to their original positions. Lost if: restitution is impossible; the contract is affirmed; there's delay; third-party rights intervene; the contract is executed; or the party seeking relief acted unconscientiously.
DamagesRestore the plaintiff to where they'd be "if the contract has been performed properly". Available for any breach of contract, breach of a collateral contract, or fraudulent or negligent misrepresentation. Types: nominal, exemplary, liquidated, unliquidated. The plaintiff must mitigate their loss (Payzu Ltd v Saunders).
Equitable remediesWhere damages are not a satisfactory remedy: specific performance (perform the contract) or an injunction ("more likely").
Statutory remediesIf the breached term was implied by the ACL or Sale of Goods legislation, the extra statutory remedies apply.
Condition vs warrantyBreach of a condition → terminate (repudiation) + damages (Poussard v Spiers). Breach of a warranty → damages only (Bettini v Gye).
MisrepresentationFraudulent → rescind + damages (Derry v Peek). Negligent → damages available. Innocent → rescind, but no damages at common law (Oscar Chess v Williams). Parties usually prefer the ACL, which has wider remedies.
Collateral contract / oral promiseBreach entitles the innocent party to damages.
Promissory estoppelThe promisor keeps the promise (specific performance), or the promisee is compensated for the detriment (damages) (Waltons Stores v Maher; Giumelli v Giumelli).
Disclaimer as a "defence"A defendant in breach relies on an exclusion clause. Run the two issues: is it a term, and does it cover this breach? (W6/W7)

ACL: W9 slides 61–62

Remedies a court can grant
  • Damages to anyone who suffered loss because of the contravention: s 236.
  • Compensation orders for injured persons: ss 237–241.
  • Injunctions: ss 232–235.
  • Rescission of the contract.
  • Orders declaring a contract void, varying it, refusing to enforce it, ordering a refund, compensation or repair.
  • Pecuniary penalties (ss 224–231); adverse publicity order (s 247); non-punitive orders (community service, compliance programs).
  • The ACCC may take investigative, administrative or court action.
Typically "a broader range of remedies… than is available under contract law" (W9). The course slides don't set out the major/minor-failure remedy sections for guarantees, so use the list above.
ACL defences (Part 4-1)
  • s 207: reasonable mistake of fact, including reasonable reliance on information supplied by another person.
  • s 208: act or default of another person, an accident or a cause beyond the business's control, and the business took reasonable precautions and exercised due diligence.
  • Unfair terms: the term is transparent (s 24(2)–(3): plain language, legible, clearly presented, readily available) and/or reasonably necessary to protect legitimate interests. The slides label transparency as a "possible defence".

Competition (CCA Part IV): W10

Fine (s 76: for a corporation, the greatest of $50m, 3× the benefit, or 30% of turnover; $2.5m for a non-corporation) · injunction (s 80) · damages (s 82) · compensation orders (s 79B) · probation and community service · corrective advertising (s 86C(2)(d)) · adverse publicity (s 86D) · other orders including specific performance, rescission and variation (s 87) · enforcement of ACCC undertakings / declarations (s 87B).

IP: W8

Copyright remedies: damages (s 115(2)–(3)); injunction (s 115(2)); account of profits (s 115(2)–(3)). Trade marks: injunction, plus either damages or an account of profits. Patents: a court may refuse damages or an account of profits if the defendant didn't know the technology was patented.
Copyright defences: no substantial part copied; fair dealing for research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, reporting news, or judicial proceedings / professional advice (CA ss 40–43, 103A–104). "Fair" depends on the purpose and extent of use. W8 slide: "also consider defences."

Agency and partnership: W11, W12

  • Agent acted within actual or apparent authority → the principal is bound to the third party, and the agent has no personal liability. (W12 Q&A conclusion: "The contract would / would not be rescinded.")
  • No authority → the agent is personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority (Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]). The agent may also be liable in deceit, negligence or under s 18 ACL.
  • Agent breaches a fiduciary duty (follow instructions: Bertram Armstrong v Godfray; etc.) → the agent is personally liable to the principal for the harm caused.
  • Principal is vicariously liable for an employee-agent's torts committed while carrying out instructions.
  • Partnership: partners are jointly liable for partnership debts, and may be jointly and severally liable for a fellow partner's torts (Kendall v Hamilton).

Mark-winning checklist

  • ☐ Issue = 1 sentence, "?", names both parties + cause of action (not "did D owe a duty?")
  • ☐ Law opens by defining the area
  • ☐ Every element has a case or section
  • ☐ Tests explained (s 9(2) factors · s 11(1) both tests · Taco Bell / Annand · s 2 definitions)
  • ☐ ≥1 case analysis per issue: facts + held in Law, "Similar to / Unlike" in App
  • ☐ App mirrors the Law's order and sub-headings
  • ☐ Parties' names + specific facts + amounts used
  • ☐ Both sides argued → "the stronger argument is…"
  • ☐ Causation done separately per head of damage
  • ☐ Missing facts flagged + assumption stated
  • ☐ Conclusion answers the Issue, "on the balance of probabilities"
  • ☐ Remedies heading present; Defences only if relevant
  • ☐ NO irrelevant areas (excluded by "only"; VAR; ss 54–56 for a competitor)
  • ☐ NO cases from outside the course
  • ☐ Headings, short paragraphs, no walls of text

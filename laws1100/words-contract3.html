Opening

What this means: two short paragraphs at the very top. The first lists what you will discuss. The second explains the basic idea: a contract only binds someone who agreed freely and genuinely. A vitiating factor = something that spoils that agreement (a lie, a mistake, a threat, a dominating relationship, someone exploiting a weakness). Void = the contract never existed. Voidable = the contract is valid until the innocent person chooses to cancel it.

Write — overview

Whether [Client] can have the [contract with X / transfer of the property / will] set aside depends on whether [his/her] consent was vitiated by [unconscionable conduct, undue influence, misrepresentation and/or mistake][, and whether the contract has been discharged by frustration]. Each is considered in turn, followed by remedies.

Write — Law (general)

A contract will only be enforceable against a party if that party entered into it willingly, with free and voluntary consent. Factors that affect genuine consent are called vitiating factors, and include misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence and unconscionable conduct. Where a vitiating factor is established, the contract may be void (of no contractual effect) or voidable (valid until set aside at the option of the innocent party).

Issue 1: Unconscionable conduct

What this means: Unconscionable conduct = the stronger person took unfair advantage of a weakness in the other person that they knew about. Special disadvantage = something about the weaker person (old age, illness, poor English, grief, can't see) that stops them looking after their own interests. Equity = court rules based on fairness that sit beside ordinary contract rules. No relationship is needed before the deal, so this works even for strangers like a door-to-door salesperson. There are 3 elements: (1) special disadvantage, (2) the stronger party knew, (3) they took unfair advantage.

Write — Issue

Can [Client] have the [contract/transfer] with [Stronger party] set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?

Write — Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. While mere harshness of a term is not enough at common law, equity will intervene where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger). As Kitto J stated in Blomley v Ryan, equity intervenes "whenever one party to a transaction is at a special disadvantage in dealing with the other party because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage of the opportunity thus placed in his hands."
Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, a contract will be set aside where: (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage in relation to the stronger party; (2) the stronger party knew or ought to have known of that disadvantage; and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it, so that it was unconscientious to procure the agreement in the circumstances. In Amadio, Mr and Mrs Amadio, elderly Italian migrants with limited written English, signed a guarantee and mortgage over their property for their son's company's debts, believing it was limited to $50,000 and six months when it was unlimited. The bank manager brought the document to their home, knew of their limited English and the company's financial difficulties, and did not explain it. The High Court set the guarantee aside.

Issue 2: Undue influence

What this means: Undue influence = one person has so much power over another, because of a relationship of trust or dependence, that the weaker person's decision is really the stronger person's. It needs a relationship that existed before the deal, so strangers don't count. Presumed = for some relationships (parent–child, doctor–patient etc.) the court automatically assumes influence, so you don't have to prove it. For every other relationship (carer–patient, spouses…) you must prove the stronger person dominated. Onus shifts = once that is shown, the stronger person has to prove the gift was the weaker person's own free, well-understood choice.

Write — Issue

Can [Client] have the [transfer/will/contract] in favour of [X] set aside on the basis of undue influence?

Write — Law

Undue influence occurs where a party has a degree of controlling influence and power over another person and dominates that person's will — "every case where influence is acquired and abused, where confidence is reposed and betrayed". A transaction is voidable at the election of the weaker party where: (1) the parties are in a pre-existing relationship; (2) one party has a controlling influence over the other; (3) the stronger party takes advantage of that influence for their own benefit; and (4) the weaker party is not exercising independent judgement, so there is no genuine free and voluntary consent.
Certain recognised relationships automatically raise a presumption of undue influence, including parent and child, guardian and ward, trustee and beneficiary, doctor and patient, lawyer and client, and religious adviser and disciple (Allcard v Skinner). In other relationships, the party alleging undue influence must show that the other party exercised a dominance or controlling influence over them. The courts have recognised that this may arise between spouses, principal and agent, bank and client, accountant and client, employer and employee, and carer and patient (Johnson v Buttress). Once this is shown, the onus shifts to the stronger party to prove that the transaction was the "pure voluntary and well-understood act" of the weaker party.
In Johnson v Buttress, Mr Buttress, an elderly widower who was illiterate and of limited intelligence, became dependent on Mrs Johnson after the death of his wife and transferred his only significant asset, his home, to her without proper independent advice. The High Court held that there was a relationship of influence and dependence, that Mrs Johnson could not show the transfer was his free and well-understood act, and set the transfer aside.

Issue 3: Misrepresentation

What this means: a misrepresentation = a false statement about a fact (something true or untrue right now, or in the past) that persuaded the client to sign. A puff = obvious sales exaggeration ("best deal ever"); a promise or a prediction about the future is also NOT a misrepresentation. There are 3 types depending on what the speaker knew: fraudulent (knew it was false, or didn't care), negligent (honest but careless, and should have checked), innocent (honestly believed it). The type matters because only fraudulent and negligent get damages (money); innocent gets the contract cancelled only.

Write — Issue

Can [Client] rescind the contract with [X] on the basis of misrepresentation?

Write — Law

A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact made by one party to another which induces the other party to enter into the contract, making the contract voidable. The elements are: (1) a false statement of fact made by the representor to the representee; (2) concerning an existing fact or past event, not a statement of future intention; (3) made before or at the time the contract is concluded; and (4) intended to induce, and in fact inducing, the other party to enter into the contract. Honest statements of opinion, puffs or exaggerated statements, promises and statements of future intention are not misrepresentations. Mere silence is generally not a misrepresentation, unless a failure to disclose turns a statement into a half-truth.
A fraudulent misrepresentation is made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly (Derry v Peek). In Derry v Peek, directors stated in a prospectus that their company had the right to run steam trams when it in fact needed government consent; as they honestly believed the statement, it was not fraudulent. A negligent misrepresentation is an honest but careless statement made where a special relationship gives rise to a duty of care, about a serious or business matter, where the maker knows the recipient will rely on it, reliance is reasonable and damage results. An innocent misrepresentation is made with no intention to deceive (Oscar Chess Ltd v Williams, where a seller honestly but wrongly described a car as a 1948 model based on its log book). Rescission is available for all three; damages are available for fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation, but not innocent misrepresentation.

Issue 4: Mistake

What this means: a mistake = someone misunderstood a basic fact when they signed. The general rule is that a mistake does NOT get you out of a contract (caveat emptor = "let the buyer beware"). Only big mistakes count. Common mistake = both sides wrong about the same thing. Mutual mistake = each side meant something different. Unilateral mistake = only one side was wrong, and the other side knew and took advantage. Non est factum (Latin, "it is not my deed") = the person signed without being able to read it AND didn't even know what kind of document it was. If the other side caused the mistake by saying something false, that is misrepresentation instead.

Write — Issue

Is the contract between [Client] and [X] void for [unilateral mistake or non est factum / common mistake / mutual mistake]?

Write — Law (general)

As a general rule, a mistake by one party does not make a contract unenforceable (caveat emptor). Only in exceptional circumstances is a contract void for mistake: the mistake must exist prior to or at the time of contracting, must be fundamental, and must be a mistake rather than a misrepresentation.

Issue 5: Duress

What this means: duress = someone was forced to sign by a threat of harm: to their body or family (duress to the person), to their belongings (duress to goods), or to their business by unlawful pressure (economic duress). The threat only has to be ONE of the reasons they signed. Ordinary sales pressure ("sign now, limited-time offer") is NOT a threat, so in most exam questions you dismiss duress in one sentence.

Write — Issue

Can [Client] have the contract set aside on the basis of duress?

Write — Law

Duress is pressure exerted by one party to coerce or compel another to contract on particular terms, and makes the contract voidable. It requires that one party expressly or impliedly threatened the other with harm, and that the threat contributed to the decision to enter the contract. Duress may be to the person (Barton v Armstrong, where a threat to have Barton killed if he did not sign was enough, as it need only be one reason for signing), to goods or property (Hawker Pacific v Helicopter, where a helicopter was withheld until the owner agreed to pay more), or economic, which requires the pressure to be in some way unlawful (North Ocean Shipping v Hyundai, where a shipbuilder threatened not to deliver a tanker unless paid 10% more).

Issue 6: Frustration

What this means: frustration = after the contract was signed, something happened that nobody caused (a fire, flood, bushfire, cancelled event, new law) and it makes the contract impossible or completely different from what was agreed. It is not a "vitiating factor" (the agreement was fine at the start) but it IS in Contract Law 3. The result is discharge = the contract ends and both sides are released from future obligations. "Harder" or "more expensive" is NOT enough.

Write — Issue

Has the contract between [Client] and [X] been discharged by frustration as a result of the [bushfire]?

Write — Law

A contract may be discharged by operation of law through frustration, where something happens that is not the fault of either party and makes performance impossible or only possible in a way neither party anticipated. Performance must be substantially different, not merely more difficult or expensive. A contract is frustrated if: (1) a supervening event has made performance completely impossible or impossible in the way originally envisaged; (2) neither party caused the event; (3) the contract did not provide for the event, expressly or impliedly; and (4) it would be unjust to compel either party to proceed. In Taylor v Caldwell, a music hall hired for a series of concerts was destroyed by fire before the concerts, and the contract was frustrated.

Write — Law

The primary remedy for a vitiating factor is rescission, an equitable remedy by which the court declares the contract terminated or unenforceable and, as far as possible, restores the parties to their original positions. The right to rescind is lost if substantial restitution is impossible, the contract has been affirmed, there has been delay, third party rights have intervened, the contract has been executed, or the party seeking relief has acted unconscientiously. Damages, which aim to restore the innocent party to the position they would have been in had the misrepresentation not occurred, are available for fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation but not innocent misrepresentation; the plaintiff must mitigate their loss (Payzu Ltd v Saunders).

Full mini-answer — 2025 Semester 1, Question 2: Juan and Evergreen Solar (25 marks)

Facts in one line: Jake (ES salesperson) visits Juan (85, Spanish immigrant, poor English, poor eyesight, depressed since his wife died, stepson away), says the 35-page contract is "risk-free" and will "cut his power bills in half", insists he sign now; Juan pays a $2,500 deposit; hidden clause 10(a)(ii) = $4,000 exit fee for Juan, clause 22(b)(vi) = ES can terminate free; two weeks later a bushfire makes installation impossible for over a year.

Whether Juan can have his contract with Evergreen Solar Pty Ltd (ES) set aside depends on whether his consent was vitiated by unconscionable conduct, misrepresentation or mistake, and whether the contract has been discharged by frustration. Each is considered in turn, followed by remedies. A contract will only be enforceable against a party who entered into it willingly, with free and voluntary consent. Factors that affect genuine consent are called vitiating factors. Where established, the contract may be void, or voidable at the option of the innocent party.

Issue 1: Unconscionable conduct

Issue. Can Juan have the contract with ES set aside on the basis of unconscionable conduct?

Law. Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Equity intervenes where there is no "reasonable degree of equality between the contracting parties" (Longmate v Ledger), that is, where one party "is at a special disadvantage… because illness, ignorance, impaired faculties… affect his ability to conserve his own interests, and the other party unconscientiously takes advantage" (Kitto J, Blomley v Ryan). Under Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio: (1) the weaker party must be under a special disadvantage; (2) the stronger party must know or ought to know of it; and (3) the stronger party must take unfair advantage of it. In Amadio, elderly Italian migrants with limited written English signed an unlimited guarantee for their son's company, believing it was limited; the bank manager knew of their poor English but did not explain the document, and the guarantee was set aside.

Application. Juan was under a serious special disadvantage: he is 85, an immigrant who "still has difficulty understanding English", has poor eyesight, and suffers from depression following his wife's death. These are the "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties" described in Blomley v Ryan. Similar to the Amadios, Juan is elderly with limited English, and his position is worse, as his eyesight meant he could not read the 35-page contract at all. Jake knew of this because Juan told him directly; like the bank manager in Amadio, Jake had actual knowledge. Jake took unfair advantage: instead of letting Juan wait for his stepson, he called it a "once-in-a-lifetime deal", insisted Juan sign immediately and said it was "risk-free". As in Amadio, he explained nothing, and the contract is one-sided: clause 10(a)(ii) imposes a $4,000 exit fee on Juan, while clause 22(b)(vi) lets ES terminate without penalty. ES may argue Juan was an adult who chose to sign; however, Jake's pressure after being told Juan could not read the contract was clearly unconscientious.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, Juan can have the contract set aside for unconscionable conduct, as all three elements in Amadio are satisfied.

For completeness, undue influence is unlikely, as it requires a pre-existing relationship and Juan met Jake that afternoon. Duress is also unlikely: although Jake pressured Juan, there was no threat of harm (cf Barton v Armstrong).

Issue 2: Misrepresentation

Issue. Can Juan rescind the contract on the basis of misrepresentation?

Law. A misrepresentation is a false statement of existing fact, made before or at the time of contracting, which is intended to and does induce the other party to contract; it makes the contract voidable. Opinions, puffs, promises and statements of future intention are not misrepresentations. A misrepresentation is fraudulent if made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly (Derry v Peek, where directors who honestly believed their prospectus statement about steam trams were not fraudulent). An innocent misrepresentation is made without intention to deceive (Oscar Chess Ltd v Williams). Damages are available for fraudulent and negligent, but not innocent, misrepresentation.

Application. The statements that the system would "instantly cut his power bills in half" and "provide guaranteed energy independence" concern future results and are likely puffs or promises. By contrast, "risk-free" is a statement about an existing fact, the content of the contract Jake handed over. It was false, as clause 10(a)(ii) imposes a $4,000 exit fee. It was made before signing and induced Juan to sign "trusting Jake's assurances". As ES's representative presenting ES's own contract, Jake knew of the fee or was reckless; unlike the directors in Derry v Peek, he could not honestly believe it was risk-free.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, the "risk-free" assurance was a fraudulent misrepresentation, entitling Juan to rescind and claim damages.

Issue 3: Mistake

Issue. Is the contract void for unilateral mistake or non est factum?

Law. Generally a mistake does not affect a contract (caveat emptor). A unilateral mistake makes a contract unenforceable where a party made a serious mistake about a fundamental aspect and the other party knew of it and took advantage (Taylor v Johnson, where a buyer who knew the seller thought the price was per acre had the contract set aside). Non est factum may apply where a person had a good reason for not reading a document and was mistaken about its fundamental nature.

Application. Juan had a good reason for not reading the contract, but he knew it was a solar contract; his mistake concerned one term, so non est factum fails. Unilateral mistake is arguable: similar to the buyer in Taylor v Johnson, Jake knew Juan could not read the contract and took advantage, though ES would say an exit fee is not fundamental.

Conclusion. Mistake is arguable but weaker than Issues 1 and 2.

Issue 4: Frustration

Issue. Has the contract been discharged by frustration as a result of the bushfire?

Law. A contract is frustrated where (1) a supervening event makes performance impossible or radically different, (2) neither party caused it, (3) the contract did not provide for it, and (4) it would be unjust to compel performance (Taylor v Caldwell, where a music hall hired for concerts burnt down).

Application. The bushfire, like the fire in Taylor v Caldwell, made installation impossible, and neither party caused it. ES may say performance is only delayed, but a delay of over a year for a system promised "within a week" is substantially different. Clause 10(a)(ii) covers voluntary cancellation, not natural disasters. It would be unjust to make Juan keep paying for nothing.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, the contract has been frustrated.

Remedies

Rescission restores the parties to their original positions, but is barred by impossibility of restitution, affirmation, delay, third party rights, execution or unclean hands. None apply: Juan acted within two weeks, unlike the five-year wait in Leaf v International Galleries; nothing was installed, so there is no affirmation or execution; the $2,500 deposit can be returned; no third party is involved; and Juan acted honestly. The court would order the deposit refunded, with no exit fee payable. Juan may also claim damages for the fraudulent misrepresentation, subject to mitigation (Payzu Ltd v Saunders).

Overall conclusion

Juan should be advised that the contract is likely to be set aside, primarily for unconscionable conduct and also for Jake's "risk-free" misrepresentation, entitling him to rescission and a refund of his $2,500 deposit; in any event, the bushfire has likely frustrated the contract.

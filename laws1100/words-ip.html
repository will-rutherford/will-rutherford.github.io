Copyright Issue 1: Protection

What this means: copyright = the right to stop people copying the expression of an idea (the actual words, notes, picture) — never the idea itself. It is automatic: no registration needed. Something is protected if it is (1) a "work" (or other protected thing like a recording), (2) original (the author made it themselves — it doesn't need to be new or clever), and (3) in material form (written down, saved or recorded somewhere).

WRITE — Issue

Is [the lecture / photo / manual / song] protected by copyright under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth)?

WRITE — Law

Intellectual property is an intangible product of intellectual effort, and IP law gives its owner the right to prevent others from copying, using or exploiting it without permission. Copyright is the legal right to prevent unauthorised copying of the expression of an idea; it does not protect the idea itself. Copyright is regulated by the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) ('CA') and arises automatically, without registration. A creation is protected if: (1) it is a 'work' or 'subject matter other than works'; (2) it is original; and (3) it is expressed in a material form.

Work. Section 32 protects original literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works. A literary work is the expression of an idea in text, such as a book, article, email, manual or written information: Mirror Newspapers v Queensland Newspapers [1982]. The CA also separately protects 'subject matter other than works', including sound recordings (s 89), films (s 90), broadcasts (s 91) and published editions (s 92), so several layers of copyright may exist in the one product.
Original. A work is original if it originates from the author through independent intellectual effort rather than being copied; it need not be new or unique, and the threshold is low (s 32).
Material form. Copyright protects the expression of an idea, not ideas or facts themselves: John Fairfax & Sons v Australian Consolidated Press (1960). 'Material form' means 'any form (whether visible or not) of storage' (s 10). In Donoghue v Allied Newspapers (1938), a jockey told his stories to a journalist, who wrote them up; the journalist, who created the particular expression, was the author, and the jockey had no copyright merely because he supplied the ideas orally.

Copyright Issue 2: Ownership

What this means: who owns the copyright — not always the person who made it. Owning the physical thing (a DVD, a printed photo) is NOT owning the copyright. Default: the author (creator) owns it. But an employee's work done as part of their job belongs to the employer. An independent contractor (hired for a job, not an employee) keeps it unless agreed.

WRITE — Issue

Who owns the copyright in [the work]?

WRITE — Law

Ownership of copyright is separate from ownership of the physical object. The author is generally the first owner (s 35(2)). However, where a work is created by an employee in pursuance of the terms of their employment, the employer generally owns the copyright (s 35(6)); the creator must be an employee rather than an independent contractor, and the work must be made under their employment duties: Redrock Holdings v Hinkley (2001). An independent contractor generally keeps copyright unless otherwise agreed; a person who commissions a work for a private or domestic purpose is generally the first owner (s 35(5)); and these default rules may be changed by agreement (s 35(3)). University IP policies may allow academics to keep copyright in certain scholarly works.

Copyright Issue 3: Infringement

What this means: infringement = breaking copyright, by doing one of the owner's exclusive rights (things only the owner may do: copy, publish, perform, share online, adapt) without permission. Three requirements: a substantial part was copied (judged by importance, not amount — even a few bars can count), the copy is objectively similar (looks/sounds like the original), and there's a causal connection (the copy came from the original, not made independently).

WRITE — Issue

Have [D1 and D2] infringed the copyright in [the work]?

WRITE — Law

Under s 31 of the CA, the owner has the exclusive rights to reproduce the work in a material form, publish it, perform it in public, communicate it to the public and make an adaptation. Infringement occurs when a person exercises any of these rights without permission, and requires: (1) a substantial part was copied; (2) objective similarity; and (3) a causal connection.
Substantial part is qualitative, looking at the importance of what was copied rather than its amount. In Hawkes & Son v Paramount Film Service (1934), a short but distinctive part of a march in a newsreel was substantial; in EMI Songs Australia v Larrikin Music (2011), two bars of "Kookaburra" in the flute riff of "Down Under" were substantial.
Objective similarity compares the particular expression, not the general idea. In Zeccola v Universal Studios (1982), "Great White" copied the characters, situations and sequence of events of "Jaws", not just the idea of a killer shark, and so infringed.
Causal connection requires that the copy be derived from the original rather than created independently; it may be inferred from access plus similarity, and need not be conscious: in Bright Tunes v Harrisongs Music (1976), George Harrison subconsciously copied "He's So Fine" in "My Sweet Lord".

Trade mark infringement

What this means: a trade mark = a name, logo, shape, colour or sound that tells customers which business a product comes from. Trade marks MUST be registered to be protected under the Act. Infringement = someone uses the same or a deceptively similar mark (close enough to confuse customers) on the same/similar goods. If it isn't registered → skip to passing off.

WRITE — Issue

Can [P] bring an action against [D] for infringement of its registered trade mark under the Trade Marks Act 1995 (Cth)?

WRITE — Law

A trade mark is a distinctive word, phrase or symbol used in commercial dealings to show a connection between a business and its product (s 17 TMA). It may be letters, a word, a name, a device, packaging, a shape, a colour, a sound or a scent: in Coca-Cola v All-Fect (1999), Coca-Cola's registered contour bottle shape stopped All-Fect selling lollies in the same shape. Trade marks must be registered to be protected under the TMA. A registered trade mark is infringed if: (1) the owner's mark is registered under the TMA; (2) the defendant uses a mark substantially identical with or deceptively similar to it; and (3) on goods or services the same as or closely related to those it is registered for. A mark is deceptively similar if it nearly resembles the registered mark and is likely to mislead or cause confusion (s 44): in Torpedoes v Thorpedo (2003), 'THORPEDO' was deceptively similar to the registered 'TORPEDOES' for clothing. Protection is limited to the registered classes, unless the mark is well known in Australia: Eastman v John Griffiths (1898); Virgin Enterprises v Klapsas (2001).

Passing off

What this means: a tort (civil wrong) where a business pretends — openly or by hints like copying the look — that it is connected with, or approved by, another business or person. Works even if nothing is registered. Three elements: a misrepresentation of connection, made in trade, intended to deceive buyers.

WRITE — Issue

Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of passing off?

WRITE — Law

Where a trade mark or get-up is unregistered, the owner may still prevent its misuse through the tort of passing off. A defendant commits passing off if they misrepresent themselves or their product as having some kind of connection with the plaintiff. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant made a misrepresentation, express or implied, that its goods or services are connected with the plaintiff or have the plaintiff's endorsement or approval; (2) the misrepresentation was made in the course of trade; and (3) the misrepresentation was intended to deceive potential purchasers. In Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989), a shoe advertisement parodying the knife scene from Crocodile Dundee was passing off, because viewers would assume Paul Hogan had endorsed or was connected with the shoes.

Breach of confidence

What this means: a tort for misusing secret information you were trusted with (a recipe, customer list, business plan, an idea pitched in private). Unlike copyright, it can protect an idea that was only spoken. Three elements: the info is confidential, it was given under an obligation of confidence (said or implied, e.g. employee), and it was used without permission.

WRITE — Issue

Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of breach of confidence?

WRITE — Law

Breach of confidence is a tort committed when a person uses or discloses information given to them on a confidential basis without the owner's consent. It requires: (1) the information is confidential in nature, not trivial or public knowledge; (2) it was given in circumstances where an obligation of confidence was expressly made known and accepted, or implied from the context, such as an employer/employee relationship: NP Generations v Feneley (2001); and (3) there has been an unauthorised use or threatened use. The information need not be in material form: in Fraser v Thames Television (1984), an idea for a TV series pitched verbally was protected. A confidentiality agreement or a reasonable restraint of trade clause in an employment contract may also give a separate contractual claim.

Full mini-answer — 2021 S1 Q2: Alex and Travis (all blanks filled)

Whether Alex and Travis have committed an intellectual property breach depends on whether copyright protects the lecture, who owns that copyright, whether Alex and Travis infringed it, and whether any defence applies.

Issue 1: Protection

Issue: Is the lecture given by Professor Butler and Charley protected by copyright under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth)?

Law: Copyright is the right to prevent unauthorised copying of the expression of an idea; it does not protect the idea itself. It arises automatically under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) ('CA'). A creation is protected if: (1) it is a 'work' or 'subject matter other than works'; (2) it is original; and (3) it is expressed in material form. Section 32 protects literary works, being text such as written information (Mirror Newspapers v Queensland Newspapers [1982]); sound recordings are separately protected (s 89). A work is original if it comes from the author's independent intellectual effort; the threshold is low. 'Material form' means 'any form (whether visible or not) of storage' (s 10). In Donoghue v Allied Newspapers (1938), a journalist who wrote up a jockey's stories was the author, because he created the expression.

Application: Professor Butler read out comprehensive teaching notes he prepared before class. They are written text, so a literary work; original, because he prepared them himself; and in material form, being written and stored (s 10). Charley's talk was unscripted, so material form is arguable. On one view it was never stored by Charley and so is unprotected; on the other, Travis's recording stored it (s 10). Applying Donoghue, the author is whoever created the expression — Charley, who chose the words, not Travis, who merely pressed record. Travis's recording itself is a sound recording (s 89), but that gives him no right to the lecture content.

Conclusion: Copyright likely subsists in Professor Butler's notes, and probably in Charley's words once recorded.

Issue 2: Ownership

Issue: Who owns the copyright in Professor Butler's and Charley's material?

Law: The author is generally the first owner (s 35(2)), but an employee's work made in pursuance of their employment belongs to the employer (s 35(6)); it must be made under their duties: Redrock Holdings v Hinkley (2001). Agreements and university IP policies may change this (s 35(3)).

Application: Professor Butler is UB's "usual lecturer", so an employee, and teaching notes are central to his duties; under s 35(6) UB likely owns them, unless a UB policy lets him keep them (s 35(3)). Charley is "another academic at UB"; a guest talk is arguably within his duties (UB owns), but it drew on his separate work as a corporate lawyer, so it may fall outside them (Charley owns under s 35(2)). As the facts are silent, I assume it was part of his duties.

Conclusion: UB is likely the owner of both sessions — in any event, not Alex or Travis.

Issue 3: Infringement

Issue: Have Travis and Alex infringed the copyright in the lecture?

Law: Under s 31 the owner has exclusive rights to reproduce, publish, perform in public, communicate to the public and adapt the work. Infringement requires: (1) a substantial part copied — qualitative: two bars of "Kookaburra" were substantial in EMI Songs Australia v Larrikin Music (2011), and a short distinctive extract in Hawkes & Son v Paramount Film Service (1934); (2) objective similarity, comparing expression not ideas: Zeccola v Universal Studios (1982); and (3) a causal connection, which may even be subconscious: Bright Tunes v Harrisongs Music (1976).

Application: Travis reproduced the lecture in a material form by recording it, without the permission of UB, which has a no-recording policy. Alex procured the recording and then sold it, publishing it. The whole two-hour lecture was copied; if two bars were substantial in EMI v Larrikin, the entire lecture clearly is. A recording is an exact reproduction; unlike Zeccola, no comparison of plot is needed. Travis recorded it directly and deliberately, a stronger case than Bright Tunes.

Conclusion: Travis likely infringed by reproducing the lecture, and Alex by procuring and selling it, unless a defence applies.

Issue 4: Defences

Issue: Can Travis or Alex rely on fair dealing?

Law: Fair dealing (CA ss 40–43, 103A–104) permits use that is fair and for research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, reporting news, or judicial proceedings or professional advice. Fairness depends on purpose and extent.

Application: "No substantial part" fails, as the whole lecture was copied. Alex may argue the recording was for studying a lecture he missed. Against: the whole lecture was copied, "secretly", in breach of UB's policy, and Travis made it for someone else, so it was not fair. Alex's sales are commercial and fall outside fair dealing altogether.

Conclusion: Fair dealing might arguably cover Alex's private listening, but not Travis's secret recording, and clearly not the sales.

Remedies

UB (or Charley for his part, if he owns it) may seek an injunction to stop further copying and sales (s 115(2)), and either damages or an account of the profits Alex made from selling the recording (s 115(2)–(3)). On the balance of probabilities, both Travis and Alex have infringed copyright in the lecture, and Alex's sales attract the greatest liability.

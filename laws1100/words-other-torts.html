PART A — OTHER TORTS

Opener + general trespass law

What this means: a tort = a civil wrong you can sue for money. Trespass = directly interfering with someone's land, goods or body. It's actionable per se = you can sue even if no damage happened. All five trespass torts use the same 4 elements — only element 1 changes.

Whether [P] can recover against [D] under Tort Law depends on whether [D]'s conduct amounts to the torts of [battery / assault / false imprisonment / trespass to land / private nuisance / defamation / deceit / passing off], whether any defence applies and, if so, what remedies are available.

Write once, before the first trespass tort:

A tort is a civil wrong that allows the aggrieved person to sue the wrongdoer for the recovery of damages. Trespass is an intentional tort committed when the defendant interferes with the person or property of the plaintiff, and takes three forms: trespass to land, trespass to goods and trespass to the person. Trespass is "actionable per se", meaning the plaintiff need not prove actual damage. Each form requires (1) an interference, (2) that is direct, (3) that is intentional or negligent, and (4) for which there is no consent or lawful justification.

Trespass to land

What this means: someone goes onto, stays on, or puts something on (or over — airspace counts) land another person possesses. Exclusive possession = the right to control the land (owner or tenant).

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of trespass to land?
Law: A person commits trespass to land if they unlawfully enter, remain on, or put something on the plaintiff's land. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant interfered with their exclusive possession of land; (2) the interference was direct; (3) it was intentional or negligent; and (4) there was no consent or lawful justification. Land includes the airspace above it: in Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco [1957], a sign protruding only four inches into the airspace of premises leased to Kelsen was a trespass, and an injunction was granted.
Application: [P] has exclusive possession of [the land] as [owner / tenant], and [D] interfered with it by [entering / parking / installing a sign that overhangs by X cm]. Similar to Kelsen, where a four-inch protrusion was enough, the small size of the intrusion is irrelevant. The interference is a direct result of [D]'s own act, not something drifting over (which would be nuisance). [D] acted deliberately; even if [D] believed it was its own land, the act was voluntary. [P] never consented and [objected on DATE].
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D] has committed trespass to [P]'s land, and [P] is entitled to an injunction requiring removal, as in Kelsen, together with damages (nominal if no loss is proven).

Trespass to goods

What this means: taking, moving, searching or using someone's things without permission. The plaintiff only needs to have possession (e.g. a borrower), not ownership. No case on the slides — argue from the elements.

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of trespass to goods?
Law: Trespass to goods is an interference by the defendant with goods in the possession of the plaintiff, such as taking or using them without permission. The plaintiff must show that: (1) the defendant interfered with their possession of goods; (2) the interference was direct; (3) it was intentional or negligent; and (4) there was no consent or lawful justification. The plaintiff need not be the owner, provided they had rightful possession at the time, and need not prove damage.
Application: [P] was in rightful possession of [the bag]. [D] directly and deliberately interfered by [taking and emptying it]. [D] may argue [P] impliedly consented by [handing it over]; however, consent must be voluntary, and [P] handed it over only because [D demanded it while detaining her].
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D] committed trespass to [P]'s goods, although damages are likely to be nominal.

Battery

What this means: any unwanted physical contact — no injury needed. But ordinary everyday contact (a tap on the shoulder to get attention) doesn't count.

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of battery?
Law: Battery is a form of trespass to the person involving the actual application of physical force. It requires: (1) physical interference with another person's body; (2) that is direct; (3) intentional or negligent; and (4) without consent or lawful justification. In Battiato v Lagana [1992], Moynihan J held that "the direct intentional imposition of any unwanted physical contact on another person constitutes the tort of Battery", with no need to prove harm. However, contact "generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of everyday life" is excepted (In re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation) [1990]). In Rixon v Star City [2001], a casino employee who placed his hand on a banned patron's shoulder to get his attention did not commit battery.
Application: [D] [grabbed P's wrist / pushed / dragged]; following Battiato v Lagana, it does not matter whether this caused injury. [D] will argue that, similar to Rixon, the contact was merely to get [P]'s attention. However, Rixon is distinguishable: there the employee merely placed a hand on the shoulder, whereas here [D] [gripped and pulled P X metres], which goes well beyond everyday contact. The contact was direct and deliberate, and [P] did not consent, in fact [pulling away].
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D]'s [grab] constitutes battery against [P].

Assault

What this means: a threat that makes someone reasonably expect they're about to be hit/touched — no touching needed. It doesn't matter if they weren't actually scared, and "do X or else" threats still count.

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of assault?
Law: A person commits assault if: (1) they cause another person to have a reasonable apprehension of direct, imminent and harmful or offensive physical contact; (2) the act is direct; (3) intentional or negligent; and (4) without consent or lawful justification. No contact need occur. It is immaterial whether the person was actually put in fear: Brady v Schatzel [1911]. A conditional threat is still an assault: Rozsa v Samuels [1969]. In Stephens v Myers (1830), a defendant who advanced on the plaintiff with a clenched fist but was stopped before reaching him committed assault.
Application: [D] [raised a fist / stepped towards P / said "…"]. A reasonable person in [P]'s position would apprehend imminent contact because [D was within arm's reach / larger]. Similar to Stephens v Myers, where advancing with a clenched fist was enough, [D]'s [gesture] threatened imminent contact. That the threat was conditional ("[come with me or …]") does not prevent it being an assault (Rozsa v Samuels). [D] may argue [P] was not frightened; however, under Brady v Schatzel that is immaterial. The threat was direct, deliberate and unjustified, as [P] posed no threat.
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D] committed assault against [P].

False imprisonment

What this means: completely stopping someone leaving — no lock or force needed (being told "you must come with me" by someone asserting authority is enough). If there's a reasonable other way out, it's not imprisonment; a dangerous way out (window, moving car) doesn't count.

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of false imprisonment?
Law: A person commits false imprisonment if: (1) they cause another to be totally restrained, with no reasonable avenue of escape; (2) the act is direct; (3) intentional or negligent; and (4) without consent or lawful justification. Physical force is not required: in Symes v Mahon [1922], a man who went with a police officer who wrongly said he had a warrant for his arrest was falsely imprisoned. Restraint must be total: in Bird v Jones (1845), a person blocked from one side of a bridge was merely "obstructed", as another route existed. The escape must be reasonable: in Burton v Davies (1953), a hitch-hiker whose only way out was to jump from a moving truck was falsely imprisoned.
Application: [P] was [locked in / told she could not leave] for [duration]. Similar to Symes v Mahon, [P] complied only because [D] asserted authority ("[…]"), which is restraint, not consent. [D] may argue [P] could have left by [the window], so she was merely obstructed as in Bird v Jones. However, like jumping from the moving truck in Burton v Davies, [that exit] risked injury and was not a reasonable means of escape. The restraint was direct and deliberate. Even if a brief check was justified, any justification ended once [nothing was found].
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D] falsely imprisoned [P] for [at least the X minutes after …].

Private nuisance

What this means: indirect interference with someone's use and enjoyment of their land — noise, smell, smoke, vibration, lights coming from next door. Unlike trespass, there must be actual harm. The court weighs 6 factors to decide if it's unreasonable. No nuisance case on the slides — the marks are in the factors. (Public nuisance is not assessed.)

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of private nuisance?
Law: Nuisance is an act by the defendant that indirectly interferes with the plaintiff's use and enjoyment of land; unlike trespass, it is indirect. A person commits private nuisance if: (1) they interfere with another's use and enjoyment of private land as an indirect result of their action; (2) the other person has an interest in that land, such as an owner or tenant; (3) the other person suffers actual harm or damage; (4) the interference is intentional or reckless; and (5) it is sustained and unreasonable. In deciding unreasonableness, the court balances the right to undisturbed enjoyment of property against the right to carry on activities, considering the severity, duration, location, sensitivity, deliberateness or maliciousness of the interference, and whether precautions were taken.
Application: The [noise / smell] travels from [D]'s premises onto [P]'s land, an indirect interference with [P]'s use of it for [purpose]. [P] is the [owner / tenant], so has an interest in the land. [P] has suffered [$X lost bookings / refunds]. [D] knew of it after [complaints on DATES] and continued, which is at least reckless. Severity: […]. Duration: [every night since …]. Location: [D] may argue that in [a mixed-use area] some noise is expected; however […]. Sensitivity: [D] may argue [P]'s business is especially sensitive; however […]. Deliberateness: [D ignored complaints]. Precautions: [D refused $X insulation]. On balance, the interference is unreasonable.
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D]'s [noise] is a private nuisance, and because it is ongoing [P] should seek an injunction as well as damages.

Defamation

What this means: publishing (to at least one other person) a statement that makes people think less of someone. Three things: it's defamatory (hurts reputation), it identifies them (even without naming), and it's published. Defences: it's true, said in parliament/court, a fair report, or an honest opinion.

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of defamation?
Law: A person commits defamation if they publish to a third party, in spoken or written form, a statement about another person that would damage their reputation. The plaintiff must establish that: (1) the statement was defamatory; (2) it identified them; and (3) it was published to a third party. The test is whether the esteem in which the person is held by the community is diminished: Radio 2UE Sydney v Chesterton [2009]. In Mirror Newspapers v World Hosts (1979), a newspaper's implication that a restaurant owner had gone bankrupt was defamatory. The plaintiff need not be named if reasonably identifiable, and publication may be in any form; failing to remove material may itself give rise to liability. Defences include justification (substantial truth), absolute privilege (Defamation Act 2005 (Qld) s 26), fair reporting, and honest opinion on a matter of public interest (s 31).
Application: The statement "[quote]" conveys that [P] is [unhygienic / dishonest / insolvent / incompetent]; applying Chesterton, an ordinary reader would think less of [P]. Similar to World Hosts, where an implication of financial trouble damaged a restaurant owner, this damages [P]'s trading reputation. Although [P] is not named, "[the new café on X St]" reasonably identifies [P]. It was posted on [platform] and read by [X people]. Justification fails because [it is untrue: evidence]; honest opinion fails because [it is a statement of fact / not substantially true]; privilege and fair reporting are irrelevant.
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D] has defamed [P], and no defence is available.

Deceit

What this means: a knowing lie about a fact, told to get someone to buy/sign, which they rely on and lose money. An honest opinion or estimate is not deceit.

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of deceit?
Law: A fraudulent misrepresentation made to induce a contract is the tort of deceit. It requires: (1) a statement of fact made to another knowing it is false; (2) made with the intention that it be relied upon; (3) the other person relies on it; and (4) suffers harm as a result. A statement of opinion honestly and reasonably held is not deceit: in Bisset v Wilkinson (1927), a statement of how many sheep a farm could hold was an honest opinion, and the maker was not liable.
Application: [D] stated "[…]". Unlike the estimate in Bisset v Wilkinson, this is a verifiable fact, and [D] knew it was false because [evidence]. It was said [during negotiations, in answer to P's question] to persuade [P] to [buy]. [P] relied on it by [paying $X without inspection] and suffered [$X loss].
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D] committed the tort of deceit and is liable for [P]'s resulting losses.

Passing off

What this means: pretending (often by copying a name/logo/look or using a celebrity) that your business is connected with or endorsed by someone else. Full version on the IP words page.

Issue: Can [P] bring an action against [D] for the tort of passing off?
Law: A defendant commits passing off if they misrepresent themselves or their product as having some connection with the plaintiff. The plaintiff must show: (1) a misrepresentation, express or implied, that the defendant's goods or services are connected with the plaintiff or have its endorsement or approval; (2) made in the course of trade; and (3) intended to deceive potential purchasers. In Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989), a shoe advertisement parodying a scene from Paul Hogan's Crocodile Dundee was passing off, because it implied Hogan's association with the shoes. Passing off overlaps with s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law.
Application: By [using the name "…" / a similar logo], [D] impliedly represented a connection with [P]; similar to Pacific Dunlop v Hogan, no express claim is needed. It was done in selling [product] for profit. Intention to deceive can be inferred from [timing / copying several features / D's own words], and [customers were actually confused].
Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, [D] has committed passing off. [P] may also have a claim under s 18 ACL, but that falls outside Tort Law.

Vicarious liability

What this means: the employer (the business, which has money) can be made to pay for torts its employee commits while doing their job — even if done in a wrong way.

Law: An employer may be liable for the acts of its employees. The court asks whether the act occurred as part of the employee's authorised work (Century v Northern Ireland (1942)) and within the course of employment (Deatons v Flew (1949)).
Application: [Employee] was employed by [Business] as [a security guard], and [stopping suspected shoplifters] is exactly the work [he/she] was authorised to do. Although [grabbing / locking the door] was wrongful, it was an unauthorised way of doing authorised work, done during [his/her] shift for the employer's benefit.
Conclusion: Therefore [Business] is likely vicariously liable for [Employee]'s torts, and [P] should sue [Business].

Remedies (tort)

What this means: damages = money to put P back where they'd be without the tort. Injunction = court order to stop (for ongoing torts). Pick every row that fits.

The typical remedy in tort is damages, the objective being to restore the plaintiff, so far as money can, to the position they would have been in had the tort not been committed. An injunction, a court order to do or refrain from doing certain acts, is appropriate where the tort is ongoing, such as nuisance, trespass or defamation.

PART B — COMPETITION LAW (CCA PART IV)

Opener + universal CCA law paragraph

What this means: competition law stops businesses ganging up or bullying to reduce competition (the ACL is about consumers — different topic). Per se = illegal outright, no need to prove harm to competition (cartels, misuse of market power, RPM). Conditional = illegal only if it substantially lessens competition (SLC) in the market (exclusive dealing, mergers, secondary boycotts, s 45 agreements). Market = the area where the firms actually compete.

Whether [A and/or B] have contravened Part IV of the CCA depends upon [two] issues: first, whether [A] engaged in [resale price maintenance]; and second, whether [A and B] engaged in [cartel conduct].

Write once, in the first issue's Law:

Australia's competition laws are primarily contained in Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) ('CCA'), which is enforced by the ACCC. The objective of Part IV is to prohibit trade practices that have the effect of substantially lessening competition. Some conduct is prohibited outright (per se), including cartel conduct, misuse of market power and resale price maintenance. Other conduct is prohibited only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market, being "the area of close competition between firms … the field of rivalry between them": Re Queensland Co-operative Milling Association (1976). 'Substantial' means large, weighty, real or of substance.

For conditional conduct, the SLC sentence: "To determine whether competition is substantially lessened, it is necessary to define the market, ask whether competition was or is likely to be lessened, ask whether that lessening is substantial, and, if not, whether that was the purpose." Add "There is no indication that ACCC authorisation was sought." (The ACCC can authorise anything except misuse of market power.)

Cartel conduct / price fixing

What this means: competitors agreeing (even informally — a handshake) to fix prices, cut output, rig tenders, or split up customers/areas. Per se AND a crime. Just charging the same price with no contact (following the leader) isn't enough.

Issue: Have [A and B] engaged in cartel conduct by [fixing prices / allocating customers] in contravention of the CCA?
Law: A cartel provision is defined in s 45AD as a provision of a contract, arrangement or understanding between businesses that are, or would otherwise be, in competition, which fixes prices, restricts the quantity of goods, rigs bids or tenders, or allocates customers, suppliers or territories (TPC v TNT (1995); ACCC v Visy (2007)). It is a per se contravention (ss 45AJ, 45AK) and a criminal offence (ss 45AF, 45AG): in CDPP v Nippon Yusen Kabushiki Kaisha [2017], NYK was fined $25 million for fixing freight rates, rigging bids and allocating customers. Price fixing requires (1) a contract, arrangement or understanding with a competitor, (2) containing a provision with the purpose or likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices (ACCC v Alice (1997)). An informal understanding suffices, but parallel conduct such as price leadership is not necessarily collusion: TPC v Email (1980).
Application: [A] and [B] both [supply X in area Y], so they compete in the same field of rivalry (Re QCMA). Although nothing was written, [the handshake / "Fine by me"] shows an arrangement or understanding. [B] may argue it simply followed [A]'s price, as in TPC v Email; however, unlike mere price leadership, here there was [direct communication before the identical rise]. The provision that both would [charge $X] has the purpose of fixing prices. (Allocation:) The agreement that [A keeps north, B keeps south] allocates customers and territories, a separate cartel provision, as in CDPP v NYK. As cartel conduct is per se, it is irrelevant whether competition was actually lessened.
Conclusion: Therefore, [A and B] have likely engaged in cartel conduct in contravention of ss 45AJ and 45AK, and may face criminal prosecution under ss 45AF and 45AG.

Misuse of market power (s 46)

What this means: a dominant business using its power to crush or block a competitor (e.g. pricing below cost until the rival leaves, then raising prices = predatory pricing). Never authorisable.

Issue: Has [A] misused its market power in contravention of s 46 of the CCA?
Law: Section 46 applies only to a business with a substantial degree of market power, which must not take advantage of that power to eliminate or substantially damage a competitor, or to prevent entry into that or any other market (s 46; ACCC v Ticketek [2011]). Following the 2017 amendments, the focus is on whether the conduct has the purpose or effect of substantially lessening competition, while allowing legitimate competition even if it harms inefficient rivals. It cannot be authorised by the ACCC. In Boral Besser Masonry v ACCC (2003), BBM priced below cost to drive out C&M Bricks, but there was no misuse because BBM lacked substantial market power and could not recoup its losses.
Application: The relevant market is [product + area]; the ACCC would argue for this narrow market, while [A] would argue for a wider one of […]. [A] holds [X%] with [few rivals / high barriers to entry], so unlike BBM in Boral, it likely has substantial market power. [A] cut prices below cost only in [the town where the rival opened], suggesting a purpose of eliminating [rival], and could recoup losses by [raising prices later], distinguishing Boral. Against: [A] may say this was legitimate competition.
Conclusion: On balance, [A] has likely misused its market power in breach of s 46, and this cannot be authorised by the ACCC.

Resale price maintenance (s 48)

What this means: a supplier forcing retailers to sell at or above a minimum price (no discounting). Per se. A recommended or maximum price is fine.

Issue: Has [supplier] engaged in resale price maintenance in contravention of s 48 of the CCA?
Law: Section 48 prohibits resale price maintenance, where a supplier dictates the price at which a retailer resells its product; it is per se. Under ss 96–100, a business engages in it if it: (1) imposes a minimum price on resellers; (2) sets a price retailers are likely to understand is a minimum; (3) agrees with retailers they will not advertise below a specified price; (4) induces a retailer not to discount; or (5) threatens to refuse supply to force compliance (TPC v Sony (1990); ACCC v Chaste (2005)).
Application: [Supplier] told retailers they "[must not sell below $X]", imposing a minimum price that retailers would understand as mandatory (limbs 1–2). The ban on advertising below [$X] is a separate act (limb 3). By warning that discounters would ["have their supply reviewed"] and then cutting off [retailer], it threatened and refused supply to force compliance (limbs 4–5), as in TPC v Sony. [Supplier] may argue the price was only recommended, but ["must not" / the cut-off] shows otherwise. As RPM is per se, its commercial reasons are irrelevant, and no authorisation was sought.
Conclusion: Therefore, [supplier] has likely engaged in resale price maintenance in breach of s 48.

Exclusive dealing (s 47)

What this means: restricting who a customer can buy from or sell to. Full-line forcing = "I'll only supply you if you don't stock my rival". Third-line forcing = "you can only buy mine if you also buy someone else's product". Conditional — must prove SLC.

Issue: Has [A] engaged in exclusive dealing in contravention of s 47 of the CCA?
Law: Exclusive dealing occurs when a business restricts another's freedom to choose with whom, in what amount or where they buy products, and under s 47 it is prohibited only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market. Full-line forcing is refusing to supply unless the buyer agrees not to buy from a competitor, not to resupply a competitor's products, or not to resupply to a particular place (TPC v Massey (1983)). Third-line forcing is making supply conditional on the customer also buying another business's product (s 47(6); ACCC v Black & White Cabs [2010]): (1) the purchaser wants one product but another is forced on them; (2) the forced product is a third party's; (3) the purchaser cannot get the wanted product without it; and (4) it substantially lessens competition.
Application: [C] wants [product], but [A] will not supply it unless [C] [also buys T's insurance / does not stock rival's brand], which is [third-line / full-line] forcing. The relevant market is […]; because [A] supplies [most] of it, rivals are shut out of [x%] of customers, a real and weighty lessening of competition. Against: if the market is defined widely as […], the effect is insubstantial.
Conclusion: On balance, [A]'s conduct [is / is not] likely to contravene s 47.

Boycotts

What this means: refusing to deal with someone to shut them out. Primary = A + B themselves refuse to deal with C. Secondary = A + B pressure C (no dispute with C) to stop dealing with D.

Issue: Have [A and B] engaged in a [primary / secondary] boycott in contravention of the CCA?
Law: A boycott is action that prevents, or is intended to prevent, another from buying or selling in a market (ss 45D, 45DA, 45DB, 45DD). A primary boycott occurs where two or more businesses agree to collectively refuse to deal with a target competitor, supplier or customer (s 45; TPC v JW Bryant (1978)). In ACCC v Australian Medical Association (WA) (2001), the AMA negotiated common fees for doctors at Joondalup Health Campus and threatened collective withdrawal of services; this was price fixing and a primary boycott, with $285,000 in penalties and costs. A secondary boycott is where businesses pressure another business, with whom they have no dispute, to stop dealing with the target (ss 45D, 45DA; ACCC v CFMEU (2017–18)); it is harder to prove and prohibited where it substantially lessens competition.
Application: (Primary) [A and B] agreed that they would [stop dealing with / withdraw services from C]; similar to AMA (WA), where the threat of collective withdrawal was enough, this is a primary boycott. (Secondary) [A and B] pressured [C], with whom they had no dispute, to stop dealing with [D], by [threat]; this [would / would not] substantially lessen competition in [market] because […].
Conclusion: Therefore, [A and B] have likely engaged in a [primary / secondary] boycott.

Mergers & acquisitions (s 50)

What this means: two businesses joining, or one buying another. Allowed unless it would substantially lessen competition in a substantial market. Conditional.

Issue: Would [A]'s acquisition of [B] contravene s 50 of the CCA?
Law: A merger occurs when two or more organisations combine, and an acquisition when one acquires the ownership or assets of another. These may bring economies of scale, risk-spreading and the replacement of poorly performing organisations, but s 50 prohibits those with the effect or likely effect of substantially lessening competition in a substantial market for goods or services, having regard to the factors in s 50(3). The parties may seek ACCC authorisation by showing the public benefits outweigh the detriments.
Application: The market is […], a substantial market because […]. After the acquisition, [A] would hold [x%], leaving [only one rival]. For [A]: [efficiencies / economies of scale]. Against: [higher prices, less choice].
Conclusion: The acquisition is [likely / unlikely] to contravene s 50 unless authorised by the ACCC.

Catch-all (s 45(1)(a)): "A business cannot make a contract, arrangement or understanding with competitors that has the purpose, effect or likely effect of substantially lessening competition (ACCC v Visy)." Use the 4 SLC steps.

Penalties & remedies (CCA)

The Federal Court may order a fine for each contravention (s 76), having regard to the nature and extent of the contravention, the loss caused, the circumstances, and any previous similar conduct. For a corporation, the maximum is the greatest of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or, if that cannot be determined, 30% of annual turnover; for a non-corporation, $2.5 million. The court may also grant an injunction (s 80) [e.g. requiring X to resume supply], damages to those who suffered loss such as [retailer] (s 82), compensation orders (s 79B), corrective advertising and adverse publicity orders (ss 86C(2)(d), 86D), other orders such as rescission or variation (s 87), and accept enforceable undertakings (s 87B). (Cartel:) Criminal prosecution is also possible under ss 45AF and 45AG, as in CDPP v NYK, where a $25 million fine was imposed.

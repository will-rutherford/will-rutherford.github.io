Issue 1: Existence of a partnership

Issue

Were [A, B and C] carrying on [business name] in partnership under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld), such that each is the agent of the others?

Law

"A partnership is the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit": Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) ('PA') s 5(1). There are no registration requirements or legal formalities, and the partnership agreement may be written, oral or wholly implied from the conduct of the partners; a partnership exists as long as the 4 requirements are satisfied, regardless of the intention of the parties. Each partner is both the principal and the agent of the other partners, and each has unlimited personal liability for the partnership's debts: Kendall v Hamilton (1879).

First, persons: at least 2 persons are required, and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115.
Second, carrying on a business: a business includes a "trade, occupation or profession" (PA s 3), and 'carrying on' requires some continuity or repetition of trading activities. A single transaction or one-off project is usually not a partnership: Smith v Anderson (1880). However, if the parties begin working together to establish an identifiable business, the partnership commences from that date: Khan v Miah [2000].
Third, in common: each must act on behalf of the others as well as themselves. An employer and employee carry on a business together but are not partners, because the employer does not act on behalf of the employee: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Silent partners are still partners and are still liable.
Fourth, with a view of profit: the parties must be seeking to make a profit, and they remain a partnership even if they expect or make a loss: Minter v Minter (2000).

= Partnership: 2+ people running a business together for money. No paperwork needed. Every partner can bind the others and is personally liable for the firm's debts (their own house and savings).

Issue 2: Authority of [partner] to bind the firm

Issue

Did [A] have the authority to act on behalf of [the firm] and [his/her] partners [B and C] when [signing the $X contract with 3P for … / purchasing …], such that they are liable to [3P]?

Law

Each partner in a partnership is the agent of the firm and of the other partners, and has express, implied and apparent authority to act on their behalf when dealing with third parties such as financiers, suppliers and customers. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party. Ways in which an agent can be authorised include actual authority — express or implied — or apparent authority. Actual authority arises out of a "consensual agreement" between principal and agent: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964].

Express authority is authority expressly granted by the other partners. Implied authority is the authority to do all the usual things necessary to carry out the express authority and to carry on the business of the partnership, such as selling the firm's property, buying goods of the kind usually employed in the firm's business, hiring employees, borrowing money and incurring and paying debts. Implied authority can be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985).

For apparent authority, under s 8 PA every partner is an agent of the firm, and acts "for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm" bind the firm and the partners, unless the partner had no authority and the third party knew this, or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Therefore, where a partner acts without actual authority, the other partners are still liable if 4 requirements are satisfied: (1) the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm, judged by what the firm's usual business is and what transactions such a firm would usually engage in: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) that business was carried on in the usual way, as otherwise the third party should suspect a lack of authority: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person was a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of actual authority, relying in good faith and without notice: Crouch v IPG (2013). The partnership agreement binds only the partners, not third parties.

Partners are jointly and personally liable for the firm's debts, and may be jointly and severally liable for torts and crimes committed by a fellow partner: Kendall v Hamilton (1879). A partnership is not a separate legal entity, although it may sue and be sued.

= Jointly liable: the partners together owe the whole debt. Severally: each partner can be sued alone for the whole amount. Req 1 = WHAT was done (the right kind of deal for this firm?). Req 2 = HOW it was done (the normal way?).

Issue 3: Personal liability of [partner] to [3P]

Only if Issue 2 found NO authority. Same law as Tab 3 — the slides repeat it for partners.

Issue

As [A] had no authority to bind the firm, can [3P] hold [A] personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

Law

An agent will be personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed that they were making the contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent did not in fact have authority to act on behalf of the principal; and (3) the third party relied upon the agent's representation, and would not have entered into the contract in its absence. See: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]. The agent may also be liable in the torts of deceit or negligence, as well as for breach of s 18 of the ACL.

Issue 4: Breach of fiduciary duties by [partner]

Issue

Did [A] breach the fiduciary duties [he/she] owed to [his/her] partners [B and C]?

Law

The relationship between partners is fiduciary and based on trust, requiring each to act in good faith for the common good of the partnership. Partners owe duties to each other at common law and under the partnership legislation, including: not to profit personally from their position or from information gained through it: Chan v Zaccharia (1984); not to put themselves in a position of conflict of interest without keeping their partners informed; to fully disclose to the other partners all matters likely to affect the partnership: Law v Law (1905); PA s 31; to account for any private profits made without the other partners' consent: Birtchnell v Equity (1929); PA s 32; and not to carry on a business of the same nature in competition with the partnership: PA s 33.

Filled-in example — 2025 Semester 2, Q2: StageCorp v CT Events

Callum and Tessa started CT Events (Sydney) in mid-2022: small corporate and private events under 100 guests; they hire rather than buy equipment; website and social media. In early 2023 Robert joins: no formal agreement, introduced to clients "as part of the team", shared office, joint account, profits split equally, decisions by majority vote; each does occasional freelance work. In August 2024 Robert signs a $180,000 contract with StageCorp Pty Ltd for a custom stage "on behalf of CT Events", paying an $18,000 deposit from the joint account, and tells StageCorp his goal is large-scale concerts and festivals — "provided he could convince Callum and Tessa". Callum and Tessa refuse to pay. Advise StageCorp how to recover the remaining $162,000.

As CT Events operates in Sydney, NSW legislation technically applies; this answer assumes the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) ('PA') is equivalent.

Issue 1: Existence of a partnership

Issue. Were Callum, Tessa and Robert carrying on CT Events in partnership under the PA, such that each is the agent of the others?

Law. "A partnership is the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit": PA s 5(1). No formalities are required. Each partner is principal and agent of the others, with unlimited personal liability: Kendall v Hamilton (1879). First, there must be at least 2 and generally no more than 20 persons: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115. Second, a business with continuity; a one-off project is not enough: Smith v Anderson (1880); the partnership starts when they begin working together: Khan v Miah [2000]. Third, in common; an employer and employee are not partners: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Fourth, a view of profit: Minter v Minter (2000).

Application. There are three persons, within the limit of 20. CT Events has operated since mid-2022 with a website and growing client base; unlike Smith v Anderson, this is a continuing trade. Consistent with Khan v Miah, Robert became a partner when he began working in the business in early 2023, despite no formal agreement. They share an office and joint account, split profits equally, decide by majority vote, and Robert was introduced as part of the team, so each acts on behalf of the others. On the other hand, Callum and Tessa may argue the freelance work shows otherwise; however, unlike the employee in Degiorgio v Dunn, Robert shares profits and has an equal vote. Profits are split, so there is a view of profit.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Callum, Tessa and Robert are partners under s 5(1) PA. Therefore, Robert is an agent of the firm and of Callum and Tessa.

Issue 2: Authority of Robert to bind the firm

Issue. Did Robert have the authority to act on behalf of CT Events and his partners Callum and Tessa when signing the $180,000 contract with StageCorp, such that they are liable to StageCorp?

Law. Each partner is the agent of the firm and the other partners, with express, implied and apparent authority. Implied authority covers the usual things needed to carry on the firm's business, unless overridden by contrary agreement or instructions: Construction v Hexyl (1985). Under s 8 PA, the firm is still bound if: (1) the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) in the usual way: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person was a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of authority: Crouch v IPG (2013). Partners are jointly and personally liable for the firm's debts: Kendall v Hamilton.

Application. There is no evidence Callum or Tessa expressly authorised the purchase; they were "outraged". StageCorp will argue buying equipment is usual for an events business; however, as in Construction v Hexyl, any implied authority is overridden by the firm's practice of hiring equipment and deciding by majority vote. Therefore, Robert acted outside his actual authority. As to s 8, first, applying Mercantile v Garrod, CT Events runs small functions under 100 guests; a stage for "large-scale concerts and festivals" is a different kind of business. Second, the firm usually hired, so a $180,000 purchase by one partner was not the usual way; as in Goldberg v Jenkins, StageCorp should have suspected a lack of authority. Third, StageCorp knew Robert was a partner: he signed on behalf of CT Events and paid from the joint account. Fourth, Robert said the purchase depended on whether "he could convince Callum and Tessa", so StageCorp was on notice and did not rely in good faith without notice (Crouch v IPG). Therefore, Robert did not have apparent authority.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Robert had no actual or apparent authority, so CT Events, Callum and Tessa are not bound by the stage contract. Therefore, StageCorp cannot enforce the contract against Callum or Tessa; its remedy lies against Robert.

Issue 3: Personal liability of Robert to StageCorp

Issue. Can StageCorp hold Robert personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

Law. An agent is personally liable if: (1) they claimed to contract on behalf of the principal; (2) they did not in fact have authority; and (3) the third party relied on that representation and would not otherwise have contracted: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020].

Application. First, Robert signed "on behalf of CT Events" and paid the deposit from the joint account. Second, he had no authority. Third, StageCorp would not have built the stage for Robert personally. On the other hand, Robert may argue there was no reliance because he said he still needed his partners' support; however, by signing in the firm's name and paying a deposit from its account, he represented that the firm was committed.

Conclusion. On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely find that Robert is personally liable to StageCorp for breach of warranty of authority. Therefore, StageCorp would be entitled to damages from Robert of up to the $162,000 balance, less what it recovers by taking back and reselling the stage. In the alternative, if s 8 applied, all three partners would be jointly liable for the $162,000 (Kendall v Hamilton).

1 · Learn it in 20 minutes

Big picture. The ACL (TW9, T6) governs how a business deals with consumers. Competition law (TW10) governs how it deals with competitors. "Freedom of contract" is not unrestrained: competitors can't agree to whatever they like, because competition brings economic growth, product quality, lower prices, jobs and innovation. The rules are in Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) (CCA), whose objective is to prohibit trade practices that substantially lessen competition. The ACCC enforces it. (History: the TPA 1974 was renamed the CCA in 2010; the 2017 Harper reforms changed misuse of market power.)

The two key concepts

  • Market = "the area of close competition between firms … the field of rivalry between them" — Re Queensland Co-operative Milling Association (1976). Defining the market is the starting point. The complainant wants it narrow (easier to show SLC); the defendant wants it wide.
  • Substantially lessening competition (SLC). "Substantial" = large, weighty, real, of substance, not insubstantial. Steps: (1) define the market → (2) did the conduct lessen competition, or would it be likely to? → (3) was the lessening substantial? → (4) if not, was the purpose to substantially lessen competition?

THE split (slide 13)

ACCC authorisation: on application, the ACCC can authorise agreements that lessen competition, price fixing, boycotts, exclusive dealing, RPM and mergers if the public benefit outweighs the public detriment. It can never authorise misuse of market power.

1 · Cartel conduct (ss 45AD, 45AJ, 45AK civil; ss 45AF, 45AG criminal)

A cartel provision (s 45AD) is a provision in a contract, arrangement or understanding between two or more businesses that are (or would be) in competition with each other, which:

  1. fixes prices;
  2. controls or restricts the quantity of goods (to inflate prices);
  3. rigs bids or tenders;
  4. allocates customers, suppliers or territories.

It is per se: illegal regardless of its actual effect on competition (ss 45AJ, 45AK), and it is also a criminal offence (ss 45AF, 45AG). Cartels can arise in any industry, between local small businesses or multinationals. TPC v TNT (1995); ACCC v Visy (2007). CDPP v Nippon Yusen Kabushiki Kaisha (NYK) [2017] was the first successful criminal cartel prosecution: NYK, shipping cars to Australia, was party to provisions on freight rates, bid rigging and customer allocation, and was fined $25 million. Wigney J: anti-competitive conduct "will not be tolerated and will be dealt with harshly".

Price fixing (the most common cartel form; s 45AD; ACCC v Alice (1997)). Two requirements: (1) a contract, arrangement or understanding with one or more competitors; (2) containing a provision with the purpose or likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices. There's no need to agree an exact price: an agreed formula, exchanging price-setting data, restricting output to lift prices, or setting the same rebates/discounts all count. An informal "arrangement or understanding" is enough. But mere parallel conduct (price leadership: one firm sets a price and others follow, e.g. petrol stations) is not necessarily collusion — TPC v Email (1980).

2 · Misuse of market power (s 46)

Applies only to a business with a substantial degree of market power (e.g. major supplier, deep pockets). It can't take advantage of that power to eliminate or substantially damage a competitor, or to prevent entry into that or any other market (s 46; ACCC v Ticketek [2011]). The 2017 object: target anti-competitive conduct by firms with substantial power "while allowing legitimate pro-competitive behaviour even if this results in harm to inefficient competitors". The slides treat it as per se, and the ACCC cannot authorise it.

Predatory pricing: pricing below cost to wipe out a competitor, then raising prices. Boral Besser Masonry v ACCC (C&M Bricks) (2003): BBM cut prices below cost to drive C&M out and deter new entrants. The High Court held there was no misuse, because (1) BBM did not have substantial market power, and (2) a firm cannot be said to "take advantage" of power if it has no intention (ability) of recouping its losses.

The current s 46 (post-2017) is framed around conduct with the purpose, effect or likely effect of substantially lessening competition. The slide still describes the "eliminate / damage a competitor" formulation and calls it per se. In an answer, write the slide test, then add one line: "Following the 2017 amendments, the focus is on whether the conduct has the purpose or effect of substantially lessening competition." That way you're covered either way.

3 · Resale price maintenance (s 48; ss 96–100)

A supplier tells a retailer how to sell (e.g. bans discounting). Per se. A business engages in RPM if it: (1) imposes a minimum price on resellers; (2) sets a price retailers are likely to understand is a minimum; (3) agrees with retailers they won't advertise below a specified price; (4) induces a retailer not to discount; or (5) threatens to refuse supply to force compliance (ss 96–100). TPC v Sony (1990); ACCC v Chaste (2005). Illegal since 1971, yet one of the ACCC's biggest areas of litigation. (A recommended or maximum price is not a minimum. Only minimum prices are caught.)

4 · Boycotts (s 45; ss 45D, 45DA, 45DB, 45DD)

A boycott is action that prevents (or is intended to prevent) someone buying or selling in a market.

  • Primary boycott (s 45): A + B agree that they themselves will refuse to deal with target C (competitor, supplier or customer). TPC v JW Bryant (1978). ACCC v Australian Medical Association (WA) (2001): the AMA collectively negotiated common fees for independent doctors at Joondalup Health Campus and threatened that the doctors would withdraw services and discharge patients. Held: price fixing + primary boycott (s 45 TPA); $285,000 in penalties and costs.
  • Secondary boycott (ss 45D, 45DA): A + B pressure C (with whom they have no dispute) so that C stops dealing with target D. Harder to prove. ACCC v CFMEU (2017–18).

Memory line: Primary = A + B → refuse C. Secondary = A + B → pressure C → C drops D.

5 · Exclusive dealing (s 47) — conditional

A business restricts another's freedom to choose with whom, how much, or where they buy or resell. Prohibited only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market.

  • Full-line forcing: refusing to supply unless the buyer agrees not to buy from a competitor, not to resupply a competitor's products, or not to resupply to a particular place. TPC v Massey (1983).
  • Third-line forcing (s 47(6)): supply is conditional on the customer also buying a third party's product. ACCC v Black & White Cabs [2010]; the travel insurance / Cannon Investments example. Elements: (1) there's one product the purchaser wants, but another is forced on them; (2) the forced product is a third party's; (3) the purchaser can't get what they want without also taking it; (4) it SLCs in the relevant market.

6 · Mergers & acquisitions (s 50) — conditional

A merger combines two organisations into one; an acquisition buys another's shares or assets. These have benefits (economies of scale, spreading risk, replacing poor performers), but s 50 prohibits those with the effect or likely effect of SLC in a substantial market for goods or services. The court considers the s 50(3) factors. In practice, smart businesses go to the ACCC first to show that the public benefits outweigh the detriment and that there is no SLC.

7 · Agreements that lessen competition (s 45(1)(a)) — conditional

The catch-all: a business can't make a deal with competitor(s) that is intended or likely to substantially lessen competition (ACCC v Visy). As with price fixing, a formal agreement isn't needed — an "arrangement or understanding" is enough.

8 · Penalties & remedies

  • Fine (s 76) · injunction (s 80) · damages (s 82) · compensation orders (s 79B) · probation / community service · corrective advertising (s 86C(2)(d)) · adverse publicity (s 86D) · other orders incl. specific performance, rescission, variation (s 87) · enforceable undertakings to the ACCC / declaration (s 87B).
  • Fine factors: nature and extent of the contravention; the loss or damage caused; the circumstances; any previous similar conduct.
  • s 76 maximum (corporation) = the greatest of: $50 million; 3× the benefit obtained (if it can be determined); or, if not, 30% of annual turnover. Non-corporation: $2.5 million.

3 · ILAC scripts (per provision)

Universal Law opener — write it once, at the start of the first issue

Australia's competition laws are primarily contained in Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) ("CCA"), which is enforced by the ACCC. The objective of Part IV is to prohibit trade practices that have the effect of substantially lessening competition, in order to promote competition in Australian industry. Some conduct is so obviously anti-competitive that it is prohibited outright (per se), including cartel conduct, misuse of market power and resale price maintenance. Other conduct is prohibited only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market. The relevant market is "the area of close competition between firms … the field of rivalry between them" (Re Queensland Co-operative Milling Association (1976)).
ISSUE — CARTEL / PRICE FIXING
Have [A] and [B] engaged in cartel conduct by fixing prices in contravention of the CCA?
LAW
A cartel provision is defined in s 45AD as a provision of a contract, arrangement or understanding between two or more businesses that are, or would otherwise be, in competition with each other, which fixes prices, restricts the quantity of goods, rigs bids or tenders, or allocates customers, suppliers or territories (TPC v TNT (1995); ACCC v Visy (2007)). Cartel conduct is a per se contravention, illegal regardless of its actual effect on competition (ss 45AJ, 45AK), and it is also a criminal offence (ss 45AF, 45AG). In CDPP v Nippon Yusen Kabushiki Kaisha [2017], the first successful criminal cartel prosecution, NYK was party to provisions fixing freight rates, rigging bids and allocating customers for shipping cars to Australia, and was fined $25 million.
Price fixing is established where: (1) the business has a contract, arrangement or understanding with one or more competitors; and (2) it contains a provision with the purpose or likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices (s 45AD; ACCC v Alice (1997)). A formal agreement is unnecessary; an arrangement or understanding is sufficient, and the competitors need not agree on a particular price. An agreed formula, exchanging price data or matching discounts may suffice. However, parallel conduct such as price leadership, where one business sets a price and competitors independently follow, is not necessarily collusion (TPC v Email (1980)).
APPLICATION
Competitors: [A] and [B] both [supply X in area Y], so they compete in the same field of rivalry (Re QCMA).
Arrangement or understanding: Although there was no written contract, [the handshake / "we'll both…" / the emails] shows a meeting of minds, which is sufficient. Against: [B] may argue it simply followed [A]'s price, and that, as in TPC v Email, parallel conduct is not collusion. However, unlike mere price leadership, here there was [direct communication before the change].
Purpose/effect of fixing prices: The provision that both would [charge $X / raise by Y% / not discount] has the purpose of fixing or maintaining prices. (Allocation:) The agreement that [A] would not approach [B]'s customers [in area] allocates customers/territories, which is also a cartel provision.
Per se: Because cartel conduct is per se, it is irrelevant whether competition was actually lessened, and [A/B]'s argument that [the market is large / prices were still reasonable] does not assist. There is no indication that ACCC authorisation was sought.
CONCLUSION
Therefore, [A] and [B] have likely engaged in cartel conduct in contravention of ss 45AJ and 45AK, and may also face criminal prosecution under ss 45AF and 45AG.
ISSUE — MISUSE OF MARKET POWER
Has [A] misused its market power in contravention of s 46 of the CCA?
LAW
Section 46 applies only to a business with a substantial degree of power in a market. Such a business must not take advantage of that power to eliminate or substantially damage a competitor, or to prevent a competitor's entry into that or any other market (s 46; ACCC v Ticketek [2011]). Following the 2017 amendments, s 46 targets anti-competitive conduct by firms with substantial market power "while allowing legitimate pro-competitive behaviour even if this results in harm to inefficient competitors". It is prohibited per se and cannot be authorised by the ACCC. A common example is predatory pricing, where a firm prices below cost to drive out a competitor and later raises prices. In Boral Besser Masonry v ACCC (2003), BBM cut its prices below cost to drive C&M Bricks out of the market and deter new entrants, but the High Court held there was no misuse, because BBM did not have substantial market power and could not "take advantage" of power when it had no intention of recouping its losses.
APPLICATION
Market and power: The relevant market is [product + area]. [A] holds [70%] of it, [faces few rivals / has high barriers to entry], so unlike BBM in Boral, it likely has substantial market power. (Narrow v wide market:) The ACCC would argue for a narrow market of […]; [A] would argue for a wider one of […].
Taking advantage / purpose: [A] cut prices below cost only in [the town where the new entrant opened], which suggests a purpose of eliminating [rival]. It could also recoup its losses by [raising prices once the rival leaves], which distinguishes [it] from Boral. Against: [A] may say it was legitimate competition, which s 46 permits even if inefficient rivals are harmed.
CONCLUSION
On balance, [A] has likely misused its market power in breach of s 46, and this cannot be authorised by the ACCC.
ISSUE — RESALE PRICE MAINTENANCE
Has [supplier] engaged in resale price maintenance in contravention of s 48 of the CCA?
LAW
Section 48 prohibits resale price maintenance, which occurs where a supplier dictates the price at which a retailer resells its product. It is a per se contravention. Under ss 96–100, a business engages in resale price maintenance if it: imposes a minimum price on resellers of its product; sets a price retailers are likely to understand is a minimum price; agrees with retailers that they will not advertise its product below a specified price; induces a retailer not to discount its product; or threatens to refuse supply to a retailer to force it to comply with any of these (TPC v Sony (1990); ACCC v Chaste (2005)). Although resale price maintenance has been illegal in Australia since 1971, it remains one of the ACCC's largest areas of litigation.
APPLICATION
Minimum price: [Supplier] told retailers they "must not sell below $[X]", which imposes a minimum resale price. Advertising: the ban on advertising below $[X] is a separate RPM act. Threat / refusal: by stating that discounters would have supply "reviewed", and then cutting off [retailer] after it discounted, [supplier] threatened and then refused supply to force compliance, as in TPC v Sony. Against: [Supplier] may argue the price was only recommended, but ["must not" / the cut-off] shows that retailers would understand it as a minimum.
Per se: It is irrelevant whether [supplier] had a commercial reason, such as protecting its brand image, or whether competition actually lessened, unless the ACCC authorised the conduct, which it did not.
CONCLUSION
Therefore, [supplier] has likely engaged in resale price maintenance in breach of s 48.
ISSUE — BOYCOTT (primary / secondary)
Have [A and B] engaged in a [primary/secondary] boycott in contravention of the CCA?
LAW
A boycott is an action by an individual or group that prevents, or is intended to prevent, another from buying or selling products in a market (ss 45D, 45DA, 45DB, 45DD). A primary boycott occurs where two or more businesses agree that they will collectively refuse to deal with a particular target competitor, supplier or customer (s 45; TPC v JW Bryant (1978)). In ACCC v Australian Medical Association (WA) (2001), the AMA negotiated common fees on behalf of independent doctors at Joondalup Health Campus and threatened that the doctors would collectively withdraw their services. The Full Federal Court held this was price fixing and a primary boycott, and penalties and costs of $285,000 were ordered. A secondary boycott is an indirect boycott, where two or more businesses put pressure on another business, with whom they have no dispute, to discourage it from dealing with the target (ss 45D, 45DA; ACCC v CFMEU (2017–18)). It is harder to prove and is prohibited where it substantially lessens competition.
APPLICATION
[A and B] [themselves agreed to stop dealing with C] → primary boycott; similar to AMA (WA), where the threat of collective withdrawal was enough… OR [A and B] pressured [C], with whom they had no dispute, to stop dealing with [D] → secondary boycott…
CONCLUSION
Therefore, [A and B] have likely engaged in a [primary/secondary] boycott.
ISSUE — EXCLUSIVE DEALING (full-line / third-line forcing)
Has [A] engaged in exclusive dealing in contravention of s 47 of the CCA?
LAW
Exclusive dealing occurs when a business imposes restrictions on another person's freedom to choose with whom, in what amount, or where they buy products. Under s 47 it is prohibited only if it has the effect of substantially lessening competition in the relevant market. Full-line forcing occurs where a business refuses to supply unless the buyer agrees not to buy from a competitor, not to resupply a competitor's products, or not to resupply to a particular place (TPC v Massey (1983)). Third-line forcing occurs where a business makes the supply of its product conditional on the customer also buying the product of another business (s 47(6); ACCC v Black & White Cabs [2010]). It requires that: (1) there is one product the purchaser wants, but another is forced on them; (2) the forced product is that of a third party; (3) the purchaser cannot obtain the wanted product without also obtaining the third party's product; and (4) the conduct substantially lessens competition in the relevant market. To determine whether competition is substantially lessened, it is necessary to define the market, ask whether competition was or is likely to be lessened, ask whether that lessening is substantial, and, if not, ask whether that was the purpose.
APPLICATION
The product [C] wants is […]; [A] will not supply it unless [C] also buys [third party T]'s [insurance/service] → third-line forcing elements (1)–(3) are met. SLC: the relevant market is […]; because [A] supplies [most] of it, [T]'s rivals are shut out of [x%] of customers, which is a real, weighty lessening of competition. Against: if the market is defined widely as […], the effect is insubstantial…
CONCLUSION
On balance, [A]'s conduct [is / is not] likely to contravene s 47.
ISSUE — MERGER / ACQUISITION
Would [A]'s acquisition of [B] contravene s 50 of the CCA?
LAW
A merger occurs when two or more organisations combine to form one, and an acquisition when an organisation acquires the ownership or assets of another. These may provide benefits such as economies of scale, risk-spreading and the replacement of poorly performing organisations. However, s 50 prohibits mergers and acquisitions that would have the effect or likely effect of substantially lessening competition in a substantial market for goods or services, and the court will consider the factors in s 50(3). The parties may apply to the ACCC for authorisation by showing that the public benefits outweigh the detriments.
APPLICATION
The market is […], which is a substantial market because […]. After the acquisition, [A] would hold [x%], leaving [only one rival]… For A: efficiencies […]; against: [higher prices, less choice]…
CONCLUSION
The acquisition is [likely / unlikely] to contravene s 50 unless authorised by the ACCC.
ISSUE — AGREEMENT THAT SUBSTANTIALLY LESSENS COMPETITION
Have [A and B] made an agreement that substantially lessens competition, in breach of s 45 of the CCA?
LAW
Under s 45(1)(a), a business cannot make a contract, arrangement or understanding with one or more competitors that has the purpose, effect or likely effect of substantially lessening competition in the market (ACCC v Visy). As with price fixing, a formal agreement is not required, and an arrangement or understanding is sufficient. 'Substantial' means large, weighty, real or of substance.
APPLICATION
Market → lessening → substantial? → purpose? (use the 4 steps).
PENALTIES & REMEDIES (always finish with this)
If a contravention of Part IV is established, the Federal Court may order a fine for each contravention (s 76), having regard to the nature and extent of the contravention, the loss or damage caused, the circumstances in which it occurred, and any previous similar conduct. For a corporation, the maximum fine is the greatest of $50 million; three times the value of the benefit obtained, if it can be determined; or, if not, 30% of annual turnover. For a non-corporation, the maximum is $2.5 million. The court may also grant an injunction (s 80), damages to those who suffered loss (s 82), compensation orders (s 79B), corrective advertising orders (s 86C(2)(d)), adverse publicity orders (s 86D), other orders such as rescission or variation of contracts (s 87), and enforce undertakings given to the ACCC (s 87B). (Cartel:) Criminal prosecution is also possible under ss 45AF and 45AG, as in CDPP v NYK, where a $25 million fine was imposed.

4 · Full model answer (practice question, 25 marks)

Zest Pty Ltd manufactures cold-pressed juice and supplies it to around 60 independent grocers in South-East Queensland. Its main competitor is Orchard Press Pty Ltd. Together, the two businesses supply about 80% of cold-pressed juice sold to grocers and cafés in the region.

In March, Zest emails every grocer it supplies: "From 1 April, Zest juices must not be sold or advertised below $6.50 per bottle. Stores that discount below this price will have their supply reviewed." In May, FreshMart, a small grocer, advertises Zest juice at $5.00. Zest immediately stops supplying FreshMart.

In June, at an industry dinner, Zest's CEO, Dana, tells Orchard Press's CEO, Lee: "Our margins are terrible. If we both move our wholesale price to $3.80 a bottle from 1 July, nobody loses." Lee replies, "Fine by me. And let's stop poaching each other's cafés: you keep north of the river, we'll keep south." They shake hands. Nothing is written down. On 1 July both businesses raise their wholesale price to exactly $3.80, and neither approaches the other's cafés afterwards. When contacted by the ACCC, Orchard Press says it "simply followed Zest's price, as it always does".

Advise the ACCC whether Zest and/or Orchard Press have contravened Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), and what penalties may apply.

Answer plan
  1. Opener + one Law paragraph framing Part IV, per se vs conditional, and market.
  2. Issue 1 (Zest alone) — RPM s 48: minimum price + no-advertising + threat ("reviewed") + actual refusal (FreshMart). Per se. Counter: "recommended price" / brand protection — irrelevant.
  3. Issue 2 (Zest + Orchard) — cartel: competitors? (same market, Re QCMA) → arrangement/understanding (handshake suffices) → price fixing ($3.80) AND customer/territory allocation (north/south). Counter: parallel conduct / price leadership (TPC v Email) — distinguished by the prior communication. Per se + criminal (CDPP v NYK).
  4. No authorisation sought; RPM and cartel could only have been authorised on public benefit grounds — not applicable.
  5. Penalties: s 76 max ($50m / 3× benefit / 30% turnover) + factors; injunction; damages to FreshMart (s 82); criminal ss 45AF/45AG.

Whether Zest and Orchard Press have contravened Part IV of the CCA depends upon two issues: first, whether Zest engaged in resale price maintenance in its dealings with grocers; and second, whether Zest and Orchard Press engaged in cartel conduct through their agreement at the industry dinner.

Issue 1: Resale price maintenance

Issue
Has Zest engaged in resale price maintenance in contravention of s 48 of the CCA?

Law
Australia's competition laws are primarily contained in Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) ("CCA"), which is enforced by the ACCC. The objective of Part IV is to prohibit trade practices that substantially lessen competition, in order to promote competition in Australian industry. Some conduct is so obviously anti-competitive that it is prohibited outright, regardless of its actual effect on competition. These per se offences include cartel conduct, misuse of market power and resale price maintenance. Other conduct, such as exclusive dealing and mergers, is prohibited only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market.

Section 48 prohibits resale price maintenance, which occurs where a supplier or manufacturer dictates how a retailer sells its product, for example by prohibiting discounting. Under ss 96–100, a business engages in resale price maintenance if it: (1) imposes a minimum price on resellers of its product; (2) sets a price retailers are likely to understand is a minimum price; (3) agrees with retailers that they will not advertise its product below a specified price; (4) induces a retailer not to discount its product; or (5) threatens to refuse supply to a retailer to force it to comply with any of the above. In TPC v Sony (1990) and ACCC v Chaste (2005), suppliers were held to have engaged in resale price maintenance by pressuring retailers not to sell below set prices. Although this conduct has been illegal since 1971, it remains one of the largest areas of ACCC litigation. The ACCC may authorise resale price maintenance on application, if the public benefit outweighs the public detriment.

Only cite cases the slides list. Here the slides give no facts for Sony/Chaste, so a short neutral description is safest. Don't invent detail.

Application
Minimum price. Zest's email stated that its juices "must not be sold … below $6.50 per bottle". The words "must not" leave no doubt that retailers would understand $6.50 as a mandatory minimum resale price, not a mere recommendation. This satisfies limbs (1) and (2).

Advertising. The email also stated that the juices must not be "advertised" below $6.50. This is a separate form of resale price maintenance under limb (3).

Threat and refusal to supply. The statement that discounting stores "will have their supply reviewed" is a threat to refuse supply in order to force compliance (limb (5)). It also induces retailers not to discount (limb (4)). Zest then carried out that threat by immediately cutting off FreshMart after it advertised the juice at $5.00. As with the suppliers in TPC v Sony, the refusal of supply to a discounting retailer is clear evidence of resale price maintenance.

Possible arguments for Zest. Zest may argue that $6.50 was only a recommended price, or that it was protecting its brand's premium image. However, the mandatory wording and the cut-off of FreshMart contradict the first argument. As to the second, because resale price maintenance is a per se offence, Zest's commercial reasons, and whether competition was actually lessened, are irrelevant. There is no indication that Zest applied to the ACCC for authorisation.

Conclusion
Zest has likely engaged in resale price maintenance in contravention of s 48 of the CCA.

Issue 2: Cartel conduct

Issue
Have Zest and Orchard Press engaged in cartel conduct, by fixing prices and allocating customers, in contravention of the CCA?

Law
A cartel provision is defined in s 45AD as a provision of a contract, arrangement or understanding between two or more businesses that are, or would otherwise be, in competition with each other, which: fixes prices; controls or restricts the quantity of goods to inflate prices; rigs bids or tenders; or allocates customers, suppliers or territories (TPC v TNT (1995); ACCC v Visy (2007)). Cartel conduct is prohibited per se, meaning it is illegal regardless of its actual impact on competition (ss 45AJ, 45AK), and it is also a criminal offence (ss 45AF, 45AG). In CDPP v Nippon Yusen Kabushiki Kaisha [2017], the first successful criminal cartel prosecution, NYK was party to provisions fixing freight rates, rigging bids and allocating customers for shipping vehicles to Australia. It was fined $25 million, and Wigney J warned that anti-competitive conduct "will not be tolerated and will be dealt with harshly".

Price fixing, the most common form of cartel conduct, requires that: (1) the business has a contract, arrangement or understanding with one or more businesses with which it is in competition; and (2) it contains a provision with the purpose or likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices (s 45AD; ACCC v Alice (1997)). A formal or written agreement is not required, and an "arrangement or understanding" is sufficient. However, parallel conduct is not necessarily collusion. Price leadership, where one business sets a price and competitors subsequently set the same price, may occur without any arrangement (TPC v Email (1980)).

Whether businesses are in competition depends on the relevant market, being "the area of close competition between firms … the field of rivalry between them" (Re Queensland Co-operative Milling Association (1976)).

Application
Competitors. Zest and Orchard Press both supply cold-pressed juice to grocers and cafés in South-East Queensland. The fact that they "poach" each other's cafés shows that they are rivals in the same market, and together they supply about 80% of it. They are therefore in competition with each other.

Contract, arrangement or understanding. Nothing was written down. However, Dana proposed that both move to $3.80, Lee expressly agreed ("Fine by me"), and they shook hands. This is at least an arrangement or understanding, which is sufficient. Orchard Press will argue that, as in TPC v Email, it merely followed Zest's price as a price leader, which is legitimate parallel conduct. This argument should fail. Price leadership involves a competitor independently following another's price. Here, the identical price rise on the same day was preceded by a direct conversation in which both CEOs committed to it. The parallel price is evidence of the arrangement, not an innocent explanation for it.

Price fixing. The understanding that both would set their wholesale price at $3.80 from 1 July has the purpose, and in fact the effect, of fixing prices. Dana's comment that "nobody loses" confirms the purpose was to raise margins without losing customers to each other.

Customer and territory allocation. Lee's proposal, accepted by the handshake, that Zest "keep north of the river" and Orchard Press "keep south", and that they stop "poaching" each other's cafés, allocates customers and territories between competitors. This is a separate cartel provision under s 45AD. It was also carried out, as neither approached the other's cafés afterwards. It resembles the customer allocation in CDPP v NYK.

Per se. Because cartel conduct is a per se offence, it is unnecessary to prove that competition was substantially lessened, so arguments that margins were "terrible" or that the price was reasonable do not assist either party. In any event, with 80% of the market between them, the effect on grocers and cafés is plainly significant. Neither party sought ACCC authorisation.

Conclusion
Zest and Orchard Press have likely engaged in cartel conduct, both price fixing and customer/territory allocation, in contravention of ss 45AJ and 45AK. They are also exposed to criminal prosecution under ss 45AF and 45AG.

Penalties and remedies

The Federal Court may order each company to pay a fine for each contravention (s 76), having regard to the nature and extent of the contravention, the loss caused (for example, to FreshMart and to the grocers and cafés paying inflated prices), the circumstances (a deliberate, secret arrangement between CEOs), and any previous similar conduct. Because Zest and Orchard Press are corporations, the maximum fine for each contravention is the greatest of $50 million, three times the value of the benefit obtained, or, if that cannot be determined, 30% of annual turnover. The ACCC may also seek injunctions restraining the conduct (s 80), for example requiring Zest to resume supplying FreshMart and to withdraw its minimum-price email. Persons who suffered loss, such as FreshMart and overcharged customers, may recover damages (s 82) or obtain compensation orders (s 79B). The court may also make corrective advertising and adverse publicity orders (ss 86C(2)(d), 86D) or accept enforceable undertakings (s 87B). For the cartel conduct, the CDPP may bring criminal proceedings, as in CDPP v NYK, where a $25 million fine was imposed.

Overall: On the balance of probabilities, Zest has contravened s 48 by engaging in resale price maintenance, and Zest and Orchard Press have together engaged in cartel conduct. Both face substantial civil penalties and possible criminal liability.

5 · Traps

Don't try to prove SLC for per se conduct (cartel, s 46 per slides, RPM). Say "per se — it is irrelevant whether competition was actually lessened". DO prove SLC (with the 4 steps and market definition) for exclusive dealing, mergers, s 45 agreements and secondary boycotts.
"Nothing was in writing" is NOT a defence: an arrangement or understanding is enough.
Same price ≠ automatically price fixing (TPC v Email — price leadership). Look for communication or a meeting of minds and argue both ways.
RPM is supplier → retailer (vertical). Price fixing is competitor ↔ competitor (horizontal). Don't call a manufacturer's minimum price "price fixing".
Third-line forcing needs a third party's product. If the supplier forces its own second product, it isn't third-line forcing as defined on the slides.
The ACCC can authorise everything except misuse of market power. And authorisation requires an application, so if the facts don't mention one, say "no authorisation was sought".
Consumer-facing wrongs (misleading ads, guarantees) belong to the ACL (T6). Don't drift.
Primary vs secondary boycott: who refuses? If the boycotters refuse, it's primary. If they make someone else refuse, it's secondary.

T7

Competition law — CCA Part IV — exam sheet

Opener (Law): "Australia's competition laws are primarily contained in Part IV of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), enforced by the ACCC. Its objective is to prohibit trade practices that substantially lessen competition. Some conduct is prohibited per se (cartel conduct, misuse of market power, RPM); other conduct only if it substantially lessens competition in the relevant market — 'the area of close competition … the field of rivalry' (Re QCMA (1976))."
SLC — 4 steps: (1) define market (complainant → narrow; defendant → wide) · (2) did it / is it likely to lessen competition? · (3) substantial? (large, weighty, real, of substance) · (4) if not, was the PURPOSE to SLC?
PER SE: cartel · s 46 · RPM s 48. CONDITIONAL (SLC): secondary boycott · exclusive dealing s 47 · mergers s 50 · s 45 agreements.
ACCC authorisation (on application; public benefit > detriment): all EXCEPT misuse of market power.
CARTEL (s 45AD; civil ss 45AJ/45AK; criminal ss 45AF/45AG): contract/arrangement/understanding between competitors that (a) fixes prices, (b) restricts output, (c) rigs bids, (d) allocates customers/suppliers/territories. TPC v TNT; ACCC v Visy; CDPP v NYK [2017] (freight rates, bid rigging, customer allocation; $25m; first criminal conviction).
Price fixing: (1) CAU with competitor(s); (2) purpose/likely effect of fixing, controlling or maintaining prices (ACCC v Alice). Formula, data exchange, output cuts, same discounts all count. Informal is enough. Counter: parallel conduct / price leadership ≠ collusion (TPC v Email).
MISUSE OF MARKET POWER s 46: substantial market power → take advantage → eliminate/substantially damage competitor or prevent entry (ACCC v Ticketek [2011]). Not authorisable. Predatory pricing: Boral (C&M Bricks) — HC: no misuse; no substantial power + no intention to recoup. 2017: legit competition OK even if inefficient rivals are hurt; add the post-2017 "purpose/effect of SLC" line.
RPM s 48 (ss 96–100) — supplier → reseller: (1) minimum price · (2) price understood as a minimum · (3) no advertising below $X · (4) induce not to discount · (5) threaten/refuse supply to force it. TPC v Sony (1990); ACCC v Chaste (2005). Per se; illegal since 1971.
BOYCOTTS (ss 45D, 45DA, 45DB, 45DD): Primary (s 45) A+B refuse C — TPC v JW Bryant; ACCC v AMA (WA) (2001): collective fee negotiation + threat to withdraw doctors' services = price fixing + primary boycott; $285k. Secondary A+B pressure C → C drops D — ACCC v CFMEU (2017–18); harder to prove.
EXCLUSIVE DEALING s 47 (SLC needed): restricts with whom/how much/where. Full-line: supply only if the buyer won't buy from/resupply a competitor or resupply to a place (TPC v Massey). Third-line s 47(6): (1) wanted product + forced product; (2) the forced one is a third party's; (3) can't get one without the other; (4) SLC (ACCC v Black & White Cabs [2010]; travel insurance / Cannon Investments).
MERGERS s 50: effect/likely effect of SLC in a substantial market; s 50(3) factors; benefits (scale, risk, replacing poor performers); go to the ACCC first. s 45(1)(a): competitor deal intended/likely to SLC (ACCC v Visy); a CAU is enough.
PENALTIES: fine s 76 · injunction s 80 · damages s 82 · compensation s 79B · probation/community service · corrective ads s 86C(2)(d) · adverse publicity s 86D · rescission/variation/specific performance s 87 · undertakings/declaration s 87B. Fine factors: nature & extent; loss caused; circumstances; prior conduct. Max (corp) = greatest of $50m / 3× benefit / 30% turnover (if benefit unknown). Individual: $2.5m. Cartels: criminal too.

Answer 1 — 2024 S1 Q2: Betty (pothole, drunk driver, PTSD, painkiller addiction)

Question text — Semester One 2024, Question 2 (25 marks)

Woobalong is a residential suburb in the city of Rockhampton, which is located in Central Queensland. Over the last 5 years, parts of Central Queensland have been subjected to extensive flooding due to heavier than normal rainfall. As a result of constant localized flooding in the Rockhampton area, one section of the main road (Sefton Road) in Woobalong has suffered significant deterioration which has led to the formation of a deep pothole in the middle of the road. (A pothole is a depression or hollow in a road surface caused by wear and tear and/or adverse weather.) Furthermore, the angle and position of the pothole makes it impossible for road users to detect.

In January 2024, the Rockhampton Mayor's office (RMO) received five separate reports from Woobalong residents warning that the pothole was bad in a bad position and was a hazard. However, the complaints were dismissed by the RMO on the basis that there had been no reported road incidents or car accidents at that location on Sefton Road in the past.

One afternoon in February 2024, Betty is walking on the footpath along Sefton Road, on her way home from work. She is looking at her phone, transferring money onto her credit card and not paying much attention to her surroundings. Suddenly, Betty is hit by an out-of-control car, driven by Logan. An investigation finds that Logan lost control of the car when his tyres hit the pothole. The police also found that Logan was over the legal blood/breath alcohol limit at the time of the incident.

As a result of the accident, Betty is hospitalized with extensive injuries. After 6 months in intensive care and one year in rehabilitation, her total hospital bill amounts to $755,000. During her stay in hospital, Betty's husband suggests that she takes a higher dosage of painkillers than what is prescribed by her doctor to reduce the severe pain she is experiencing. Betty develops an addiction to these painkillers and later checks herself in to a rehabilitation clinic to help treat her addiction, at a cost of $20,000. After the accident, Betty experiences nightmares, flash backs and sleep deprivation. She is diagnosed with severe PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder) and has to undergo weekly therapy for four years, at a cost of $79,000.

Betty comes to you seeking advice and asks whether she can recover the amounts she paid for the hospital bill, rehabilitation clinic and therapy.

Advise Betty as to her most appropriate course of action under the Tort Law. Please use ILAC to answer this question.

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Issue: Can Betty bring an action against the RMO for the tort of negligence? (Logan = second defendant, short.)
  2. Duty: Donoghue neighbour principle; public authority controls road; hidden risk → Nagle, Swain (contrast Romeo, Vairy).
  3. Breach: s 9(1)(a)–(c) — five reports; "no accidents" = Bolton v Stone argument, distinguish; s 9(2): seriousness (Paris), cheap burden, Watt.
  4. Causation, each head: hospital $755k ✔ (material contribution despite Logan — Chappel, Cook); rehab $20k ✘ (husband → Yates; Schneider); PTSD $79k ✔ (Schneider).
  5. Defences: contributory negligence (phone) — weak, Manley distinguished; voluntary assumption of risk not relevant.
  6. Remedies: ordinary damages $834,000 less any reduction; also sue Logan.

Whether Betty can recover her hospital bill ($755,000), rehabilitation clinic fees ($20,000) and therapy costs ($79,000) depends on whether she can establish the tort of negligence against the Rockhampton Mayor's Office (RMO), and then whether each of these losses was caused by the RMO's breach. Her most appropriate course of action is an action in negligence against the RMO; she may also join Logan as a defendant (see Issue 2).

Issue 1

ISSUECan Betty bring an action against the RMO for the tort of negligence?

Law

A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another person. To succeed, Betty must prove on the balance of probabilities that: (1) the RMO owed her a duty of care; (2) the RMO breached that duty; and (3) the breach caused her loss (causation). The court will then consider any defences and the remedy. In Queensland the relevant statute is the Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) (CLA).

Duty of care

In Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, Mrs Donoghue suffered nervous shock and gastro-enteritis after drinking ginger beer from an opaque bottle containing a decomposed snail. The House of Lords held that the manufacturer owed her a duty of care. Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle" states that you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour, being persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation. The question is whether it was reasonably foreseeable that the defendant's conduct could harm someone in the plaintiff's position.

A duty is easily found where the relationship falls into an established category, such as occupiers, who owe a duty to people who come onto their premises (Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987)). Public authorities such as councils have control over public spaces like roads, parks and waterways and can be occupiers. Whether they owe a duty depends on whether there is a hidden risk requiring a warning or prevention, or whether the risk is so obvious to a reasonable person that no duty exists. In Nagle v Rottnest Island Authority (1993), a submerged rock in an area signed as a "swimming area" was a hidden risk, and the authority owed a duty. In Swain v Waverley (2005), a submerged sandbar at a patrolled beach was a hidden risk and there was a duty to warn. By contrast, in Romeo v Conservation Commission (1998) (unfenced cliff top) and Vairy v Wyong Shire Council (2005) (natural rock platform at an unpatrolled beach with no signs), the risks were obvious and the authorities were not liable.

Breach of duty

Under s 9(1) CLA, a defendant breaches its duty by failing to take precautions against a risk of harm if: (a) the risk was foreseeable (one the defendant knew or ought reasonably to have known about); (b) the risk was not insignificant; and (c) in the circumstances, a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have taken those precautions. Under s 9(2), in deciding what a reasonable person would have done, the court considers: (a) the probability of harm; (b) the likely seriousness of the harm; (c) the burden of taking precautions; and (d) the social utility of the activity. In Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket ball hit out of the ground struck a passer-by; because balls had only very rarely cleared the ground, the probability of harm was so low that the club was not liable. In Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951), a welder with sight in only one eye was not given goggles and lost the sight of his other eye; the seriousness of the harm meant precautions should have been taken. In Watt v Hertfordshire (1954), a fireman injured by unsecured equipment on the way to an emergency failed, because the social utility of saving life justified the risk.

Causation

Section 11(1) CLA sets out two tests, which must be applied to each loss. (a) Factual causation: the breach must be a necessary condition of the harm — "but for" the defendant's carelessness, would the plaintiff have suffered the harm? Where the harm has several causes, the court applies the material contribution test: the defendant is liable if its conduct increased the risk of injury and that risk happened, even if other factors also contributed (Chappel v Hart (1998), McHugh J). In Cook v ACT Racing Club Inc [2001], an overheated sauna was only one cause of a jockey's heart attack, but it significantly increased the risk, so the club was liable. (b) Scope of liability: it must be appropriate for liability to extend to the harm, which requires the type of harm to be reasonably foreseeable and not too remote (Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]). In Yates v Jones [1990], a car accident victim became addicted to heroin after a friend suggested it to ease her pain; the addiction was held to be caused by the friend, not the accident. In Schneider v Smith (2016), the plaintiff's use of illegal drugs to cope with pain was a deliberate, voluntary act that was not part of prescribed or recommended treatment, so causation failed for it; but damages for medical costs and psychological treatment were awarded as ordinary and natural consequences of the accident.

Defences

If the plaintiff contributed to her own injury, liability is apportioned between the parties (contributory negligence): Ingram v Britten (plaintiff driving a tractor at speed, defendant had not secured its frame); Manley v Alexander (plaintiff drank 12 stubbies and slept in the middle of the road; defendant negligently drove over him).

Application

Duty of care

The RMO is a public authority with control over Sefton Road, a main road in Woobalong. Assumption: the facts do not say who maintains the road, so I assume the RMO (on behalf of the council) is responsible for it. The pothole's angle and position made it "impossible for road users to detect". Similar to the submerged rock in Nagle and the sandbar in Swain, this is a hidden risk, not an obvious one like the cliff edge in Romeo or the rock platform in Vairy. The RMO may argue that the pothole endangers drivers, not pedestrians on the footpath. However, applying the neighbour principle in Donoghue, it is reasonably foreseeable that a car losing control on a deep pothole on a main road will leave the road and strike people walking on the adjoining footpath. Betty is therefore a person closely and directly affected, and the RMO owed her a duty of care.

Breach of duty

s 9(1)(a) — foreseeable: The risk was not only foreseeable but actually known. In January 2024 the RMO received five separate reports warning that the pothole was in a bad position and was a hazard.

s 9(1)(b) — not insignificant: The RMO dismissed the complaints because there had been no reported incidents. However, a deep, undetectable pothole in the middle of a main road, getting worse through five years of flooding, is a real risk, as five separate residents recognised.

s 9(1)(c) and s 9(2) — reasonable person: As to probability, the RMO will rely on Bolton v Stone, arguing that, with no history of accidents, the chance of harm was remote. This can be distinguished: in Bolton the low risk was proven by many years of play, whereas here the hazard is recent and growing, it is on a main road used every day, and five warnings arrived within one month. As to seriousness, a car losing control can cause death or catastrophic injury; like the loss of sight in Paris, such serious possible harm demands precautions. As to the burden, warning signs, a lower speed limit, a barrier or filling the pothole would have been relatively quick and inexpensive. The RMO may argue that flood damage across the region stretches its resources, but no facts suggest it could not afford to deal with one reported hazard. As to social utility, maintaining roads is useful, but unlike the emergency in Watt, nothing justified leaving the hazard in place. A reasonable authority would have taken precautions, so the RMO breached its duty.

Causation — applied to each loss

(i) Hospital bill — $755,000. Factual causation: The investigation found that Logan lost control "when his tyres hit the pothole". The RMO will argue that the real cause was Logan's drink-driving. Because there are several causes, the material contribution test applies. Similar to Cook, where the sauna was only one cause of the heart attack, the pothole need not be the only cause of the accident; it is enough that the RMO's failure to repair it increased the risk of exactly this kind of accident and that risk happened (Chappel v Hart). Scope of liability: Physical injury to a person near the road from an out-of-control car is the very type of harm residents warned of, so it is reasonably foreseeable and not remote (Overseas Tankships). Both tests are satisfied and the $755,000 is recoverable.

(ii) Rehabilitation clinic — $20,000. Factual causation: Betty will argue that, but for the accident, she would not have been in severe pain or taking painkillers at all. However, her addiction arose because her husband suggested she take a higher dose than her doctor prescribed. Similar to Yates v Jones, where the friend's suggestion of heroin, not the car accident, was held to cause the addiction, the husband's suggestion is an intervening act that breaks the chain of causation. Scope of liability: As in Schneider, taking medication beyond the prescription was a voluntary act that was not part of prescribed or recommended treatment, and the law expects individual responsibility. Betty may argue she was in severe pain and vulnerable in hospital, but on balance the addiction is outside the scope of the RMO's liability, and the $20,000 is unlikely to be recoverable.

(iii) PTSD therapy — $79,000. Factual causation: Betty's nightmares, flashbacks and sleep deprivation began after the accident and no other cause is suggested, so but for the accident she would not have developed PTSD. Scope of liability: Psychiatric injury (nervous shock) is a recognised type of injury, as in Donoghue itself. Similar to Schneider, where psychological treatment was compensated as an ordinary and natural consequence of the accident, PTSD after being struck by a car is reasonably foreseeable. The RMO may dispute whether four years of weekly therapy is reasonable, but that goes to the amount, not liability. The $79,000 is recoverable.

Defences

The RMO will argue contributory negligence because Betty was looking at her phone, transferring money and "not paying much attention to her surroundings". However, unlike the plaintiff in Manley v Alexander, who lay drunk in the middle of the road, Betty was walking on the footpath where pedestrians are entitled to be, and the car was "out-of-control" and hit her "suddenly". Even an attentive pedestrian is unlikely to have avoided it. Any reduction for contributory negligence is therefore likely to be small, if any. Voluntary assumption of risk is not relevant, as Betty had no knowledge of the pothole and did not accept any risk.

Feedback: only discuss defences that the facts raise. One line dismissing voluntary assumption of risk is enough.

Conclusion

CONCLUSIONOn the balance of probabilities, Betty can bring an action in negligence against the RMO and recover her hospital bill and PTSD therapy costs, but probably not the rehabilitation clinic fees.

Issue 2 — Logan (alternative defendant)

Short on time? This Logan issue is optional (~250 words). The core answer without it is ~1,900 words; cut the Romeo/Vairy contrast and Watt first if you need to save more.

ISSUECan Betty also bring an action against Logan for the tort of negligence?

LAWMotorists owe a duty of care to other road users, an established category; the same s 9 and s 11 tests apply.

APPLICATIONLogan, as a motorist, owed Betty, a pedestrian beside the road, a duty of care. Driving over the legal alcohol limit creates a foreseeable and not insignificant risk; under s 9(2) the probability and seriousness of harm are high and the burden of not drinking is nil, so a reasonable driver would not have driven. On causation, Logan will argue that the pothole was undetectable and that a sober driver would also have lost control. The facts do not say whether his intoxication affected his control, so I assume that it at least reduced his ability to react; on that basis it materially contributed to the accident (Chappel v Hart). The same analysis of each loss applies as above.

CONCLUSIONBetty should sue both the RMO and Logan, so that she can recover even if one of them is found not liable.

Remedies

The usual remedy is ordinary damages, which aim to restore Betty, so far as money can, to the position she would have been in had the tort not occurred. They are assessed "once and for all", so she should also claim any future losses now, such as lost wages (the facts are silent on whether she lost income). Betty can claim $755,000 + $79,000 = $834,000, reduced by any contributory negligence. Exemplary damages are unlikely, as the RMO was careless rather than deliberately and maliciously disregarding her rights.

Answer 2 — 2023 S2 Q2: Margaret (pothole, careful driver, prescribed opioids)

Question text — Semester Two 2023, Question 2 (25 marks)

Sherwood is a suburb in Brisbane managed and maintained by the Brisbane City Council (BCC). Due to wear and tear and environmental conditions over several years, Freshman Street in Sherwood begins to deteriorate, eventually creating a pothole (a pothole is a depression or hollow in a road surface caused by wear or substance). The angle and positioning of the pothole makes it not easily visible to road users.

In 2023, the BCC received three separate reports from Sherwood residents warning that the pothole was a driving risk, with the potential to cause serious road accidents. However, the BCC dismissed these complaints, believing that the chance of injury was insignificant because no car accidents had ever occurred on or near that street.

One morning, Margaret is walking down Freshman Street, on her way to the bus stop. She is hit by an out-of-control car and is hospitalised. An investigation finds that the car lost control due to the driver running over the pothole. It was also determined that the driver was not speeding or driving recklessly at the time.

As a result of the accident, Margaret pays $15,000 in medical fees to treat her broken arm and dislocated shoulder. Margaret also undergoes 3 years' worth of ongoing treatment for severe back pain that costs $65,000. As a result of the back pain, Margaret's doctor prescribes her opioids, which is a class of drug medication used to reduce severe pain. Over time, however, Margaret develops an addiction to these opioids and, as a result of the addiction, gradually starts ingesting them beyond the prescribed dose. She then pays $10,500 to a rehabilitation clinic to treat her addiction.

Advise Margaret as to the most appropriate action she can bring under Tort Law and as to any damages (if any) she is able to claim. Please use ILAC to answer this question.

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Issue: Can Margaret bring an action against the BCC for the tort of negligence? (Driver not negligent — one line.)
  2. Duty: neighbour principle; BCC "manages and maintains" street; pothole not easily visible = hidden risk (Nagle, Swain v Romeo, Vairy).
  3. Breach: three reports; BCC said "insignificant" → rebut s 9(1)(b); Bolton distinguished; Paris; low burden.
  4. Causation: $15k ✔; $65k ✔; $10.5k rehab — prescribed opioids, distinguish Yates & Schneider → probably ✔.
  5. Defences: no facts of contributory negligence (state assumption); VAR irrelevant.
  6. Remedies: ordinary damages $90,500.

Issue

ISSUECan Margaret bring an action against the BCC for the tort of negligence?

Law

A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another. Margaret must prove on the balance of probabilities that (1) the BCC owed her a duty of care; (2) the BCC breached that duty; and (3) the breach caused her loss; any defences and remedies are then considered. The Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) (CLA) applies.

Duty of care

Under Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, a person must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which they can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure persons so closely and directly affected that they ought to have them in contemplation. In that case, a manufacturer owed a duty to the consumer of a ginger beer containing a decomposed snail which could not be seen through the bottle. Occupiers owe a duty to people who come onto their premises (Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987)). Public authorities that control public spaces such as roads can be occupiers, and they will owe a duty where there is a hidden risk that requires a warning or prevention, rather than a risk that is obvious to a reasonable person. In Nagle v Rottnest Island Authority (1993) a submerged rock in a signed swimming area, and in Swain v Waverley (2005) a submerged sandbar at a patrolled beach, were hidden risks giving rise to a duty. In Romeo v Conservation Commission (1998) and Vairy v Wyong Shire Council (2005), the risks (a cliff edge; diving from a natural rock platform) were obvious and the authorities were not liable.

Breach of duty

Under s 9(1) CLA, the BCC breached its duty if (a) the risk was foreseeable, (b) the risk was not insignificant, and (c) a reasonable person in its position would have taken precautions. Under s 9(2), the court considers (a) the probability of harm, (b) its likely seriousness, (c) the burden of taking precautions, and (d) the social utility of the activity. In Bolton v Stone (1951) a cricket club was not liable when a ball left the ground and hit a passer-by, because this had happened so rarely that the probability of harm was very low. In Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951), the serious consequences for a one-eyed welder meant goggles should have been provided.

Causation

Under s 11(1) CLA, (a) the breach must be a necessary condition of the harm ("but for" test — factual causation), and (b) it must be appropriate for the defendant's liability to extend to the harm (scope of liability): the type of harm must be reasonably foreseeable and not too remote (Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]). Both tests must be applied to each loss. In Yates v Jones [1990], a heroin addiction suggested by a friend was caused by the friend, not the car accident. In Schneider v Smith (2016), illegal drugs taken to cope with pain were a deliberate, voluntary act that was not part of prescribed or recommended treatment, so causation failed; but medical and psychological treatment costs were recoverable as ordinary and natural consequences of the accident.

Defences

If the plaintiff contributed to her own harm, liability is apportioned (contributory negligence): Ingram v Britten; Manley v Alexander.

Application

Duty of care

The pothole's angle and position made it "not easily visible to road users". Similar to the submerged rock in Nagle and the sandbar in Swain, it was a hidden risk, unlike the obvious cliff in Romeo. Applying Donoghue, it is reasonably foreseeable that a car hitting a hidden pothole could lose control and strike a pedestrian walking along that street, so Margaret was the BCC's neighbour and it owed her a duty of care.

Breach of duty

s 9(1)(a): The risk was foreseeable because the BCC actually knew of it: three separate residents reported that the pothole was a driving risk "with the potential to cause serious road accidents".

That case can be distinguished: in Bolton the risk had been tested over many years of matches, whereas this pothole developed recently and three residents independently warned of it within one year. On seriousness, residents specifically warned of "serious road accidents"; like Paris, the gravity of possible harm called for precautions. On burden, repairing the pothole, or at least placing warning signs or cones, was relatively quick and inexpensive. On social utility, maintaining roads is useful, but there was no reason to leave a known hazard in place. A reasonable council would have taken precautions, so the BCC breached its duty.

Causation — applied to each loss

Factual: The investigation found the car lost control "due to the driver running over the pothole", and the driver was not speeding or reckless, so there is no other cause. But for the BCC's failure to repair or warn of the pothole, Margaret would not have been hit. Scope: Physical injury to a pedestrian from an out-of-control car is precisely the type of harm residents warned of, so it is reasonably foreseeable (Overseas Tankships). Recoverable.

(ii) Three years' treatment for severe back pain — $65,000. Factual: The back pain is stated to be "a result of the accident"; but for the breach, Margaret would not have needed this treatment. Scope: Similar to Schneider, where medical costs were awarded as ordinary and natural consequences of the accident, ongoing treatment of an injury suffered when hit by a car is reasonably foreseeable. The BCC may question whether three years of treatment was reasonable, but that goes to the amount. Recoverable.

(iii) Rehabilitation clinic for opioid addiction — $10,500. Factual: But for the accident, Margaret would not have had back pain and her doctor would not have prescribed opioids. Scope: The BCC will argue that Margaret broke the chain of causation by taking the opioids "beyond the prescribed dose", relying on Schneider, where the plaintiff's deliberate use of illegal drugs was not recoverable. However, this case can be distinguished from Schneider: Margaret's opioids were prescribed by her doctor as part of her treatment, and she began exceeding the dose only "as a result of the addiction", which itself developed from the prescribed use. It is also unlike Yates v Jones, because no third person suggested she take the drugs. Addiction is a reasonably foreseeable consequence of a strong prescribed painkiller, so on balance the addiction is within the scope of the BCC's liability and the $10,500 is likely to be recoverable.

Law

A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another person. Matt must prove on the balance of probabilities that (1) Harry owed him a duty of care; (2) Harry breached it; and (3) the breach caused his loss. Defences and remedies are then considered. A duty is readily found where the relationship falls into an established category; motorists owe a duty of care to other road users.

Breach of duty

Under s 9(1) CLA, a defendant breaches its duty if (a) the risk was foreseeable, (b) it was not insignificant, and (c) a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have taken precautions. Under s 9(2), the court considers the probability of harm, its likely seriousness, the burden of taking precautions and the social utility of the activity. In Bolton v Stone (1951) a cricket club was not liable because the chance of a ball leaving the ground and hitting someone was very small. Causation

(b) Scope of liability: the type of harm must be reasonably foreseeable and not too remote (Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961]). An intervening act can break the chain of causation: in Yates v Jones [1990], an accident victim's heroin addiction was caused by the friend who suggested it, not the driver. Where the plaintiff contributed to his own injury, liability is apportioned between the parties (contributory negligence). In Ingram v Britten the plaintiff was driving a tractor at speed, although the defendant had failed to secure its frame; in Manley v Alexander the plaintiff drank 12 stubbies and slept in the road before the defendant negligently drove over him.

An employer may be liable for the acts of its employees committed in the course of their employment (Deatons v Flew (1949)).

He can claim $450,000 (plus any costs of re-listing the property, if proven), and, if the court accepts the disorder was within the scope of liability, part of his treatment costs, all reduced for his contributory negligence. Answer 5 — 2021 S2 Q1: Tom & Bill (hidden sandbar between the flags; wages; son's psychiatric harm)

Question text — Semester Two 2021, Question 1 (15 marks in 2021 — write it at 25-mark length now)

Over the Christmas holidays, Tom decides to take his young son Bill (who is 10 years old) to a beach. The beach is located in the Caloundra Shire Council.

As Tom and Bill walk onto the beach, Tom sees a surf life-saving tower and a sign that says: "Safe water conditions. Please remember to swim between the flags."

It is a hot, sunny day and Tom decides to go for a swim. Tom takes Bill down to the ocean and enters the water between the flags and in front of the surf life-saving tower. Tom holds Bill's hand and walks out about 5 metres into the water. As a large wave approaches, Tom says to Bill, "Watch this Billy, I'll show you how to not get knocked over by the waves! If you dive into the wave, it is really easy. Stay here and watch a pro!" Bill watches on as his father dives into the wave.

Unfortunately, submerged below the water, and not visible from the water surface, is a large sand bar that has caused a number of other swimmers minor injuries over the previous months. As Tom dives into the wave, his head hits the sand bar and sustains very serious neck and spinal injuries.

Tom, who is a lawyer in a top law firm in Brisbane, is hospitalized for 3 months and is unable to work for 12 months. He incurs $655,000 in medical costs and lost wages. His son, Bill, is also diagnosed with an acute stress and anxiety disorder as a result of having witnessed the incident. Bill is prescribed medication as a result.

Tom comes to you seeking advice on whether he can claim his medical costs, loss of wages and his son's medical condition under the tort of negligence. He also asks you who he should bring the claim against and whether there are any defences that could be raised against him. You must use the ILAC Template on Blackboard to answer this question.

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Who to sue + Issues: Caloundra Shire Council. Issue 1: Can Tom bring an action against the Council for negligence? Issue 2: Can Bill (through Tom) do so for his disorder?
  2. Duty: public authority, hidden risk — Swain v Waverley (almost identical) + Nagle ("Safe water" sign); distinguish Vairy, Romeo.
  3. Breach: prior injuries over months → s 9(1)(a)–(b); s 9(2): not Bolton; Paris seriousness; cheap sign / move flags.
  4. Causation: Tom's medical + wages $655k ✔; Bill's disorder — but-for ✔, scope argued (neighbour, witness, recognised injury) → probably ✔.
  5. Defences (asked for): obvious risk / VAR ss 13–15, 17–19, Agar v Hyde → fails (hidden risk); contributory negligence ("watch a pro") → possible reduction.
  6. Remedies: ordinary damages $655,000 (less any reduction) + Bill's treatment costs.

Tom's claim should be brought against the Caloundra Shire Council (the Council), as the public authority in control of the beach. Assumption: the facts do not say who erected the sign or operated the patrol; I assume it was the Council. If a surf life-saving organisation was responsible, it could be joined as a further defendant on the same reasoning. Two issues arise: Tom's own claim, and the claim for Bill's condition, which Bill (as a child) would bring through Tom as his parent.

Issue 1

ISSUECan Tom bring an action against the Council for the tort of negligence?

Law

A person commits the tort of negligence if they carelessly cause harm to another person. Tom must prove (1) a duty of care, (2) breach and (3) causation, after which defences and remedies are considered. The Civil Liability Act 2003 (Qld) (CLA) applies.

Duty of care

Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 requires reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that could reasonably be foreseen to injure persons closely and directly affected; in that case a manufacturer owed a duty to a consumer who drank ginger beer containing a hidden decomposed snail. Occupiers owe a duty to those who enter their premises (Australian Safeway Stores Pty Ltd v Zaluzna (1987)). Public authorities with control over places such as beaches and waterways can be occupiers, and they owe a duty where there is a hidden risk that requires a warning or prevention, rather than an obvious risk. In Swain v Waverley (2005), a surfer dived into a wave at a patrolled beach between the flags and hit a submerged sandbar; this was a hidden risk and there was a duty to warn. In Nagle v Rottnest Island Authority (1993), a sign saying "Swimming area" encouraged people to swim over a submerged rock, a hidden risk. By contrast, in Vairy v Wyong Shire Council (2005), the plaintiff dived from a natural rock platform at an unpatrolled beach with no signs, and in Romeo v Conservation Commission (1998) the plaintiff fell from an unfenced cliff top; those risks were obvious and the authorities were not liable.

Breach of duty

Under s 9(1) CLA, the defendant breaches its duty if (a) the risk was foreseeable, (b) it was not insignificant, and (c) a reasonable person in its position would have taken precautions; s 9(2) requires consideration of the probability of harm, its likely seriousness, the burden of precautions and the social utility of the activity. In Bolton v Stone (1951), a very low probability of a cricket ball hitting someone meant no breach. In Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951), the seriousness of possible blindness meant goggles were required.

Causation

Under s 11(1) CLA, (a) "but for" the breach, would the harm have occurred (factual causation)? and (b) is it appropriate for liability to extend to the harm, being a reasonably foreseeable type of harm that is not too remote (scope of liability; Overseas Tankships v Morts [1961])? Both tests apply to each loss.

Defences

Voluntary assumption of risk requires that the plaintiff had full knowledge and appreciation of the precise risk and freely accepted it; it is very difficult to establish. In Agar v Hyde (2000), rugby players injured in a collapsed scrum had voluntarily assumed the risks inherent in rugby. Under ss 13–14 CLA, a plaintiff injured by an obvious risk is presumed to have been aware of it; under s 15 there is no duty to warn of an obvious risk; and under ss 17–19 a defendant is not liable for harm from the obvious risk of a dangerous recreational activity. Contributory negligence: if the plaintiff contributed to his own injury, liability is apportioned (Ingram v Britten; Manley v Alexander).

Application

Duty of care

The beach is within the Council's area and was patrolled, flagged and signed, so the Council controlled it as an occupier. The sandbar was "submerged below the water, and not visible from the water surface". The facts are almost identical to Swain v Waverley: Tom, like the surfer in Swain, dived into a wave between the flags at a patrolled beach and hit a hidden sandbar. Further, similar to the "Swimming area" sign in Nagle, the Council's sign — "Safe water conditions. Please remember to swim between the flags" — actively encouraged Tom to swim there. Unlike Vairy, the beach was patrolled and signed, and unlike Romeo, the danger was not obvious. The Council owed Tom a duty of care.

Breach of duty

s 9(1)(a)–(b): The sandbar had "caused a number of other swimmers minor injuries over the previous months", so the risk was known or ought to have been known, and was not insignificant. s 9(1)(c) and s 9(2): The probability of harm was real; unlike Bolton v Stone, injuries had already happened repeatedly. The likely seriousness was very high, since anyone diving onto a sandbar risks neck and spinal injury; similar to Paris, this called for precautions. The burden was low: moving the flags, adding a "submerged sandbar — do not dive" sign or warning swimmers from the tower. A patrolled beach has high social utility, but that does not justify telling the public that conditions were "safe". The Council breached its duty.

Causation — Tom's medical costs and lost wages ($655,000)

Factual: But for the Council's failure to warn of the sandbar, or to place the flags away from it, Tom would not have dived there and would not have suffered his neck and spinal injuries. Scope: Serious neck and spinal injury, three months in hospital and 12 months off work are natural and ordinary consequences of diving onto a hidden sandbar, and are exactly the kind of harm the earlier injuries warned of. Both tests are satisfied. Note: the facts combine medical costs and lost wages at $655,000 without a breakdown; I assume that figure is accurate. As Tom is a lawyer at a top firm, any future loss of earnings must also be claimed now.

Defences (Tom asks specifically)

Obvious risk / voluntary assumption of risk: The Council will argue that diving into surf is an obviously risky recreational activity (ss 13–15, 17–19 CLA), and, relying on Agar v Hyde, that Tom accepted the risk. However, the risk that actually materialised — a large sandbar "not visible from the water surface" — was hidden, not obvious, and was contrary to the sign saying conditions were safe. Unlike the rugby players in Agar v Hyde, who knew the inherent risks of a scrum, Tom had no knowledge of the precise risk. This defence is likely to fail.

Contributory negligence: The Council will argue that Tom was showing off ("Stay here and watch a pro!") and dived head-first only about five metres from shore, where water may be shallow, so he failed to take reasonable care for his own safety (Ingram v Britten). Tom will reply that he was between the flags, in front of the tower, relying on a sign saying "Safe water conditions", and that diving under a wave is ordinary surf behaviour — as in Swain. On balance, there may be a modest reduction of Tom's damages.

Conclusion

CONCLUSIONOn the balance of probabilities, Tom can bring an action in negligence against the Council and recover his medical costs and lost wages, possibly reduced for contributory negligence.

Issue 2

ISSUECan Bill, through Tom as his parent, bring an action against the Council in negligence for his acute stress and anxiety disorder?

Law

The same three elements apply. Nervous shock, or mental and psychiatric injury, is a recognised type of injury in negligence (Mrs Donoghue herself suffered nervous shock in Donoghue v Stevenson). The duty question is whether it was reasonably foreseeable that the Council's conduct could harm someone in Bill's position (the neighbour principle), and causation requires both s 11(1) tests.

The course slides do not cover special mental-harm rules or cases, so do not import any. Run it through the neighbour principle and s 11 — and say that's what you are doing.

Application

Duty: Bill was in the water between the flags with his father, a beach user whom the Council invited to swim there. It is reasonably foreseeable that if a swimmer is seriously injured by a hidden hazard, a family member standing beside him — particularly a 10-year-old child — would witness it and suffer psychiatric harm. Bill is a person so closely and directly affected that the Council ought to have had him in contemplation, so a duty is likely owed. The breach is the same as for Tom. Factual causation: Bill was diagnosed "as a result of having witnessed the incident"; but for the Council's breach, Tom would not have been injured and Bill would not have suffered the disorder. Scope: The Council will argue that Bill suffered no physical injury and that his harm is too remote (Overseas Tankships). However, a diagnosed psychiatric disorder in a young child who watches his father suffer a catastrophic spinal injury is a reasonably foreseeable type of harm. Contributory negligence does not apply to Bill, who did nothing careless.

Conclusion

CONCLUSIONOn the balance of probabilities, Bill can recover the cost of treating his disorder from the Council, although this claim is less certain than Tom's.

Remedies

Both will be awarded ordinary damages, which aim to restore them, so far as money can, to their position before the accident, assessed "once and for all". Tom can claim $655,000 (plus any future loss of earnings and medical costs), reduced by any contributory negligence; Bill can claim his medication and treatment costs (not stated in the facts). Tom should sue the Council, and any body that ran the patrol if it was separate.

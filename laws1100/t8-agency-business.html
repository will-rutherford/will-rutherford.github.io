① Learn it in 20 minutes

The big picture (read this first)

Every agency / partnership exam question is the same story: someone signed or bought something, the people behind them now say “they had no authority”, and a third party wants to be paid. You answer three questions, in this order:

  1. Is there an agency (or a partnership)? Who is principal (P), who is agent (A), who is the third party (3P)? If several people run a business together, test the 4 partnership requirements (s 5(1) Partnership Act 1891 (Qld)) — because if it is a partnership, every partner is the agent of the others.
  2. Did A have authority? Go through express actual → implied actual → apparent. If ANY one exists, P is bound to 3P and A is not personally liable.
  3. Who pays? If authority → P (and in a partnership, all partners jointly and personally). If no authority → P is not bound; 3P sues A personally for breach of warranty of authority. Then, if relevant, fiduciary duties — P can sue A for going outside instructions.

Week 12 slide 14: “Potentially multiple issues … Does a partnership exist? (4 requirements) · If a partnership exists, is there authority to act? · Have any fiduciary duties been breached?” Week 13 revision: always finish with Remedies.

1. What agency is

  • Definition: “a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party.” Any of the three can be an individual or a corporation.
  • The relationship is fiduciary: A must act in P’s best interests, avoid conflicts, and act within the scope of their authority.
  • A makes the contract between P and 3P but is not a party to it and is not liable under it — P is. A has a separate contract with P: the contract of agency.
  • Caution — the label “agent” is not decisive. The key question is whether the person has authority to bind the principal with third parties. An employee who just promotes/sells without power to bind the owner is not necessarily an agent.
  • Capacity: P needs full contractual capacity (and can’t authorise A to do what P couldn’t legally do). A does not need full capacity — even a minor can be an agent.
  • Outcome (slide 28): privity of contract is established between P and 3P, provided the agency was disclosed and A acted within actual or apparent authority.

2. Actual authority — comes from agreement between P and A

Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964] (Lord Diplock): “An actual authority is a legal relationship between principal and agent created by a consensual agreement to which they alone are parties.”

(a) Express actual authority

P expressly authorises A, in writing or orally, e.g. by deed (power of attorney), other writing, or orally. (Duration / scope / commission are practical tips, not elements you need to prove.)

(b) Implied actual authority

Whenever A is expressly authorised, there is implied authority to do all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the express instructions (the main task), and to give business efficacy to them. Appointment to a position often implies authority to make the contracts that position normally makes. Peterson v Moloney (1951) — implied authority was not found (use as the “against” case).

  • Real estate agent appointed to sell → implied authority to give buyers information about the house.
  • Partners → implied authority to act for the others in matters incidental to the partnership business.
  • Company officers (Giltrap City Ltd v Commerce Commission [2004]: the more senior the role, the more implied authority): single director or Chair alone → generally none; MD/CEO alone → contracts in the ordinary course of business; Company Secretary → administrative contracts only.

3. Apparent (ostensible) authority — comes from P’s representation to 3P

Where P leads 3P to reasonably believe A has authority, P is bound even though A had no actual authority — even if P expressly told A not to do it (slide 26). It protects third parties, who usually can’t see A’s actual authority. Three elements (slide 21):

  1. Lack of knowledge — 3P did not know A lacked actual authority;
  2. Holding out — P held A out as having authority (by words or conduct, e.g. appointing A to a position, or letting A act for P in the past);
  3. Reasonable reliance — 3P reasonably relied on P’s representation when dealing with A.
CaseFacts → held → use
Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971)Company secretary hired cars in FFF’s name; used some personally. FFF refused to pay, saying no actual authority. Held: FFF bound — hiring cars was within the usual authority of a company secretary, so within apparent authority. Use when A’s position/role makes the deal look normal.
Essington v Regency (2004)Agent purported to vary a contract. Held: no apparent authority — the principal had done nothing to represent the agent could make contractual changes. Use when the only “representation” came from A (A cannot create own authority by claiming it).
Summers v Solomon (1857)Supplier didn’t know A’s employment had ended → P still bound; A kept apparent authority.
Tooth v Laws (1888)Key question is whether 3P knew A’s actual authority had been withdrawn.

Termination/withdrawal: ending A’s actual authority does not end apparent authority. P must expressly inform 3P that A is no longer authorised (duty to inform, slide 24).

4. Agency of necessity (rare — only if facts scream emergency)

Four requirements: (1) A has possession/control of P’s property; (2) the property is under threat; (3) A cannot contact P for instructions; (4) A acts in good faith to protect it. Springer v Great Western Railway [1921] — failed requirement 3: they could have contacted the principal and did not.

5. Personal liability of the agent — breach of warranty of authority

Order (slide 29): first decide authority; then, if none, ask whether A is personally liable to 3P. A is liable if: (1) A claimed to be contracting on behalf of P; (2) A did not in fact have authority; (3) 3P relied on that representation and would not have contracted without it. Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]. A may also be liable in deceit, negligence, or under s 18 ACL (mention only).

Agent’s entitlements (= principal’s duties): no inherent right to remuneration (only if agreed); inherent right to indemnity and reimbursement for payments, expenses and liabilities incurred carrying out P’s instructions; a lien over P’s property in A’s possession if P refuses to pay.

Principal’s liability for A’s torts/crimes: depends on control. Independent contractor → P unlikely to be liable. Employee → P more likely vicariously liable for torts committed while carrying out P’s instructions.

Authority of partners

  • Express — granted by the other partners.
  • Implied — all the usual things necessary to carry out express authority and carry on the firm’s business: selling firm property, buying goods of the kind usually employed in the firm’s business, hiring employees, borrowing, incurring and paying debts. Can be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary — Construction v Hexyl (1985).
  • Apparent — s 8 PA: every partner is agent of the firm; acts done “for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm” bind the firm, unless the partner had no authority and the 3P knew that, or did not know/believe they were a partner. So where a partner lacks actual authority, the others are still liable if 4 requirements:
    1. the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm — Mercantile v Garrod (1962) (court looks at what the firm’s usual business actually is and what transactions a firm of that kind would usually engage in);
    2. it was done in the usual way — Goldberg v Jenkins (1889) (if not done the usual way, the 3P should suspect a lack of authority);
    3. 3P knew or believed they were a partner;
    4. 3P did not know or suspect the lack of actual authority — relied in good faith without notice — Crouch v IPG (2013).
  • The partnership agreement binds only the partners, not third parties (slide 10) — so a secret internal limit (e.g. “needs another partner’s permission”) does not stop apparent authority unless the 3P knew of it.

Partners’ fiduciary duties (to each other)

  • Not to profit personally from their position/information — Chan v Zaccharia (1984);
  • No undisclosed conflict of interest;
  • Full disclosure of all matters likely to affect the partnership — Law v Law (1905); s 31 PA;
  • Account for private profits made without consent — Birtchnell v Equity (1929); s 32 PA;
  • Not to compete with the firm — s 33 PA.

③ ILAC SCRIPT — write it in this order

Booklet order for a 25-mark agency/partnership question:
  1. Opener (1 line).
  2. Issue 1 — Partnership? (skip only if it is clearly a single principal + agent, e.g. home owner + real estate agent).
  3. Issue 2 — Authority for transaction #1 (express → implied → apparent). If there are two purchases, do Issue 3 separately for transaction #2 — the facts are always chosen so one is authorised and one is not.
  4. Issue 4 — Agent personally liable (breach of warranty of authority) — only for a transaction with no authority.
  5. (If the question is from the principal’s side) fiduciary duties.
  6. Remedies — who pays whom, how much.

Opener

Whether [3P] can recover [$X / the price of the …] depends upon three issues: first, whether [A, B and C] are in partnership; second, whether [A] had authority to bind [the firm / P] when [signing / purchasing …]; and third, if not, whether [A] is personally liable to [3P].

Script A — Does a partnership exist?

ISSUE
Were [A, B and C] carrying on business in partnership under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld), such that each was the agent of the others?
LAW
A partnership is “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit”: Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) (‘PA’) s 5(1). There are no registration requirements or formalities, and a partnership agreement may be written, oral or wholly implied from the partners’ conduct; a partnership exists whenever the four requirements are satisfied, regardless of the parties’ intention. The key concept is mutual liability: each partner is both principal and agent of the other partners, and each has unlimited personal liability for the firm’s contracts and debts: Kendall v Hamilton (1879).
Persons: at least two persons are required, and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115.
Carrying on a business: ‘business’ includes any trade, occupation or profession (PA s 3), and ‘carrying on’ requires some continuity or repetition of trading activities. A single transaction or one-off project is usually not a partnership: Smith v Anderson (1880). However, a partnership commences from the date the parties begin working together to establish an identifiable business: Khan v Miah [2000].
In common: each person must act on behalf of the others as well as themselves. An employer and employee carry on a business together but are not partners, because while the employee acts on behalf of the employer, the employer does not act on behalf of the employee: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Not all partners need take an active part; even silent partners are liable.
With a view of profit: the parties must be seeking a profit; it remains a partnership even if they expect or make a loss: Minter v Minter (2000).
APPLICATION
Persons: there are [three] persons, [names], which is within the limit in s 115, so this requirement is satisfied.
Business: [name] has operated [an event planning business / as a band] since [date], [performing across Australia for two years / with a website and growing client base]. Unlike Smith v Anderson, this is not a one-off project but a continuing trade, so they are carrying on a business. [Late joiner:] Consistent with Khan v Miah, [Robert] became a partner from the time he began working in the business in [date].
In common — for: they [share an office / manage finances through a joint account / split profits equally / decide by majority vote / were introduced to clients as part of the team], which shows each acting on behalf of the others. The absence of a formal agreement is irrelevant, as the agreement may be implied from conduct.
In common — against: [X] may argue that [Y] is merely an employee, like the employee in Degiorgio v Dunn, because [Y receives a salary / pursues individual freelance work]. However, this is unlikely to succeed because [Y] [shares in the profits / can draw on the joint account / has an equal vote] — rights an employee would not have; the [salary] merely compensates [extra responsibilities].
Profit: the business has [generated $800,000 in profits / profits split equally], so it is clearly carried on with a view of profit (Minter v Minter).
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [A, B and C] are partners under s 5(1) PA, so each is the agent of the firm and of the other partners.

Script B — Did the agent / partner have authority?

ISSUE
Is [P / the firm and its partners] bound by the contract [A] made with [3P] for [the stage / drum set], on the basis that [A] had authority under the law of agency?
LAW
Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party and the agent incurs no personal liability. An agent may be authorised by actual authority — express or implied — or by apparent authority.
Actual authority “is a legal relationship between principal and agent created by a consensual agreement to which they alone are parties”: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express actual authority is given by the principal in writing (including by deed, a power of attorney) or orally. Implied actual authority extends to all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the express instructions, or needed to give them business efficacy; it is not found merely because it would be convenient: Peterson v Moloney (1951).
(Partnership add-on:) Each partner has implied authority to do the usual things necessary to carry on the firm’s business, such as buying goods of the kind usually employed in that business, borrowing money and incurring debts. Implied authority can be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985).
Apparent authority arises where the principal leads a third party to reasonably believe the agent has authority; the principal is then bound even if the agent had no actual authority, and even if the principal expressly prohibited the act. Three requirements must be met: (1) the third party did not know the agent lacked actual authority; (2) the principal held the agent out as having authority, for example by appointing them to a position or by allowing them to act in the past; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on that representation. In Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), a company secretary hired cars in the company’s name, partly for personal use; the company was bound because hiring cars fell within a secretary’s usual, and therefore apparent, authority. The representation must come from the principal, not the agent: in Essington v Regency (2004) there was no apparent authority to vary a contract because the principal had done nothing to represent that the agent could do so.
(Partnership — use instead of / as well as the 3 elements:) Under s 8 PA, every partner is an agent of the firm, and acts done “for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm” bind the firm and the partners, unless the partner had no authority and the third party knew this or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Accordingly, where a partner lacks actual authority, the firm is still bound if: (1) the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm — assessed by what the firm’s usual business actually is and what transactions a firm of that kind would usually engage in: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) the business was carried on in the usual way, as otherwise the other party should suspect a lack of authority: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person was a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of authority, relying in good faith and without notice: Crouch v IPG (2013). The partnership agreement binds only the partners, not third parties.
APPLICATION

Relationship:

[P] is the principal, [A] is the agent and [3P] is the third party. (Partnership:) As established above, [A] is a partner and therefore an agent of the firm and of [B and C] (s 8 PA).

Express:

[A] was expressly authorised to [spend up to $50,000 on upgrading musical equipment with another member’s permission / sell to the first buyer over $1 million]. The [purchase / sale] [fell within / fell outside] this because [it was musical equipment under $50,000 / but no permission was obtained / the price was below $1 million]. There is no evidence that [B and C] expressly authorised [A] to […]; indeed they were [“furious” / “outraged”] on learning of it.

Implied:

[3P] will argue that [A] had implied authority because [buying band equipment / event equipment] is a usual thing necessary to carry on the firm’s business. However, [the firm only ever hired equipment / decisions were made by majority vote / the agreement required another member’s permission], and as in Construction v Hexyl, implied authority is overridden by that express arrangement. As in Peterson v Moloney, implied authority is unlikely to be found.

Apparent — the 3 elements / s 8 PA:

Kind of business (for): [a drum set is exactly the equipment a touring band uses], so similar to Panorama, the purchase falls within what a partner of such a firm would usually do. (against): [the firm runs small functions for under 100 guests and a $180,000 custom-built stage is for large-scale concerts and festivals — a new kind of business / a painting is a personal item unrelated to performing music], so applying Mercantile v Garrod, it is not a transaction a firm of this kind would usually engage in.
Usual way: [A] [regularly buys equipment from 3P on behalf of the band, paid with the band card and had it sent to the studio] — the usual way. OR The firm usually [hires] equipment, so [buying a $180,000 item / delivering to a home address] was not the usual way, and as in Goldberg v Jenkins [3P] should have suspected a lack of authority.
Holding out / knew or believed A a partner: [B and C] held [A] out as having authority by [introducing him to clients as part of the team and updating promotional materials / allowing her to buy band equipment from 3P on previous occasions]. This is a representation by the principals themselves, unlike Essington v Regency. (If only A spoke:) [A]’s own statement that [“I am authorised…”] cannot create authority (Essington), but [P] held A out by [the sign / appointment].
No knowledge / reasonable reliance: there is no evidence [3P] knew of [the internal permission requirement / the $1 million limit], which binds only the partners, so [3P] relied in good faith. OR [A] told [3P] that [he still needed to convince B and C], so [3P] was on notice of the lack of authority and cannot satisfy requirement 4 (Crouch v IPG).
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [A] [had / did not have] apparent authority, so [P / the firm and partners B and C] [is / are not] bound to pay [3P] for [item].

Script C — Is the agent personally liable to the third party?

ISSUE
Can [3P] hold [A] personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?
LAW
Where an agent lacks authority, the agent will be personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed to be making the contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent did not in fact have authority; and (3) the third party relied upon the representation and would not have entered into the contract without it: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]. The agent may also be liable in deceit or negligence, or for misleading conduct under s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law.
APPLICATION
First, [A] [signed the agreement “on behalf of CT Events” / charged it to the band’s card], representing that [A] was contracting for [the firm]. Second, as concluded above, [A] had no actual or apparent authority. Third, [3P] would not have [supplied a $180,000 stage] to [A] personally; it contracted because it believed [the firm] stood behind the deal. Against: [A] may argue [3P] did not rely on any representation of authority because [A told it he still needed approval].
CONCLUSION
On the balance of probabilities, [A] is personally liable to [3P] for breach of warranty of authority.

Script D — Did the agent / partner breach fiduciary duties? (when advising the principal)

ISSUE
Did [A] breach the fiduciary duties owed to [P / the other partners]?
LAW
Agents owe fiduciary duties to their principal because of the relationship of trust and confidence between them; these duties arise in equity and are often also terms of the contract of agency. They include the duty to follow the principal’s lawful instructions, failing which the agent is personally liable for the resulting harm (Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830)); to exercise due care, skill and diligence (Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991)); to act in person (John McCann v Pow (1975)); to act in the principal’s interests and avoid conflicts, secret profits and commissions (Hewson v Sydney (1968)); to communicate relevant information (John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002)); to keep confidentiality; and to keep proper accounts.
(Partners:) Partners owe each other fiduciary duties at common law and under the PA, including not to profit personally from their position (Chan v Zaccharia (1984)), to disclose all matters affecting the partnership (Law v Law (1905); s 31 PA), to account for private profits made without consent (Birtchnell v Equity (1929); s 32 PA), and not to compete with the firm (s 33 PA).
APPLICATION
[A] breached the duty to follow instructions because [P instructed in writing to sell only for over $1 million, yet A accepted $850,000], as in Bertram Armstrong v Godfray. [A] also failed to act in [P]’s interests and with due care, since [accepting $350,000 below valuation] is not what a reasonable agent would do. As the instructions were in writing, this is also a breach of the contract of agency.
CONCLUSION
Therefore, [A] breached [the duties to follow instructions and act in P’s interests], and [P] may recover damages from [A] for the loss.

Remedies (always finish with this)

If authority: [3P] may enforce the contract against [P / the firm] and claim [$X]. As partners have unlimited personal liability and are jointly liable for the firm’s debts (Kendall v Hamilton), [3P] may sue [A, B and C] and recover from their personal assets; a partnership is not a separate legal entity, although it may be sued in the firm name.
If no authority: [P] is not bound and may [return the goods / recover the payment]; [3P]’s remedy is damages against [A] personally for breach of warranty of authority.
Internal: [P / B and C] may also sue [A] for breach of fiduciary duty and the contract of agency / partnership, to recover [the deposit / loss]; an agent who acted outside instructions has no right to indemnity for that act, and has no right to remuneration unless agreed.

④ Full model answers

Model answer 1 — 2025 Semester 2, Question 2 (StageCorp v CT Events) 25 marks

Question text (click)

In mid-2022, Callum and Tessa launched an event planning business in Sydney called CT Events, specialising in small corporate functions and private celebrations with fewer than 100 guests. To minimise costs, they did not buy equipment but instead arranged to hire equipment from a hire company. They created a website and developed a growing social media presence under the CT Events name, and the business steadily expanded.

In early 2023, Callum and Tessa invited their friend, Robert, to join the business. Robert brought considerable experience in design and business management. Although the business continued operating as CT Events and no formal agreement was made, Callum and Tessa began introducing Robert to clients as part of the team, and the promotional materials were updated to include him.

They all began working out of a shared office and managed finances through a joint account. Profits from business projects were split equally, although each person occasionally pursued individual freelance work. CT Events’s business decisions were made informally, with disagreements resolved by majority vote.

In August 2024, while Callum and Tessa were working at an event, Robert signed a $180,000 contract with StageCorp Pty Ltd to purchase a custom-built stage. He paid an $18,000 deposit from the joint account and signed the agreement on behalf of CT Events. Robert told StageCorp’s representative that his long-term goal was to expand the business into large-scale concerts and festivals, and that owning stage equipment would help them remain competitive – provided he could convince Callum and Tessa to support the idea.

Two months later, the stage was delivered to the office. Callum and Tessa, outraged by the purchase, informed StageCorp that CT Events would not be paying for it, stating that the stage would be returned. They argued Robert had no authority to make the purchase on behalf of the business.

Issue 1: Partnership

Issue: Were Callum, Tessa and Robert carrying on CT Events in partnership under the PA, such that each was the agent of the others?

Law: A partnership is “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit”: PA s 5(1). No registration or formalities are required, and the partnership agreement may be written, oral or wholly implied from the parties’ conduct. A partnership exists whenever the four requirements are satisfied, regardless of the parties’ intention. Each partner is both a principal and an agent of the others, and each has unlimited personal liability for the firm’s debts: Kendall v Hamilton (1879).

First, there must be at least two persons, and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115. Second, they must be carrying on a business, which includes a trade, occupation or profession (PA s 3) and requires some continuity or repetition of activity; a one-off project is usually not a partnership: Smith v Anderson (1880). A partnership begins from the date the parties begin working together in an identifiable business: Khan v Miah [2000]. Third, the business must be carried on in common, meaning each acts on behalf of the others as well as themselves. An employer and employee are not partners, because the employer does not act on the employee’s behalf: Degiorgio v Dunn (2004). Fourth, the business must be carried on with a view of profit, even if a loss is in fact made: Minter v Minter (2000).

Application:

Persons. There are three persons — Callum, Tessa and Robert — well within the limit of 20. This requirement is satisfied.

Carrying on a business. CT Events has operated as an event planning business since mid-2022, with a website, a growing social media presence and a steadily expanding client base. Unlike Smith v Anderson, this is a continuing trade, not a single project. Robert joined in early 2023 and began working out of the shared office; consistent with Khan v Miah, he became a partner from the time he began working in the business, despite no formal agreement being made.

In common. The three work from a shared office, manage finances through a joint account, split profits equally and resolve disagreements by majority vote. Callum and Tessa introduced Robert to clients “as part of the team” and updated promotional materials to include him. These facts show each acting on behalf of the others, not one acting for an employer. Callum and Tessa may argue that Robert was merely brought in for his design and management experience, and that each person’s individual freelance work shows they were not truly in business together. However, unlike the employee in Degiorgio v Dunn, Robert receives an equal share of profits, has access to the joint account and has an equal vote — none of which is consistent with employment. Occasional freelance work does not change the character of the CT Events business itself (although it may raise a separate issue of competing with the firm under s 33 PA, which is not relevant to StageCorp).

View of profit. Profits from projects are split equally, so the business is clearly carried on with a view of profit (Minter v Minter).

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Callum, Tessa and Robert are partners in CT Events under s 5(1) PA, so Robert is an agent of the firm and of Callum and Tessa.

Issue 2: Robert’s authority to purchase the stage

Issue: Are CT Events and its partners bound by the $180,000 contract Robert signed with StageCorp, on the basis that Robert had authority under the law of agency?

Law: Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. Where the agent acts within their authority, the contract is between the principal and the third party. Authority may be actual (express or implied) or apparent.

Actual authority is “a legal relationship between principal and agent created by a consensual agreement to which they alone are parties”: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Express authority is given in writing or orally. Partners have implied authority to do the usual things necessary to carry on the firm’s business, such as buying goods of the kind usually employed in that business and incurring debts; but implied authority may be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985). Implied authority will not always be found: Peterson v Moloney (1951).

Apparent authority. Under s 8 PA, every partner is an agent of the firm, and acts “for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm” bind the firm and the partners, unless the partner had no authority and the third party knew this, or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Accordingly, where a partner lacks actual authority, the firm is still bound if: (1) the partner was carrying on business of the kind carried on by the firm, judged by what the firm’s usual business actually is and what transactions such a firm would usually engage in: Mercantile v Garrod (1962); (2) the business was carried on in the usual way, because otherwise the third party should suspect a lack of authority: Goldberg v Jenkins (1889); (3) the third party knew or believed the person to be a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect that the partner lacked authority, relying in good faith and without notice: Crouch v IPG (2013). This reflects the general rule that apparent authority depends on the principal holding the agent out, not on the agent’s own claims: Essington v Regency (2004).

Notice: s 8 gives FOUR requirements for partners — use them (not just the three general agency elements) when it is a partnership question.

Application:

Express actual authority. There is no evidence that Callum or Tessa expressly authorised Robert to buy a stage. The purchase was made while they were working at an event, and they were “outraged” on learning of it. Robert himself acknowledged he still needed to “convince Callum and Tessa”. There was no express authority.

Implied actual authority. StageCorp will argue that buying event equipment is a usual thing necessary to carry on an event planning business. However, CT Events deliberately did not buy equipment — it hired it to minimise costs — and decisions were made by majority vote. That established practice operates, as in Construction v Hexyl, to override any implied authority for one partner alone to commit the firm to a $180,000 purchase. As in Peterson v Moloney, implied authority is unlikely to be found.

Apparent authority — business of the kind carried on by the firm. StageCorp will argue that a stage is event equipment and CT Events is an events business. However, applying Mercantile v Garrod, the court looks at what CT Events’ business actually is: small corporate functions and private celebrations of fewer than 100 guests. A custom-built stage was, on Robert’s own words, for expanding into “large-scale concerts and festivals” — a different kind of business. This requirement is probably not met.

Usual way. Even if the stage were within the firm’s kind of business, CT Events’ usual way was to hire equipment, not buy it, and a $180,000 custom-built purchase by one partner is out of proportion to a small events business. As in Goldberg v Jenkins, a transaction not carried out in the usual way should have caused StageCorp to suspect a lack of authority.

Knew or believed Robert to be a partner. This requirement is satisfied. Robert signed “on behalf of CT Events” and paid the deposit from the joint account, and Callum and Tessa themselves held Robert out by introducing him to clients as part of the team and including him in promotional materials. Unlike Essington v Regency, the holding out came from the principals, not merely from Robert.

No knowledge or suspicion of lack of authority. This is StageCorp’s greatest difficulty. Robert told StageCorp’s representative that the purchase was part of his own “long-term goal” and would help — “provided he could convince Callum and Tessa to support the idea”. StageCorp was therefore on notice that Callum and Tessa had not yet agreed, and that Robert needed their approval. StageCorp did not rely in good faith and without notice (Crouch v IPG); a reasonable supplier would have sought confirmation from the other partners before accepting a $180,000 order.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Robert had no actual or apparent authority, so CT Events and Callum and Tessa are not bound by the stage contract.

Issue 3: Robert’s personal liability

Issue: Can StageCorp hold Robert personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?

Law: Where an agent lacks authority, the agent is personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed to make the contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent did not in fact have authority; and (3) the third party relied on the representation and would not have contracted without it: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020]. The agent may also be liable in deceit, negligence or under s 18 of the Australian Consumer Law.

Application: First, Robert signed the agreement “on behalf of CT Events” and paid the deposit from the firm’s joint account, representing that he was contracting for the firm. Second, as concluded in Issue 2, he had no authority. Third, StageCorp would not have built a $180,000 custom stage for Robert personally; it contracted because it believed an established business stood behind the order. Robert may argue that StageCorp cannot have relied on any claim of authority, because he disclosed that he still needed his partners’ support. However, by nonetheless signing a binding contract in the firm’s name and paying a deposit from the firm’s account, Robert represented that the firm was committed, and StageCorp proceeded on that basis.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Robert is personally liable to StageCorp for breach of warranty of authority.

Remedies and advice

StageCorp’s best course is to claim damages against Robert personally for breach of warranty of authority, for the loss caused by having no enforceable contract with CT Events — up to the $162,000 balance, less what StageCorp can recover by taking back and reselling the stage, which Callum and Tessa have offered to return. In the alternative, StageCorp may argue that Robert had apparent authority under s 8 PA. If a court accepted that argument, the firm would be bound and Callum, Tessa and Robert would be jointly and personally liable for the $162,000 (Kendall v Hamilton), as a partnership is not a separate legal entity, and StageCorp could recover from their personal assets. Given Robert’s statement that he still needed to convince his partners, this alternative is weaker. Separately, Callum and Tessa may pursue Robert for breach of his fiduciary duties to the partnership (for example, failing to disclose matters affecting the firm under s 31 PA) to recover the $18,000 deposit taken from the joint account, but that is a matter between the partners.

Remedies answers the actual question asked (“best course of action to recover $162,000”) — say WHO to sue first and why.

Issue 1: Partnership

Issue: Are Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne partners in Pop Rocks under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) (‘PA’), such that Roxanne is an agent of the others?

Law: A partnership is “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit”: PA s 5(1). It exists whenever four requirements are met, regardless of formality or intention, and each partner is both principal and agent of the others (PA s 8), with unlimited personal liability (Kendall v Hamilton (1879)). The requirements are: (1) at least two persons (and generally no more than 20: Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) s 115); (2) carrying on a business — a trade, occupation or profession (s 3) with continuity, not a one-off transaction (Smith v Anderson (1880)); (3) in common — each acting for the others as well as themselves, which distinguishes partners from an employer and employee (Degiorgio v Dunn (2004)); and (4) with a view of profit (Minter v Minter (2000)).

Application: There are three persons. Pop Rocks has performed in major cities across Australia for two years, generating $800,000 in profit — a continuing trade, unlike the one-off venture in Smith v Anderson. The profit requirement is plainly satisfied.

The key dispute is whether Roxanne is acting “in common” or is, as Kevin and Maxine claim, their employee. In support of their claim, Roxanne is paid a $50,000 salary and holds the role of marketing manager, which resembles employment as in Degiorgio v Dunn. However, Roxanne receives the same 20% profit share as Maxine and Kevin, and has the same right to spend from the shared account. These are the rights of a co-owner acting on behalf of all members, not an employee. Her salary is expressly paid only to compensate her “extra responsibilities”, which does not change her status as a band member.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne are partners, so Roxanne is an agent of the firm and of Maxine and Kevin.

Issue 2: The electronic drum set

Issue: Are Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne (the firm) liable to Bob for the $36,000 drum set, on the basis that Roxanne had authority to purchase it under the law of agency?

Law: Agency is a legal relationship in which an agent is authorised to act on behalf of a principal in dealings with a third party. If the agent acts within their authority, the contract binds the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability. Authority may be actual or apparent.

Actual authority is created by consensual agreement between principal and agent: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. It may be express (written or oral) or implied. A partner has implied authority to do the usual things needed to carry on the firm’s business, including buying goods of the kind usually employed in that business; however, implied authority can be overridden by the partnership agreement or express instructions to the contrary: Construction v Hexyl (1985).

Apparent authority arises where the principal leads a third party to reasonably believe the agent has authority, by appointing the agent to a position or allowing them to act in the past; the principal is bound even if it expressly told the agent not to act. In Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), a company secretary hired cars in the company’s name without actual authority, partly for personal use; the company was bound because such contracts were within the secretary’s usual, and therefore apparent, authority. For partners, s 8 PA provides that the firm is bound where the partner: (1) carries on business of the kind carried on by the firm (Mercantile v Garrod (1962)); (2) in the usual way (Goldberg v Jenkins (1889)); (3) the third party knew or believed them to be a partner; and (4) the third party did not know or suspect the lack of authority, relying in good faith without notice (Crouch v IPG (2013)). The partnership agreement binds only the partners, not third parties.

Application:

Express authority. The members agreed that each could spend up to $50,000 on upgrading musical equipment, with the permission of at least one other member. The drum set is musical equipment and its $36,000 price is within the limit. However, there is no evidence that Roxanne obtained Maxine’s or Kevin’s permission — both were furious to learn of the purchase. Roxanne therefore acted outside her express authority.

Implied authority. Buying band equipment is a usual part of carrying on a band’s business. However, as in Construction v Hexyl, the members’ express agreement requiring permission overrides any wider implied authority. Roxanne lacked actual authority.

Apparent authority. First, a drum set is exactly the type of goods a touring band uses, and was bought to upgrade equipment for the band’s Christmas performances; it is business of the kind carried on by the firm. Second, it was carried out in the usual way: Roxanne “regularly buys band equipment” from Bob “on behalf of the band”, paid on the card linked to the band’s shared account, and had the drum set sent to the “normal address”, the band’s recording studio. Third, Bob knew Roxanne as a member of the band who bought on its behalf; Maxine and Kevin held her out as having authority by allowing these purchases in the past. Similar to Panorama, where the secretary’s position made the car hire look normal, Roxanne’s established role made this purchase appear authorised. Fourth, there is no evidence that Bob knew of the internal permission requirement, which binds only the members. Maxine and Kevin may argue that the 20% discount should have made Bob suspicious; however, the discount was explained by Bob’s regular dealings with the band and his being a fan, and does not suggest any lack of authority.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Roxanne had apparent authority, so Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne are liable for the drum set and are not entitled to a refund.

Issue 3: The limited-edition painting

Issue: Are Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne liable to Bob for the $10,000 painting, on the basis that Roxanne had authority to purchase it under the law of agency?

Law: The law set out in Issue 2 applies. In addition, apparent authority requires a representation by the principal; an agent cannot create their own authority: Essington v Regency (2004), where an agent had no apparent authority to vary a contract because the principal had done nothing to represent that the agent could do so.

Application: Roxanne had no express authority, because a painting is not “musical equipment” and no permission was obtained; nor any implied authority, because buying artwork is not a usual thing needed to carry on a band’s business. As to apparent authority, first, a signed painting of Roxanne’s “favourite musician” is a personal item, not a transaction a band would usually engage in (Mercantile v Garrod). Second, it was not done in the usual way: Roxanne asked Bob to send it to her home address rather than the band’s studio, and as in Goldberg v Jenkins, this departure should have made Bob suspect a lack of authority. Third, while Maxine and Kevin had held Roxanne out as able to buy band equipment, they had never represented that she could buy art; as in Essington v Regency, Roxanne’s own instruction to “put it on the same card” cannot create authority. Bob may argue he reasonably assumed any purchase on the band’s card was authorised, but given the personal nature of the item and the home delivery, that reliance is not reasonable.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Roxanne had no authority to buy the painting, so Maxine and Kevin are not liable for it.

Issue 4: Roxanne’s personal liability for the painting

Issue: Can Bob hold Roxanne personally liable for the painting?

Law: An agent who lacks authority is personally liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority if: (1) the agent claimed to contract on behalf of the principal; (2) the agent had no authority; and (3) the third party relied on that representation and would not otherwise have contracted: Oliana Foods Pty Ltd v Culinary Co [2020].

Application: By telling Bob to put the painting “on the same card” — the band’s card — Roxanne represented that the band was paying. She had no authority (Issue 3), and Bob, who discounted the item because of his relationship with the band, accepted payment on the basis that the band’s card would honour it. Alternatively, if Roxanne is treated as having bought the painting for herself, she is simply the buyer and personally liable under the contract of sale.

Conclusion: Either way, Roxanne is personally liable to Bob for the $10,000.

Remedies

Bob may retain the $36,000 for the drum set; the firm is bound, and Maxine, Kevin and Roxanne are jointly and personally liable for it (Kendall v Hamilton). As to the painting, Bob may need to refund the $10,000 charged to the band’s account, but he may recover $10,000 (or damages for breach of warranty of authority) from Roxanne personally, or seek the return of the painting. Bob should also be advised that Maxine and Kevin may separately pursue Roxanne for breach of her fiduciary duty as a partner not to use her position for private profit and to account for it (Birtchnell v Equity (1929); s 32 PA) — but that is a matter between the partners.

Issue 1: Davo’s authority

Issue: Did Davo have authority to act on behalf of his principal, Cristina, when accepting Patrizia’s $850,000 offer, so that Cristina is bound by the contract?

Law: Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. The relationship gives rise to obligations on both principal and agent which are both contractual and fiduciary in nature. An agent may be authorised by actual authority — express or implied — or by apparent authority. If the agent acts within the scope of their authority, the resulting contract is between the principal and the third party, and the agent incurs no personal liability.

Express actual authority is authority expressly given by the principal to the agent, in writing (including by deed, such as a power of attorney) or orally; actual authority arises from a consensual agreement to which principal and agent alone are parties: Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties Ltd [1964]. Implied actual authority is the authority to do all things necessarily incidental to carrying out the express instructions, or needed to give them business efficacy; for example, a real estate agent appointed to sell a house has implied authority to give buyers information about it. Implied authority is not found merely because it would be convenient: Peterson v Moloney (1951).

Apparent authority: if the principal allows a third party to believe the agent has authority, the principal will be bound to honour the agent’s dealings with the third party even if the agent was not actually authorised, and even if the principal told the agent not to act that way. The three requirements are that: (1) the third party did not know the agent lacked actual authority; (2) the principal held the agent out as having authority, for example by appointing them to a particular position; and (3) the third party reasonably relied on the principal’s representation. In Panorama v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics (1971), a company secretary without actual authority hired cars in the company’s name; the company was bound because the contracts fell within the usual authority of the secretary’s position. However, the representation must come from the principal: an agent cannot create their own authority by claiming it: Essington v Regency (2004).

Application: A relationship of principal and agent exists: Cristina is the principal, Davo is the agent and Patrizia is the third party.

Actual authority. Cristina gave Davo express authority, in writing, to sell the house to the first buyer offering over $1 million, and to put up a “For Sale” sign directing enquiries to him. Davo also had implied authority to do things necessarily incidental to that task, such as answering enquiries, showing Patrizia through the house and providing information about it. However, in accepting $850,000, Davo acted outside his actual authority, which was expressly confined to offers over $1 million.

Apparent authority. As was the case in Panorama, where the company was bound by contracts within the usual authority of the position it had given the secretary, Patrizia would argue that Davo had apparent authority. First, Patrizia did not know of the $1 million limit — Davo said nothing to her about it. Second, Cristina herself held Davo out as her agent with authority to deal with buyers: she appointed him as her selling agent and instructed him to erect a sign at her property stating “All enquiries to David Barnes”. Cristina will rely on Essington v Regency to argue that Davo’s statement that he was “authorized by the owner to accept any offer that I determine to be reasonable” came only from the agent and cannot create authority. That is correct as far as it goes, but Patrizia does not rely on Davo’s words alone; Cristina’s own appointment of Davo and her sign are representations by the principal. Third, Patrizia, a first home buyer, saw the sign, contacted the agency it named and dealt with the agent Cristina had appointed; her reliance was reasonable.

Conclusion: On the balance of probabilities, Davo had apparent authority to act on behalf of Cristina, so Patrizia can enforce the contract against Cristina and the contract will not be rescinded.

Issue 2: Davo’s fiduciary duties

Issue: Did Davo breach the fiduciary duties he owed to Cristina?

Law: Agents owe fiduciary duties to their principal as a result of the relationship of trust and confidence between them. These include the duty to follow the principal’s lawful instructions, a breach of which makes the agent personally liable to the principal for the resulting harm (Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830)); to act in the principal’s interests and not put the agent’s interests in conflict with them (Hewson v Sydney (1968)); to exercise due care, skill and diligence, failing to do what a reasonable agent would do in the circumstances (Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991)); and to communicate relevant information to the principal (John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002)). These duties may also be imposed as contractual duties under the agency agreement.

Application: It is likely that Davo breached his fiduciary duties to Cristina because: first, he disobeyed her lawful, written instruction to sell only for over $1 million, as in Bertram Armstrong v Godfray; second, accepting $850,000 for a house valued at around $1.2 million was contrary to Cristina’s interests, and is not what a reasonable agent exercising due care and skill would do; and third, he did not communicate the below-threshold offer to Cristina before accepting it — although she was overseas, there is no suggestion she could not be contacted. Since the instructions were in writing, this is also a breach of the terms of the contract of agency, which amounts to a breach of contract by Davo.

Conclusion: Davo has breached his fiduciary duties and his contract of agency with Cristina.

Remedies

On the balance of probabilities, a court would likely decide that Patrizia can enforce the contract under apparent authority, so Cristina cannot rescind it, and she has no claim against Patrizia, an innocent third party who acted in good faith. However, Cristina is entitled to damages from Davo for the loss caused by his breaches — at least the $150,000 difference between the sale price and her $1 million minimum (and arguably more, given the $1.2 million valuation). Davo has no right to remuneration for the sale unless the agency agreement provides for it, and he cannot claim an indemnity for acting outside Cristina’s instructions.

This mirrors the lecturer’s own template conclusion: “Patrizia can enforce … However, Davo has breached his … Therefore Cristina would be entitled to [damages] from Davo.”

⑤ Traps

Stopping after “no actual authority”. ALWAYS go on to apparent authority — that is where the marks are, and the facts are written to make it arguable.
Treating the agent’s own words (“I’m authorised…”) as holding out. The representation must come from the principal (Essington v Regency). Look for what P did: appointment, title, past dealings, signs, promotional materials.
Accepting a party’s label. “Employee”, “company card”, “agent”, “joint venture” — test the facts against s 5(1) PA (and check whether any company was actually registered) before using the label.
Forgetting that a secret internal limit (spending cap, “needs another member’s permission”) binds only the partners — it stops actual authority but not apparent authority unless the third party knew of it.
Missing the “notice” fact. A single line like “provided he could convince Callum and Tessa” or “send it to my home address” is the examiner telling you requirement 2 or 4 of s 8 fails.
Two purchases = two separate authority analyses. Don’t lump them together — one is usually authorised, the other not.
Skipping the agent’s personal liability. If you find no authority, the third party is not left empty-handed: breach of warranty of authority (Oliana Foods). That is the “remedy” for the person you’re advising.
Out-of-course doctrines: ratification and undisclosed principal are not in the LAWS1100 slides. Don’t build an issue on them (citing outside material is a red flag). Slide 28 only says privity arises “provided the existence of the agency was disclosed”.
Using company law (Salomon, directors) for a partnership. A partnership is NOT a separate legal entity — partners are personally liable (Kendall v Hamilton).
Jurisdiction: facts set in Sydney etc. — say you are applying the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld) as taught and assume the local Act is equivalent.

Opener

Whether [3P] can recover [$X] depends upon three issues: first, whether [A, B and C] are in partnership; second, whether [A] had authority to bind [the firm] when [purchasing …]; and third, if not, whether [A] is personally liable to [3P].
I1 — Partnership
Issue: Were [A, B, C] carrying on business in partnership under the Partnership Act 1891 (Qld), such that each was the agent of the others?
Law: “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit” — s 5(1) PA. No formalities; agreement may be implied from conduct; exists regardless of intention. Each partner = principal + agent of others; unlimited personal liability — Kendall v Hamilton (1879).
  1. Persons ≥2, ≤20 (s 115 CA; prof. exceptions reg 2A.1.01).
  2. Carrying on a business — trade/occupation/profession (s 3); continuity; one-off ≠ partnership Smith v Anderson (1880); starts when they begin working together Khan v Miah [2000].
  3. In common — each acts for the others; employer/employee ≠ partners Degiorgio v Dunn (2004); silent partners still liable.
  4. View of profit — even if loss Minter v Minter (2000).
Facts ✔: joint account · equal profit split · majority vote · shared office · “part of the team” · promo materials · years of trading.
Facts ✘: fixed salary only, no profit share/no say · single project · freelance on the side (weak).
I2/I3 — Authority
Issue: Is [P / the firm] bound by the contract [A] made with [3P] for [item], on the basis that [A] had authority under the law of agency?
Law opener: Agency is a legal relationship in which one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) in dealings with a third party. If A acts within authority, the contract is between P and 3P and A incurs no personal liability. Authority: actual (express / implied) or apparent.
Actual = consensual agreement between P and A alone — Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park [1964].
Express — writing (deed/power of attorney) or oral.
Implied — all things necessarily incidental to express instructions / business efficacy; not found in Peterson v Moloney (1951). Partners: usual things to carry on the business — sell firm property, buy goods of the kind usually used, hire staff, borrow, incur/pay debts; overridden by agreement/express contrary instruction — Construction v Hexyl (1985).
Company officers: director/Chair alone → none; MD/CEO → ordinary course; Secretary → administrative — Giltrap City v Commerce Commission [2004].
Apparent — general (3 elements)
P leads 3P to reasonably believe A has authority → P bound even if A had none / was told not to.
  1. Lack of knowledge — 3P didn’t know A lacked authority;
  2. Holding out by P — appointment to position / past dealings;
  3. Reasonable reliance on P’s representation.
Panorama v Fidelis (1971) — co. secretary hired cars (some personal use); within usual → apparent authority; company bound.
Essington v Regency (2004) — agent varied contract; P did nothing to represent authority → none. A can’t create own authority.
Withdrawal: apparent authority survives until 3P told — Summers v Solomon (1857); Tooth v Laws (1888); P has duty to inform 3P.
Apparent — partners (s 8 PA, 4 reqs)
Every partner is agent of the firm; acts “for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm” bind the firm unless no authority AND 3P knew / didn’t believe them a partner.
  1. Business of the kind carried on by firm — what is the firm’s actual business + what deals such a firm usually does — Mercantile v Garrod (1962);
  2. Done in the usual way — otherwise 3P should suspect — Goldberg v Jenkins (1889);
  3. 3P knew/believed they were a partner;
  4. 3P didn’t know/suspect lack of authority — good faith, no notice — Crouch v IPG (2013).
Partnership agreement binds partners only, not 3Ps.

Application starters (authority)

[P] is the principal, [A] the agent and [3P] the third party. As established, [A] is a partner and therefore an agent of the firm and of [B and C] (s 8 PA).
Express: [A] was expressly authorised to [spend up to $X on … with permission]. The purchase [fell within / outside] this because [no permission was obtained / not musical equipment / price below limit]. [B and C] were [“furious”], showing no express authority.
Implied: [3P] will argue buying [equipment] is a usual thing to carry on the business. However, [the firm only hired / majority vote / permission rule] overrides this, as in Construction v Hexyl.
Kind of business: [item] is [exactly what a … uses] → within the firm’s business, similar to Panorama. / [item] is [personal / for a different, larger-scale business], so applying Mercantile v Garrod it is not a transaction a firm of this kind would usually engage in.
Usual way: [A] regularly buys from [3P] on behalf of [firm], paid on the firm card, delivered to the [studio] — the usual way. / [home delivery / $180k purchase when they always hire] is not the usual way; as in Goldberg v Jenkins, [3P] should have suspected.
Holding out: [B and C] held [A] out by [introducing as part of the team / past purchases / the sign], a representation by the principals themselves, unlike Essington v Regency. / [A]’s own claim that [“I am authorised…”] cannot create authority (Essington).
Notice: no evidence [3P] knew of [the internal rule], which binds only the partners. / [A] told [3P] [he still needed B and C’s approval], so [3P] was on notice and did not rely in good faith (Crouch v IPG).
C: On the balance of probabilities, [A] [had / did not have] apparent authority, so [the firm and partners] [are / are not] bound to pay [3P] for [item].
I4 — Agent personally liable (no authority)
Issue: Can [3P] hold [A] personally liable for breach of warranty of authority?
Law: A liable if (1) A claimed to contract on behalf of P; (2) A had no authority; (3) 3P relied and wouldn’t have contracted otherwise — Oliana Foods v Culinary Co [2020]. Also possibly deceit, negligence, s 18 ACL.
A: signed “on behalf of [firm]” / used firm card → (1); Issue 2 → (2); [3P] wouldn’t have [built $180k stage] for [A] personally → (3). Against: A disclosed he needed approval → no reliance.
Agency of necessity (only if emergency): A has P’s property · property under threat · can’t contact P · good faith. Springer v GWR [1921] — failed: could have contacted P.

Capacity: P needs full capacity; A needn’t (even a minor).
Outcome: privity between P and 3P if agency disclosed + A within actual/apparent authority; A not party, not liable.
P liable for A’s torts? Employee → likely vicarious (within instructions); independent contractor → unlikely.
Fiduciary duties — agent → principal
Law: Agents owe fiduciary duties to P from the relationship of trust and confidence; often also contract terms.
  • Follow lawful instructions — Bertram Armstrong v Godfray (1830)
  • Due care, skill, diligence (else negligence) — Provincial Insurance v Consolidated Wood (1991)
  • Act in person (no sub-agent) — John McCann v Pow (1975) (agent appointed another agent to sell → breach)
  • Act in P’s interests; no secret profit/commission/bribe — Hewson v Sydney (1968)
  • Communicate relevant info — John D Wood v Knatchbull (2002)
  • Confidentiality · keep accounts, keep P’s money separate
P’s duties to A: pay only if agreed · indemnify/reimburse expenses within instructions · A has lien if unpaid.
Fiduciary duties — partners ↔ partners
  • No personal profit from position/info — Chan v Zaccharia (1984)
  • No undisclosed conflict
  • Full disclosure — Law v Law (1905); s 31 PA
  • Account for private profits — Birtchnell v Equity (1929); s 32 PA
  • Don’t compete with firm — s 33 PA
Remedies lines
Authority → [3P] may enforce against [P / firm] for [$X]. Partners are jointly and personally liable (Kendall v Hamilton); a partnership is not a separate legal entity (though it may be sued), so [3P] can recover from [A, B and C]’s personal assets.
No authority → [P] not bound; may return goods / recover payment. [3P] claims damages from [A] personally (breach of warranty of authority) — up to [$ balance], less what it recovers by reselling returned goods.
Internal → [P / partners] may sue [A] for breach of fiduciary duty / contract of agency for [loss / deposit]; no indemnity for unauthorised acts; no pay unless agreed.

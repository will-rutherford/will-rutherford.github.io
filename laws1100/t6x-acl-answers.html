The paragraph for each pattern (copy, then fill the blanks)

P1 Consumer (s 3). Under s 3 of the ACL, a person acquires goods or services as a "consumer" if the amount paid was not more than $100,000, or the goods or services were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption, or the goods were a vehicle or trailer acquired principally for transporting goods on public roads. The definition is wide enough to include businesses, to the extent they are not buying products for resale: Star Kingdom Investments v Camatic [2024] FCA 329. Here, [buyer] paid [$X], which is below the $100,000 threshold, so [he/she] is a consumer even though [it was bought for a business], because [it was for use in the business, not for resale].
P2 s 18 Law. Section 18(1) of the ACL provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". There are four requirements. (1) A "person" includes individuals and corporations. (2) "Trade or commerce" means trade or commerce within Australia or between Australia and places outside Australia, and includes any business or professional activity, whether or not for profit: s 2(1); Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978). (3) "Engaging in conduct" includes doing or refusing to do an act, including making a statement or claim: s 2(2); silence can be conduct where it creates or maintains a misleading impression: Henjo Investments v Collins (1988). (4) To mislead is to lead into error; to deceive is to cause a person to believe something false: Weitmann v Katies (1977). The test is objective; intention or fault is irrelevant, and it is not necessary to prove anyone was actually misled. In Taco v Taco Bell (1982), where the US Taco Bell's use of the same name as an existing Sydney restaurant was held misleading, the Court set two tests: first identify the relevant section of the public at whom the conduct was directed; then consider the effect on all who come within it, "including the astute and the gullible". Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979) adds that the test is the effect on a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed", but not someone "quite unusually stupid". Only a small percentage of the audience needs to be misled: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010). Fine print or disclaimers will not necessarily correct a misleading impression: ACCC v Telstra (2007).
P3 Puffery. A statement that is not literally true is not necessarily misleading — mere sales "puff" (vague, exaggerated praise no reasonable person takes literally) is generally not misleading. However, exaggeration may be misleading where the advertisement as a whole conveys a misleading impression: Given v Pryor (1980), and a statement that is literally true can still mislead through its overall impression: Henderson v Pioneer Homes (1980). Mere confusion is not enough: McWilliam's Wines v McDonald's (1980). In Optus v Telstra [2018] FCA 745, Telstra's national campaign promoting "unlimited" internet was held to breach ss 18 and 29 even though Telstra argued the word, in context, conveyed "nothing definitive"; a specific word can carry a definite meaning to the audience. Apply: "[best / revolutionary]" is vague, subjective praise that even the less-than-average-intelligence viewer (Annand) would see as sales talk — likely puffery. By contrast, "[specific measurable claim]" is a specific, checkable claim about [quality/composition], like "unlimited" in Optus v Telstra, and is likely misleading.
P4 Silence. "Engaging in conduct" includes refusing to do an act (s 2(2)), and silence can amount to misleading conduct where, in the circumstances, failing to disclose information creates or maintains a misleading impression: Henjo Investments v Collins (1988). Here [seller] knew [the defect / unsuitability] and, when [buyer] [asked / stated their purpose], [changed the subject / said nothing]. That silence maintained the impression that [product was suitable], so it is conduct capable of breaching s 18.
P5 s 29 + Egg case. Section 29 prohibits a person, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or promotion of goods or services, from making false or misleading representations, including that goods are of a particular standard, quality, value, grade, composition, style or model (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)), that services are of a particular standard or quality (s 29(1)(b)), or that goods or services have particular performance characteristics, uses or benefits (s 29(1)(g); Hartnell v Sharp (1975)). There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" (s 29) and "misleading or deceptive" (s 18): ACCC v Coles Supermarkets [2014] FCA 634 at [40]. In ACCC v C.I. & Co Pty Ltd [2010] FCA 1511 (the Egg case), cage eggs were sold labelled "free range" and later "Fresh Range"; the conduct was misleading and falsely represented their quality, and North J called it a "cruel deception" with a "high level of dishonesty" because consumers had no practical way to detect it. Similarly, in ACCC v Reckitt Benckiser, Nurofen "specific pain" products were identical to standard Nurofen but sold at a premium. Apply: like the eggs, [buyers] cannot check [protein content / how chickens were raised] for themselves, so they rely entirely on the label.
P6 Future matters (s 4). Under s 4 of the ACL, if a person makes a representation about a future matter and does not have reasonable grounds for making it, the representation is taken to be misleading: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010). "[guaranteed to … / will …]" is a promise about the future. [Seller] had no reasonable grounds because [facts — e.g. product is 95% sugar / staff had no training / knew of reported faults], so it is deemed misleading.
P7 s 55 disclosed purpose. Under s 55(1), where goods are supplied in trade or commerce to a consumer, there is a guarantee that they are reasonably fit for any disclosed purpose and for any purpose for which the supplier represents they are reasonably fit. A disclosed purpose is a particular purpose (whether or not a purpose for which the goods are commonly supplied) which the consumer makes known, expressly or by implication, to the supplier or to a person by whom any prior negotiations were conducted (eg an agent), or to the manufacturer: s 55(2). The guarantee does not apply if the circumstances show the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable to rely, on the skill or judgment of the supplier. (Section 61 is the equivalent for services.) Apply: [buyer] expressly told [staff] they needed it for [purpose] and [made clear they were relying on the advice]. It was reasonable to rely on [a staff member in uniform / the Senior Sales Manager]. The goods [failed within weeks in exactly that use], so the s 55 guarantee was not met.
P8 s 54 acceptable quality. Under s 54, in every supply of goods to a consumer in trade or commerce (other than by auction), there is a guarantee that the goods are of acceptable quality: fit for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied, acceptable in appearance and finish, free from defects, safe and durable, as a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the state and condition of the goods (including any hidden defects) would regard as acceptable: Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas Pty Ltd [2015] VCAT 2044; Mitchell v Oz Design Furniture Pty Ltd [2016] VCAT 108. Apply: a reasonable consumer who knew that [the $X product would fail after weeks / had a design flaw] would not regard it as acceptable — it is neither free from defects nor durable.
P9 s 64. Section 64 of the ACL provides that any term of a contract that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify the consumer guarantees is automatically void; a business cannot contract out of its obligations to consumers. Further, fine print or information provided elsewhere will not necessarily correct a misleading impression: ACCC v Telstra (2007). Apply: "[warranty void / not liable for any delay]" is void under s 64 to the extent it would stop [buyer] relying on [s 54 / s 55 / s 60 / s 61]; and a limitation buried in [the manual] that [buyer] never saw cannot undo what [staff] said at the point of sale.
P10 Unfair terms Law. A term of a contract is unfair and therefore void under s 23 of the ACL if (1) the contract is a consumer contract or small business contract, (2) it is a standard form contract, and (3) the term is unfair. A consumer contract is a contract for the supply of goods, services or an interest in land to an individual whose acquisition is wholly or predominantly for personal, domestic or household use: s 23(3) (narrower than "consumer" in s 3). A standard form contract is one not individually negotiated — the same standard terms for all customers: s 27 (s 27(2) lists the matters the court considers). Under s 24(1) a term is unfair if it (a) would cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations, (b) is not reasonably necessary to protect the legitimate interests of the advantaged party, and (c) would cause detriment (financial or otherwise) to a party if applied or relied on. The court must consider the extent to which the term is transparent — expressed in reasonably plain language, legible, presented clearly and readily available (s 24(2)(a), (3)) — and the contract as a whole (s 24(2)(b)). Section 25 gives examples of unfair terms, including terms that permit one party (but not the other) to avoid or limit performance, to terminate the contract (Director v AAPT (2006)), to penalise the other party for a breach, to vary the terms (Director v Trainstation (2006)), or to limit one party's right to sue the other.
P11 Unconscionable Law. Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Section 20 prohibits conduct, in trade or commerce, that is unconscionable within the meaning of the unwritten law — the equitable doctrine in Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio, which requires that (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage so there was no real equality between the parties, (2) the stronger party knew or should have known of it, and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it. Kitto J in Blomley v Ryan (1956) described special disadvantage as arising from "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties" affecting a person's ability to conserve their own interests. Section 20 does not apply where conduct falls within s 21. Section 21 prohibits a person, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply of goods or services to a person (other than a listed public company), from engaging in conduct that is, in all the circumstances, unconscionable. The court may consider the terms of the contract and the manner in which and extent to which it is carried out, not only its formation: s 21(4)(c). Section 22 lists matters the court may consider — both procedural unconscionability (inequality of bargaining power from age, infirmity or illiteracy; undue pressure and unfair tactics: s 22(1)(a), (c), (d)) and substantive unconscionability (harsh, one-sided terms, exclusion clauses or excessive payment clauses: s 22(1)(b), (j), (k)). No single factor is determinative. In ACCC v Coles [2014] FCA, Coles breached s 21 by misusing its bargaining power against suppliers in a way that was "serious, deliberate and repeated".
P12 s 36 Law. Under s 36 of the ACL, a person must not accept payment for goods or services where it does not intend to supply them, or knows or should know that it will be unable to supply them within the specified time or a reasonable time: Dawson v World Travel (1981). The ACCC took Ozdirect to the Federal Court for accepting payment for electronics it knew or ought reasonably to have known it could not supply within a reasonable time. (If no time for supply of services was fixed, s 62 also guarantees supply within a reasonable time.)
P13 Competitor standing. Although ss 18 and 29 sit in the "consumer protection" part of the ACL, they are not confined to protecting consumers. Section 18 may be relied on in disputes between consumers, businesses and competitors, and many significant s 18 cases involve competitors suing each other: Gillette v Energiser (2002); s 29 may also be used by competitors. In Taco v Taco Bell it was the existing Sydney restaurant that sued its rival. The consumer guarantees in ss 54–56 are not relevant, because [claimant] did not acquire any goods as a consumer.
P14 Staff statements. A "person" includes a corporation, and a business engages in conduct through its staff when they deal with customers on its behalf. Section 55(2)(a) expressly treats a purpose disclosed to "a person by whom any prior negotiations or arrangements … were conducted" (eg an agent) as disclosed to the supplier. [Staff member] was [wearing the store's uniform / the Senior Sales Manager / the company's sales representative] and dealt with [buyer] on the shop floor, so [business] cannot escape liability by saying [he] was untrained or unauthorised (see also apparent authority on T8).
P15 Intention. Intention or fault is irrelevant under s 18, so it does not matter whether [business] meant to mislead. However, deliberate conduct is highly relevant to remedies: in the Egg case the deliberate, hard-to-detect mislabelling was described as a "high level of dishonesty", and the penalty was set to punish and deter the industry. [Management's approval because "consumers would not be able to tell"] places this case squarely within that reasoning and points to a substantial pecuniary penalty (ss 224–231).

Q1 · 2024 S1 Q1 — Ben v Brisbane Earth Works 25 marks

Question text (click)

During the Christmas holidays, Ben wishes to build a fence along the back boundary of his property. To complete this work, he decides to hire a mini excavator to help with the digging of the post holes.

Ben searches online and makes a hire booking for a mini excavator through 'Brisbane Earth Works' (BEW). As part of the online booking, Ben arranges to collect the machine at 12pm on Tuesday 20th December and to return it by 12pm on Friday 30th December. He pays a $1,000 deposit to BEW after reading through the online terms and conditions of the hire agreement.

At 12pm on the 20th December, Ben arrives at BEW. The manager of BEW tells Ben: "We are still waiting for your excavator to arrive back from being serviced at our workshop. Due to being short-staffed, they are taking longer than usual to return our machinery. It should be here by late afternoon." Eventually, Ben collects the excavator at 5pm, after a lengthy wait.

On the 30th December, Ben is late returning the excavator to BEW due to heavy rain and localized flooding. He eventually arrives at BEW at 2pm (2 hours later than agreed). Upon arrival, Ben is informed by the manager of BEW that his $1,000 deposit is forfeited. The manager refers Ben to the following terms in the online agreement:

10. BEW will make all reasonable efforts to ensure that their hire equipment is available for collection by the customer at the agreed time. However, BEW will not be held liable for any delay that may occur, however that delay may arise.
11. The customer must return any hired equipment to BEW by no later than the agreed upon time as written into the hire agreement. Any failure to comply with this, for whatever reason, will result in a complete forfeiture of the deposit price.

Advise Ben as to whether he has any claims against BEW under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL)? (Note: you must only look at the ACL provisions.) Please use ILAC to answer this question.

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Opener + preliminary: Ben is a "consumer" (s 3) and the hire is a consumer contract (s 23(3)).
  2. Issue 1 — Clause 11 (forfeit whole deposit for any lateness) is an unfair term: ss 23, 24, 25, 27 → void.
  3. Issue 2 — Clause 10 ("not liable for any delay") is void under s 64 (excludes s 60 due care and skill) and also unfair (s 25 limit performance / limit right to sue).
  4. Issue 3 — Is enforcing the forfeiture unconscionable under s 21 (s 21(4)(c), s 22)? Arguable but weaker.
  5. Issue 4 — Did BEW wrongly accept payment / mislead about availability (s 36, s 18 + s 4)? Unlikely on these facts.
  6. Remedies: term declared void, refund of $1,000 (ss 237–241), damages for the 5-hour delay if loss proved (s 236).

Whether Ben has claims against BEW under the ACL depends on four issues: (1) whether clause 11 is an unfair term; (2) whether clause 10 can exclude BEW's liability for the delay; (3) whether BEW's conduct is unconscionable; and (4) whether BEW wrongly accepted payment or misled Ben. First, it must be established that Ben is protected by the ACL.

Preliminary: is Ben a consumer?

The ACL is a Schedule to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth). Under s 3, a person acquires goods or services as a "consumer" if the amount paid was not more than $100,000, or they were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption. Ben hired the excavator to build a fence at his own home during the holidays. The question does not state the total hire price, but the deposit was $1,000 and a 10-day hire of a mini excavator will clearly be far below $100,000. Ben is therefore a consumer, and he acquired the hire wholly for personal or household use.

Issue 1: Clause 11 — unfair term

Issue

Can Ben have clause 11 declared void as an unfair term under s 23 of the ACL?

Law

Under s 23 of the ACL, a term of a contract is unfair and therefore void if (1) the contract is a consumer contract or small business contract, (2) the contract is a standard form contract, and (3) the term is unfair.

A consumer contract is a contract for the supply of goods, services or an interest in land to an individual whose acquisition is wholly or predominantly for personal, domestic or household use or consumption: s 23(3). This is narrower than the s 3 definition of "consumer". A standard form contract is one that is not individually negotiated — the same standard terms are used for all customers: s 27. Section 27(2) sets out the matters the court considers.

Under s 24(1), a term is unfair if: (a) it would cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations under the contract (significant, not minor or trivial); (b) it is not reasonably necessary to protect the legitimate interests of the party advantaged by the term; and (c) it would cause detriment, financial or otherwise (including inconvenience, delay or distress), to a party if applied or relied on. In deciding this, the court must take into account the extent to which the term is transparent and the contract as a whole: s 24(2). A term is transparent if it is expressed in reasonably plain language, legible, presented clearly and readily available to the affected party: s 24(3).

Section 25 gives a non-exhaustive list of examples of unfair terms — terms that allow one party to do something but not the other — including terms permitting one party to avoid or limit performance of the contract, to terminate the contract (Director v AAPT (2006)), to penalise the other party for a breach, to vary the terms (Director v Trainstation (2006)), and to limit one party's right to sue the other.

Application

Consumer contract. The hire agreement is a contract for the supply of goods (the excavator) and associated services to Ben, an individual, for building a fence at his home. It is wholly for personal, domestic or household use, so it is a consumer contract under s 23(3).

Standard form contract. Ben booked online and accepted BEW's "online terms and conditions". There is no suggestion that any term was negotiated; the same online terms would apply to every customer who books. Applying s 27, it is a standard form contract.

Significant imbalance. Clause 11 requires a "complete forfeiture" of the $1,000 deposit for "any failure … for whatever reason" to return the equipment on time — even by minutes and even where the delay is caused by events outside Ben's control, such as the heavy rain and localised flooding here. By contrast, clause 10 means BEW bears no consequence at all when it is late: Ben waited five hours for the excavator, yet BEW claims it "will not be held liable for any delay … however that delay may arise". The parties' obligations about time are therefore completely one-sided. This is precisely the kind of term identified in s 25: it penalises the consumer for a breach while BEW is free to limit its own performance. The imbalance is significant rather than trivial, because the whole deposit is lost for a two-hour delay at the end of a 10-day hire.

Reasonably necessary to protect legitimate interests? BEW will argue that it has a legitimate interest in having its machinery returned on time so it can be serviced and hired to the next customer, and that a forfeiture clause encourages customers to be punctual. That interest is legitimate. However, the clause goes well beyond what is reasonably necessary to protect it. There is no evidence that BEW suffered any loss from the two-hour delay or that another hire was affected. An hourly late fee, or forfeiture of an amount reflecting BEW's actual loss, would fully protect BEW. A forfeiture of the entire $1,000 regardless of the reason, the length of the delay or any loss to BEW operates as a penalty rather than protection.

Detriment. If the clause is relied on, Ben suffers clear financial detriment: he loses $1,000.

Transparency and the contract as a whole (s 24(2)). BEW's strongest argument is that the term is transparent: it is expressed in plain language, it was readily available online, and Ben read the terms before paying. Under the rationale of s 24(2), if a consumer can understand a term, he could have objected or walked away. However, transparency is only one matter the court must consider; a transparent term can still be unfair. Looking at the contract as a whole, clause 11 sits directly alongside clause 10, which excuses BEW from all liability for its own delay. Read together, the two clauses show that the contract places all of the risk of delay on the consumer.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, clause 11 is an unfair term in a standard form consumer contract and is void under s 23, so BEW cannot rely on it to keep Ben's $1,000 deposit.

Issue 2: Clause 10 — exclusion of liability for BEW's delay

Issue

Can BEW rely on clause 10 to avoid liability to Ben for the five-hour delay under the ACL?

Law

The ACL implies consumer guarantees into consumer contracts. These include that services will be rendered with due care and skill (s 60), that services will be fit for any disclosed purpose (s 61), and that services will be supplied within a reasonable time if no time for supply was fixed by the contract (s 62). Under s 64, any term of a contract between a business and a consumer that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify the guarantees is automatically void; this ensures that a business cannot avoid its legal obligations to consumers by a term in its contract. Clause 10 may also be tested as an unfair term under ss 23–25, as in Issue 1: s 25 lists terms permitting one party to avoid or limit performance and terms limiting a party's right to sue.

Application

Guarantees. BEW supplied a hire service to Ben, including making the excavator available at the time agreed. Section 62 does not strictly apply because a collection time of 12pm was fixed by the contract. However, s 60 requires BEW to render its services with due care and skill. BEW knew the excavator was being serviced, and the manager explained the delay was "due to being short-staffed". A business exercising due care and skill would schedule its servicing and staffing so that equipment already booked and paid for is ready at the agreed time, or would at least contact the customer in advance. Ben was left waiting five hours. It is therefore arguable that s 60 was not met.

Effect of s 64. Clause 10 states BEW "will not be held liable for any delay that may occur, however that delay may arise". That is a term that seeks to exclude or restrict BEW's liability, including liability under the s 60 guarantee. To that extent, clause 10 is automatically void under s 64. BEW cannot contract out of the guarantee.

Unfair term. Alternatively, the same analysis as in Issue 1 applies. Clause 10 lets BEW limit its own performance (the agreed time) and limits Ben's right to sue for its delay — both s 25 examples — while clause 11 punishes Ben severely for his delay. This is a significant imbalance; excluding liability for delays "however [they] may arise", including BEW's own understaffing, is not reasonably necessary to protect any legitimate interest of BEW; and it causes Ben detriment in the form of a lost half-day of a 10-day hire, inconvenience and delay. Although BEW will again point to the clause's plain language and Ben's reading of it, the contract as a whole is one-sided.

Conclusion

BEW is unlikely to be able to rely on clause 10: it is void under s 64 to the extent it excludes the s 60 guarantee, and it is likely void as an unfair term under s 23.

Issue 3: Unconscionable conduct

Issue

Has BEW engaged in unconscionable conduct towards Ben in breach of s 21 of the ACL?

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience. Section 21 provides that a person must not, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply of goods or services to a person (other than a listed public company), engage in conduct that is, in all the circumstances, unconscionable. Section 20, which adopts the unwritten law (Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio), does not apply where s 21 applies. Under s 21(4)(c), the court may consider the terms of the contract and the manner in which and extent to which it is carried out. Section 22 lists factors covering both procedural unconscionability (eg inequality of bargaining power, unfair tactics) and substantive unconscionability (eg harsh or one-sided terms, excessive payment clauses). No single factor is determinative. In ACCC v Coles [2014] FCA, Coles' misuse of its bargaining power against suppliers was unconscionable because it was "serious, deliberate and repeated".

Application

BEW acted in trade or commerce in connection with the supply of hire goods and services to Ben, who is not a listed public company. In Ben's favour, the terms are substantively one-sided (s 22: excessive payment and exclusion clauses advantaging the stronger party), and the manner in which BEW carried out the contract (s 21(4)(c)) was harsh: it delivered five hours late without consequence, yet immediately forfeited the whole deposit when Ben was two hours late because of flooding. Against Ben, there is little procedural unconscionability. Ben is not elderly, ill or otherwise at a special disadvantage; he read the terms before paying and could have hired elsewhere. Unlike ACCC v Coles, there is no evidence that BEW's conduct was repeated or part of a deliberate pattern.

Conclusion

A court is unlikely to find BEW's conduct unconscionable under s 21; Ben's stronger claims are under the unfair terms provisions and s 64.

Issue 4: Accepting payment and representations about availability

Issue

Did BEW wrongly accept Ben's payment under s 36, or mislead him under s 18, in relation to the availability of the excavator?

Law

Under s 36, a person must not accept payment for goods or services if it does not intend to supply them, or knows or should know it will be unable to supply them within the specified time: Dawson v World Travel (1981); Ozdirect. Under s 18, a person must not in trade or commerce engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead or deceive; and under s 4 a representation about a future matter is taken to be misleading if the person had no reasonable grounds for making it (ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010)).

Application

By confirming a 12pm collection, BEW represented that the excavator would be ready at that time — a representation about a future matter. If, when it took the $1,000 deposit, BEW already knew its workshop was short-staffed and running late, it arguably lacked reasonable grounds (s 4) and should have known it could not supply at the specified time (s 36). However, the facts do not say when the booking was made or when BEW became aware of the delay, and BEW did supply the excavator on the same day. Unlike Ozdirect, where customers waited weeks and received nothing, the delay here was five hours.

Conclusion

On the facts given, a claim under s 36 or s 18 is unlikely to succeed, unless evidence shows that BEW knew of the delay when it accepted payment.

Remedies

Ben should seek a declaration that clauses 10 and 11 are void (s 23; s 64) and an order that BEW refund his $1,000 deposit (ss 237–241). If he can show loss caused by the five-hour delay — for example extra labour costs or lost working time — he may claim damages under s 236 for BEW's failure to comply with the s 60 guarantee. He may also report BEW to the ACCC, which can seek injunctions (ss 232–235) to stop BEW using the terms with other customers.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Ben is entitled to the return of his $1,000 deposit because clause 11 is an unfair term and void, and BEW cannot rely on clause 10 to exclude liability for its own delay.

Q2 · 2023 S1 Q1 — John v Spectra Ltd 25 marks

Question text (click)

Spectra Ltd is one of the few companies in Australia that provides high-speed internet services to regional Queensland. John is an 84-year-old man who lives by himself in the small town of Warwick. One day he is approached by a door-to-door salesman named Nick, who asks him about his current internet connection.

John tells Nick that his internet frequently cuts out during video calls with his family in Victoria. Nick then tries to talk John into signing a standard four-year contract with Spectra internet for $120 a month. He hands John a 30-page agreement containing all the contractual terms.

Clause 15(i) states that John cannot terminate the contract for any reason unless he pays a $2,000 exit fee. Clause 20(ii) states that Spectra can terminate the contract for any reason without penalty. Furthermore, Clause 51 states that Spectra will not be liable if they are unable to provide their service for a period of time, for any reason beyond their control.

John tells Nick that he cannot read the fine print in the agreement due to poor eyesight and that his 21-year-old grandson is usually responsible for managing his financial affairs. Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Preliminary: John is a consumer (s 3); contract is a standard form consumer contract (ss 23(3), 27).
  2. Issue 1 — Unconscionable conduct in getting John to sign (ss 20–22; Amadio; Blomley v Ryan) → strongest claim → rescission.
  3. Issue 2 — Nick's promises ("solve all his problems", "uninterrupted Zoom calls"): s 18, s 29(1)(b), (g), s 4; puffery argument.
  4. Issue 3 — Clauses 15(i), 20(ii), 51 unfair terms (ss 23–25, 27; Director v AAPT).
  5. Issue 4 — Guarantees (s 61 disclosed purpose, s 62 reasonable time) + s 64 voids cl 51; s 36 demanding $120/month for a service it cannot supply.
  6. Remedies: rescission / contract void, refund of $1,000 + payments, no exit fee; injunction; ACCC penalties.

John's options under the ACL depend on five issues: (1) whether Spectra, through Nick, engaged in unconscionable conduct; (2) whether Nick's statements were misleading or false representations; (3) whether clauses 15(i), 20(ii) and 51 are unfair terms; (4) whether Spectra has failed the consumer guarantees; and (5) whether Spectra is wrongly accepting payment.

Preliminary: is John a consumer?

Under s 3 of the ACL, a person acquires services as a consumer if the amount paid is not more than $100,000 or the services are of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use. John's contract is worth $120 × 48 months = $5,760, plus a $1,000 deposit, and home internet for family video calls is ordinarily acquired for personal use. John is a consumer.

Issue 1: Unconscionable conduct

Issue

Can John have the contract set aside because Spectra engaged in unconscionable conduct in breach of s 21 of the ACL?

Law

Unconscionable conduct is conduct that is unfair, unjust and against good conscience; a person engages in it when they unfairly take advantage of a superior bargaining position or of another person's weakness or disadvantage. Part 2-2 of the ACL prohibits it. Section 20 prohibits conduct in trade or commerce that is unconscionable within the meaning of the unwritten law, as developed in Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio. Under Amadio, a contract may be set aside where (1) the weaker party was under a special disability or disadvantage so there was no real equality between the parties, (2) the stronger party knew or should have known of it, and (3) the stronger party took unfair advantage of it. Kitto J in Blomley v Ryan (1956) explained that special disadvantage arises where "illness, ignorance, impaired faculties" affect a person's ability to conserve their own interests.

Section 20 does not apply where conduct falls within s 21. Section 21 provides that a person must not, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or possible supply of goods or services to a person (other than a listed public company), engage in conduct that is, in all the circumstances, unconscionable. The court may consider the terms of the contract and the manner in which it is carried out, not only its formation: s 21(4)(c). Section 21 does not define "unconscionable", but s 22 lists matters the court may consider, covering procedural unconscionability — inequality of bargaining power from factors like age, infirmity or illiteracy; undue pressure or unfair tactics (s 22(1)(a), (c), (d)) — and substantive unconscionability — harsh, one-sided terms, exclusion clauses and excessive payment clauses favouring the stronger party (s 22(1)(b), (j), (k)). No single factor is determinative. In ACCC v Coles [2014] FCA, Coles breached s 21 by misusing its bargaining power over suppliers in a way that was "serious, deliberate and repeated" and inconsistent with standard business practice.

Application

Preliminary elements. Spectra, a company, acted through its salesman Nick, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply of internet services to John, who is not a listed public company.

Procedural unconscionability — special disadvantage. John is 84, lives alone in a small country town, has poor eyesight so cannot read the fine print, and relies on his grandson to manage his financial affairs. These are the "impaired faculties" and age-related inequality described in Blomley v Ryan and s 22(1)(a). There was no real equality between an elderly man alone at his front door and a trained salesman for one of the few high-speed providers in regional Queensland.

Knowledge. Spectra cannot argue that it was unaware of John's disadvantage. John expressly told Nick he could not read the fine print and that his grandson usually manages his financial affairs. As in Amadio, where the stronger party knew of the weaker party's position, Nick had actual knowledge.

Unfair advantage and tactics. Rather than giving John time to have his grandson read the 30-page agreement, Nick pressed him with a 10% discount available only "if John signs today". That is the kind of pressure and unfair tactic identified in s 22(1)(c) and (d). Nick also gave sweeping assurances ("Spectra will solve all his problems") in place of explaining the terms.

Substantive unconscionability. The terms themselves are one-sided (s 22(1)(b), (j), (k)): John is locked in for four years and must pay a $2,000 exit fee, while Spectra may terminate at any time without penalty and excludes its own liability for non-supply. In carrying out the contract (s 21(4)(c)), Spectra now insists that John keep paying $120 a month for a service it cannot supply for up to eight months.

Spectra's arguments. Spectra may argue that John voluntarily signed, that the contract was a "standard" contract offered to all, and that the tornado was beyond its control. However, the question is whether the conduct was unconscionable "in all the circumstances", and the circumstances of the sale are strongly against Spectra. Unlike ACCC v Coles, there is no evidence that this conduct was repeated across many customers, but s 21 does not require repetition.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Spectra engaged in unconscionable conduct in breach of s 21, and John can seek to have the contract set aside.

Issue 2: Nick's statements — misleading conduct and false representations

Issue

Did Nick's assurances that Spectra would "solve all his problems" and allow "uninterrupted Zoom calls" breach ss 18 and 29 of the ACL?

Law

Section 18(1) provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". "Person" includes corporations; "trade or commerce" includes any business activity (s 2(1); Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978)); "engaging in conduct" includes making a statement or promise (s 2(2)). To mislead is to lead into error (Weitmann v Katies (1977)). The test is objective and intention is irrelevant. Under Taco v Taco Bell (1982), the court identifies the relevant audience and considers its effect on all within it, including the astute and the gullible; Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979) tests the effect on a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed". Mere puffery is generally not misleading, but exaggeration may mislead where the overall impression is misleading (Given v Pryor (1980)). Under s 4, a representation about a future matter is taken to be misleading if the person had no reasonable grounds for making it: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010). Section 29 prohibits false or misleading representations in connection with the supply of services, including that services are of a particular standard or quality (s 29(1)(b)) or have particular performance characteristics or benefits (s 29(1)(g)). There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" and "misleading or deceptive": ACCC v Coles Supermarkets [2014] FCA 634.

Application

Nick, on Spectra's behalf, engaged in conduct in trade or commerce by making statements to John in the course of selling Spectra's services. Here the "audience" is a single consumer, but applying the Taco Bell and Annand approach, the statements must be judged by their likely effect on someone in John's position — an elderly person who is not well-informed about internet technology.

Spectra will argue that "Spectra will solve all his problems" is mere puffery: vague sales talk that no one would take literally. That argument has some force for "all his problems". However, "uninterrupted Zoom calls" is a specific promise about the performance of the service, given directly in response to John's complaint that his calls cut out. It is a representation that the service would have particular performance characteristics (s 29(1)(g)) and be of a particular standard (s 29(1)(b)). It is also a representation about a future matter under s 4. No internet service can guarantee that calls will never be interrupted, so it is doubtful Nick had reasonable grounds for it. Like the advertisement in Given v Pryor, the overall impression conveyed to John was that signing would fix his video calls.

However, the reason John received no service was the tornado, not a flaw in the service itself. This may limit the loss John can show was caused by the representation.

Conclusion

It is likely that the "uninterrupted Zoom calls" assurance breached ss 18 and 29(1)(b) and (g), although "solve all his problems" alone is probably puffery.

Issue 3: Unfair terms — clauses 15(i), 20(ii) and 51

Issue

Are clauses 15(i), 20(ii) and 51 unfair terms that are void under s 23 of the ACL?

Law

Under s 23, a term is unfair and therefore void if the contract is a consumer contract or small business contract, it is a standard form contract, and the term is unfair. A consumer contract is a contract for the supply of goods or services to an individual whose acquisition is wholly or predominantly for personal, domestic or household use: s 23(3). A standard form contract is one not individually negotiated: s 27. Under s 24(1), a term is unfair if it would cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations, is not reasonably necessary to protect the legitimate interests of the party advantaged, and would cause detriment to a party if relied on. The court must consider transparency — whether the term is in plain language, legible, presented clearly and readily available (s 24(2)(a), (3)) — and the contract as a whole (s 24(2)(b)). Section 25 lists examples, including terms permitting one party (but not the other) to terminate the contract (Director v AAPT (2006)), to penalise the other for a breach, and to avoid or limit performance.

Application

John is an individual acquiring home internet for personal use, so this is a consumer contract (s 23(3)). The question describes it as a "standard four-year contract" handed over as a 30-page pre-printed document; nothing was negotiated, so it is a standard form contract (s 27).

Clauses 15(i) and 20(ii). Read together, John cannot terminate "for any reason" without paying $2,000, while Spectra can terminate "for any reason without penalty". This is the textbook s 25 example of a term allowing one party but not the other to terminate, as in Director v AAPT. The imbalance is significant. Spectra may argue that an exit fee protects its legitimate interest in recovering installation costs over a four-year term; but a $2,000 fee payable even where Spectra itself cannot supply the service is not reasonably necessary to protect that interest, and Spectra's unrestricted right to walk away protects no legitimate interest of John's. The detriment to John is $2,000 or four years of payments.

Clause 51. Clause 51 allows Spectra to avoid performance — for "a period of time" with no limit — for any reason beyond its control, while John must keep paying. This is a s 25 term permitting one party to avoid or limit performance. Spectra has a legitimate interest in not being liable for genuine disasters such as a tornado, so this clause is the most arguable. However, relying on it to excuse eight months of non-supply while still charging John causes significant imbalance and detriment.

Transparency. All three terms were buried in a 30-page agreement that John told Nick he could not read. They were neither legible nor readily available to him in any practical sense (s 24(3)), which strongly supports a finding of unfairness.

Conclusion

Clauses 15(i) and 20(ii) are very likely unfair and void; clause 51 is likely void at least to the extent Spectra uses it to keep charging John while supplying nothing.

Issue 4: Consumer guarantees, s 64 and s 36

Issue

Can Spectra rely on clause 51 to avoid liability for failing to supply the service, and is it entitled to keep demanding payment, under the ACL?

Law

The ACL guarantees that services will be fit for any purpose disclosed by the consumer and of such a nature that they would reasonably be expected to achieve the result the consumer disclosed (s 61), and, if no time for supply was fixed, that they will be supplied within a reasonable time (s 62). Under s 64, any term that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify the guarantees is automatically void. Under s 36, a person must not accept payment for goods or services where it knows or should know that it will be unable to supply them within the specified time or a reasonable time: Dawson v World Travel (1981); in Ozdirect the ACCC sued a retailer that took payment for goods it ought reasonably to have known it could not supply in time. Defences exist where a contravention was due to a cause beyond the business's control and it took reasonable precautions and exercised due diligence: s 208.

Application

John disclosed to Nick the particular purpose of the service — reliable video calls with his family (s 61). No installation date was fixed in the facts, so s 62 requires supply within a reasonable time; a delay of up to eight months for a monthly subscription service is unlikely to be reasonable. Clause 51 is void under s 64 to the extent it seeks to exclude these guarantees. Spectra will argue that the tornado was a cause beyond its control (s 208). That may excuse the original failure to install, but it does not justify the next step: once Spectra knew it could not supply for up to eight months, continuing to demand $120 a month means it is accepting payment for services it knows it cannot supply within a reasonable time, in breach of s 36, like Ozdirect.

Conclusion

Spectra cannot rely on clause 51 to exclude the guarantees, and demanding ongoing payments for a service it cannot supply likely breaches s 36.

Remedies

John's best course is to seek rescission of the contract for unconscionable conduct, or an order declaring it void, which would restore him to his original position: the return of his $1,000 deposit and any monthly payments made, with no $2,000 exit fee. Alternatively, the court may declare clauses 15(i), 20(ii) and 51 void (s 23) and order a refund (ss 237–241). He may claim damages for any loss caused by the contraventions (s 236), seek an injunction (ss 232–235), and report Spectra to the ACCC, which may seek pecuniary penalties (ss 224–231). As John's grandson manages his affairs, he should assist John in bringing the claim.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, John can have the contract set aside under s 21 and recover his deposit without paying the exit fee; Spectra cannot rely on its one-sided terms or clause 51.

Q3 · 2022 S2 Q2 — Vincent v Bruce (Go-Pro-Bars) 25 marks

Question text (click)

Bruce owns and manages a fitness store called 'Go-Pro'. He recently began manufacturing a new protein bar which he called 'Go-Pro-Bars'. 'Go-Pro-Bars' are sold in packets of 10 for $59.95 per packet. Fitness protein bars are designed to be eaten by people who exercise regularly, to help them maintain their energy, whilst not causing any extra weight gain.

In order to promote his new product, Bruce hands out advertising flyers in local shopping centres which state: "Buy our revolutionary Go-Pro-Bars – scientific tests have proven that by eating 2 bars before and after your workout, you are guaranteed to lose weight and start feeling healthier. Made with our secret formula, our bars have the highest protein content of any fitness bar in the world!"

Every day, Vincent lifts weights and undertakes intensive cardio workouts at his local gym after work. He weighs about 105 kilograms. After reading one of Bruce's flyers, Vincent goes to Go-Pro and purchases twenty packets of Go-Pro-Bars.

After one month of eating 2 Go-Pro-Bars before and after his workout, Vincent discovers that he has very little energy to lift weights at the gym anymore and has also gained 10 kilograms. He discovers a report from the Sports Institute of Australia which states that Go-Pro-Bars have little nutritional value: the bars have only 5% protein content and the remaining 95% consists of sugar.

Upset, Vincent approaches Bruce who says, "The flyer was not meant to be taken seriously. No-one who read it would believe what I said about the protein bars. There is nothing you can do about it."

Advise Vincent if he can bring any legal claims against Bruce under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL).

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Opener + preliminary: Vincent is a consumer (s 3: 20 × $59.95 = $1,199; personal consumption).
  2. Issue 1 — s 18: four elements; Taco Bell audience (shoppers / people who exercise regularly); Annand; Powerballwin; break the flyer into 4 statements; puffery (Given v Pryor) vs specific claims; s 4 "guaranteed"; Bruce's "not meant seriously" fails (intention irrelevant).
  3. Issue 2 — s 29(1)(a) composition/standard (Cadbury), (g) performance/benefits (Hartnell v Sharp); Coles "no difference"; Egg case + Nurofen analogy.
  4. Issue 3 — Guarantees: s 54 acceptable quality; s 55 purpose represented by supplier; s 56 description ("protein bar").
  5. Remedies: refund $1,199, damages s 236, injunction, ACCC penalties s 29, adverse publicity s 247.
  6. Conclusion: likely succeeds on ss 18, 29, 54, 55, 56.

Whether Vincent has claims against Bruce under the ACL depends on three issues: (1) whether the flyer was misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18; (2) whether it contained false or misleading representations under s 29; and (3) whether the Go-Pro-Bars failed the consumer guarantees in ss 54–56.

Preliminary: is Vincent a consumer?

Under s 3 of the ACL, a person acquires goods as a consumer if the amount paid is not more than $100,000 or the goods are of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption. Vincent paid 20 × $59.95 = $1,199 for food bars for his own consumption. He is a consumer.

Issue 1: Misleading or deceptive conduct

Issue

Did Bruce engage in misleading or deceptive conduct in breach of s 18 of the ACL by distributing the Go-Pro-Bars flyer?

Law

Section 18(1) of the ACL provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". There are four requirements:

  1. A person: "person" is interpreted broadly and includes individuals and corporations.
  2. In trade or commerce: trade or commerce within Australia, including any business or professional activity, whether or not carried on for profit: s 2(1); Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978).
  3. Engage in conduct: includes doing or refusing to do an act, including making a statement, claim or promise: s 2(2).
  4. Misleading or deceptive: to mislead is to lead into error; to deceive is to cause a person to believe something false: Weitmann v Katies (1977). It is enough that conduct is likely to mislead; it is not necessary to prove anyone was actually misled. The test is objective, and intention or fault is irrelevant.

In Taco v Taco Bell (1982), the US Taco Bell's use of the same name as an existing Sydney restaurant was held misleading because customers would believe the businesses were connected. The Court set two tests for conduct directed at the public: first, identify the relevant section of the public at whom the conduct is directed; second, consider the effect on all who come within it, "including the astute and the gullible, the intelligent and the not so intelligent". Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979) refined the second test: the effect on a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed, but perhaps of somewhat less than average intelligence", but not someone "quite unusually stupid". Only a small percentage of the audience need be misled: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010).

A statement that is not literally true is not necessarily misleading: mere sales "puff" is generally not. However, in Given v Pryor (1980), an exaggerated phrase that alone might be puffery was misleading because the advertisement as a whole conveyed a false impression. Under s 4, a representation about a future matter is taken to be misleading if the person had no reasonable grounds for making it (ACCC v Powerballwin.com). In ACCC v Reckitt Benckiser, Nurofen products marketed as specially formulated for particular pain were found misleading because they were identical to standard Nurofen.

Application

Person, trade or commerce, conduct. Bruce is an individual and therefore a "person". He owns and manages a fitness store and handed out the flyers to promote a product he manufactures and sells for profit; this is business activity "in trade or commerce" under s 2(1). Distributing a flyer containing claims about his product is making a statement or claim, so Bruce "engaged in conduct" (s 2(2)).

Relevant audience (Taco Bell test 1). The flyers were handed out in local shopping centres, so the audience is members of the public who shop there, particularly people who exercise regularly and are interested in fitness, weight loss and health — the market for protein bars.

Effect on that audience (Taco Bell test 2 and Annand). That audience includes the astute and the gullible. A shopper of less than average intelligence, who is not well-informed about nutrition, would read the flyer at face value. The flyer contains four separate claims, which should be considered individually and as a whole:

  • "Revolutionary" and "secret formula" — these are vague and subjective. Even a not particularly intelligent shopper would recognise them as sales talk. They are likely puffery.
  • "Scientific tests have proven…" — this is a specific factual claim that scientific testing exists and supports the product. Nothing suggests any tests exist; the only evidence, the Sports Institute report, shows the opposite. It is likely misleading.
  • "You are guaranteed to lose weight and start feeling healthier" — a promise about a future result (s 4). A bar that is 95% sugar gives Bruce no reasonable grounds for promising weight loss, so the statement is taken to be misleading. Vincent in fact gained 10 kg and lost energy.
  • "The highest protein content of any fitness bar in the world" — although phrased as a superlative, this is a measurable claim about the bars' composition. With 5% protein, it is plainly false.

As in Given v Pryor, even if parts of the flyer are exaggeration, the flyer as a whole conveys the impression that Go-Pro-Bars are a high-protein, scientifically proven weight-loss product. As in ACCC v Reckitt Benckiser, consumers were sold a premium-priced product on the basis of a quality it did not have. Only a small percentage of the audience need be misled (Powerballwin.com), and Vincent himself was actually misled into buying twenty packets.

Bruce's argument. Bruce says the flyer "was not meant to be taken seriously" and "no-one who read it would believe" it. This argument is likely to fail. First, intention is irrelevant under s 18, so what Bruce meant does not matter. Second, the test is objective and is applied to the target audience, including the gullible and less-than-averagely informed (Annand) — not Bruce's own view. Third, "scientific tests have proven" is phrased specifically to be believed; it invites the reader to take the claim seriously. The argument might succeed only for "revolutionary" and "secret formula".

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Bruce has engaged in misleading or deceptive conduct in breach of s 18.

Issue 2: False or misleading representations

Issue

Did Bruce make false or misleading representations about the Go-Pro-Bars in breach of s 29 of the ACL?

Law

Section 29 provides that a person must not, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or promotion of goods or services, make certain false or misleading representations. Unlike contract law misrepresentation, s 29 does not require proof that the statement was fraudulent, negligent or innocent. It includes representations that goods are of a particular standard, quality, value, grade or composition (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)) and that goods have particular performance characteristics, uses or benefits (s 29(1)(g); Hartnell v Sharp (1975)). There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" in s 29 and "misleading or deceptive" in s 18: ACCC v Coles Supermarkets Australia [2014] FCA 634 at [40]. In Optus v Telstra [2018] FCA 745, Telstra's advertising of "unlimited" internet breached both ss 18 and 29. In ACCC v C.I. & Co Pty Ltd [2010] FCA 1511, cage eggs were labelled "free range"; the Court held this falsely represented the eggs' quality and characteristics, and described it as a "cruel deception" because consumers had no practical way of detecting the truth.

Application

The flyer was distributed in trade or commerce in connection with the promotion and supply of goods. "The highest protein content of any fitness bar in the world" is a representation about the bars' composition and standard under s 29(1)(a); with only 5% protein and 95% sugar, it is false. "Scientific tests have proven" weight loss, and "guaranteed to lose weight and start feeling healthier", are representations that the bars have particular performance characteristics and benefits under s 29(1)(g); they too are false. Like the eggs in the Egg case, a consumer cannot tell a bar's protein content by looking at or eating it, and must rely entirely on Bruce's claims; Vincent only discovered the truth from an independent report. Following ACCC v Coles, the same analysis that establishes a breach of s 18 establishes a breach of s 29.

Conclusion

Bruce has very likely breached s 29(1)(a) and (g).

Issue 3: Consumer guarantees

Issue

Can Vincent claim against Bruce for failure to comply with the consumer guarantees in ss 54, 55 and 56 of the ACL?

Law

The ACL contains statutory guarantees that protect consumers and cannot be excluded by contract (s 64). Under s 54, goods supplied to a consumer in trade or commerce must be of acceptable quality: fit for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied, acceptable in appearance and finish, free from defects, safe and durable, as a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the state and condition of the goods (including hidden defects) would regard as acceptable (Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas Pty Ltd [2015] VCAT 2044; Mitchell v Oz Design Furniture Pty Ltd [2016] VCAT 108). Under s 55, goods must be reasonably fit for any purpose disclosed by the consumer and for any purpose for which the supplier represents that they are reasonably fit, unless the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable to rely, on the supplier's skill or judgment. Under s 56, goods must correspond with their description.

Application

s 54. Fitness protein bars are commonly supplied to give regular exercisers energy without causing weight gain. A reasonable consumer who knew that Go-Pro-Bars were 95% sugar — a hidden defect that cannot be seen — would not regard them as acceptable for that purpose. Vincent lost energy and gained 10 kg. The bars are not of acceptable quality.

s 55. Vincent did not expressly tell Bruce his purpose, but s 55 also covers any purpose for which the supplier represents the goods are fit. Bruce's own flyer represented that the bars were fit for weight loss when eaten before and after workouts. Vincent relied on the flyer, which was reasonable since Bruce manufactures the bars and runs a fitness store. The bars were not fit for that purpose.

s 56. The goods were described and sold as "protein bars" with "the highest protein content of any fitness bar in the world". A bar that is 5% protein and 95% sugar does not correspond with that description.

Bruce's statement that "there is nothing you can do about it" has no legal effect: under s 64 a business cannot exclude or limit these guarantees.

Conclusion

Bruce has failed to comply with the guarantees in ss 54, 55 and 56.

Remedies

Because Vincent has suffered loss because of Bruce's contraventions, he may seek damages under s 236 and a refund or compensation order under ss 237–241 — at least the $1,199 he paid, plus any provable consequential loss (for example costs caused by his weight gain). For the guarantee failures, he may seek a refund rather than a replacement, since replacement bars would have the same defect. He may also report Bruce to the ACCC, which may seek an injunction to stop the flyer (ss 232–235), pecuniary penalties for the s 29 breaches (ss 224–231) and an adverse publicity order (s 247). Bruce is unlikely to have a defence of reasonable mistake of fact under s 207, as he manufactures the bars himself and knows their content.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Vincent will succeed in claims against Bruce under ss 18, 29, 54, 55 and 56 of the ACL, and Bruce's argument that the flyer was not meant seriously will fail.

Margin note: Bruce also manufactures the bars, so he is a "manufacturer" (s 7) — but the s 138 strict liability is for safety defects (s 9). Weight gain is not clearly a safety defect, so one sentence at most; don't build an issue on it.

Q4 · 2022 S1 Q2 — Troy's potential liability (Polex Elite) 25 marks

Question text (click)

Troy owns a shop that sells luxury watches. Troy decides to run a television commercial promoting the range of watches he has for sale. The commercial states that the store currently has various Polex watches on sale, which are known for their durability, water and scratch resistance and are manufactured in London. The image of Polex's most expensive model called the Polex Elite is pictured throughout the entire commercial.

Daniel is a keen cyclist. After viewing the TV commercial, Daniel believes that the Polex Elite would be ideal for his needs and drives to Troy's shop. Upon arrival, Daniel says to Troy that he has been searching for a good quality, hard-wearing watch for months and after seeing the commercial is so pleased to know that the Polex Elite will be suitable for him to wear at cycling competitions in all weather conditions. Troy says, "Well, Polex is the best brand that you can buy. I'm pleased that we can help you."

Contained in the information booklet that Polex previously sent to Troy's shop, was a pamphlet prepared by Polex which correctly states, "The Polex Elite is a designer watch and should only be worn at special indoor occasions due to its easily scratchable surfaces and delicate internal mechanisms and design features. This is the watch specially designed for a businessman, wanting to impress." Troy knows about the contents of this pamphlet but does not wish to jeopardize the $25,000 sale of his most expensive watch in the store.

After wearing the watch for two weeks during various cycling competitions, the watch stops working. Daniel notices that the face of the watch has developed deep scratch marks on it and also sees that water has appeared beneath the glass surface. Daniel takes the watch to Bob who is the owner of "Bob's Watches" (a competing watch retail store). Bob tells Daniel that the watch should never have been worn whilst cycling as it is Polex's most delicate watch with highly sensitive design qualities. Bob also tells Daniel that unfortunately the watch has been damaged beyond repair.

Advise Troy of his potential liability under the ACL. Please use the ILAC method to answer this question.

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Note: you are advising Troy (the defendant) — frame issues as "Is Troy liable to Daniel…?" and give Troy his best arguments, but be honest about the outcome.
  2. Preliminary: Daniel is a consumer ($25,000 < $100,000).
  3. Issue 1 — s 18: TV commercial (overall impression: Elite pictured + "durable, water and scratch resistant") — Henderson (literally true for the range); Troy's silence in shop (Henjo); "best brand" = puffery.
  4. Issue 2 — s 29(1)(a) standard/quality, (g) performance characteristics/uses; (k) place of origin (assume "London" true — no facts).
  5. Issue 3 — Guarantees: s 55 (Daniel disclosed cycling/all weather — strongest), s 54 (arguable), s 56 (commercial's description); s 64.
  6. Remedies + Troy's defences (s 207 fails — he knew; Polex is manufacturer); conclusion: Troy likely liable.

Troy's potential liability to Daniel under the ACL depends on three issues: (1) whether the commercial and Troy's conduct in the shop were misleading or deceptive under s 18; (2) whether Troy made false or misleading representations under s 29; and (3) whether the Polex Elite failed the consumer guarantees in ss 54–56.

Preliminary: is Daniel a consumer?

Under s 3, goods are acquired by a consumer if the price is not more than $100,000 or they are of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal use. Daniel paid $25,000 for a watch for his own use. He is a consumer.

Issue 1: Misleading or deceptive conduct

Issue

Is Troy liable to Daniel for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 of the ACL in relation to the commercial and his statements in store?

Law

Section 18(1) provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". A "person" includes individuals and corporations. "Trade or commerce" includes any business activity, whether or not for profit: s 2(1); Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978). "Engaging in conduct" includes doing or refusing to do an act (s 2(2)), and silence can amount to conduct where failing to disclose information creates or maintains a misleading impression: Henjo Investments v Collins (1988). To mislead is to lead into error: Weitmann v Katies (1977). Intention is irrelevant and the test is objective.

In Taco v Taco Bell (1982), the Court held the US company's use of the "Taco Bell" name misleading, and set two tests: identify the section of the public targeted, then consider the effect on everyone within it, including the astute and the gullible. Under Annand & Thompson v TPC (1979), the test is the effect on a person "not particularly intelligent or well-informed". Only a small percentage need be misled: ACCC v Powerballwin.com (2010). In Henderson v Pioneer Homes (1980), an advertisement was misleading even though the information was technically correct, because of its overall impression. In Trivago N.V. v ACCC [2020], the way offers were presented ("Top Offer") was misleading because it led consumers to believe those offers were the cheapest. Mere puffery is generally not misleading.

Application

Elements. Troy is a person; he runs a watch shop for profit, so he acts in trade or commerce; and broadcasting a commercial and speaking to a customer are "conduct".

The commercial. The relevant audience is television viewers interested in buying luxury or quality watches, including active people like Daniel. Troy will argue that the commercial was literally true: it said only that the store sells "various Polex watches" known for durability, water and scratch resistance, and some Polex models presumably are. However, the Polex Elite — Troy's most expensive watch, which is scratchable and delicate — is "pictured throughout the entire commercial". As in Henderson v Pioneer Homes, a statement can be technically correct yet misleading through its overall impression, and as in Trivago, the way a product is presented can carry a representation. A viewer of less than average knowledge (Annand) would reasonably assume that the watch shown throughout is the watch being described as durable and water and scratch resistant. Daniel was in fact misled.

Troy's statements and silence in store. "Polex is the best brand that you can buy" is vague, subjective praise and is likely puffery. The more serious conduct is Troy's silence. Daniel expressly said he wanted a "good quality, hard-wearing watch" for "cycling competitions in all weather conditions". Troy knew from the Polex pamphlet that the Elite should only be worn at indoor occasions, but said nothing because he did not want to lose a $25,000 sale. As in Henjo Investments v Collins, his silence maintained the misleading impression created by the commercial, and his reply ("I'm pleased that we can help you") positively confirmed it.

Conclusion

Troy is likely to be found to have engaged in misleading or deceptive conduct in breach of s 18, through both the commercial and his silence.

Issue 2: False or misleading representations

Issue

Is Troy liable for making false or misleading representations about the Polex Elite under s 29 of the ACL?

Law

Section 29 prohibits a person, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or promotion of goods, from making false or misleading representations, including that goods are of a particular standard, quality or grade (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)), that goods have particular performance characteristics, uses or benefits (s 29(1)(g); Hartnell v Sharp (1975)), or concerning their place of origin (s 29(1)(k)). There is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" and "misleading or deceptive": ACCC v Coles Supermarkets [2014] FCA 634. In Optus v Telstra [2018] FCA 745, a television campaign promoting "unlimited" internet breached both ss 18 and 29.

Application

The commercial was made in connection with the promotion of goods. By picturing the Elite while describing durability and water and scratch resistance, it represented that the Elite was of that standard and quality (s 29(1)(a)) and had those performance characteristics and uses (s 29(1)(g)). Those representations were false — the Elite has "easily scratchable surfaces and delicate internal mechanisms". Like Telstra's national campaign in Optus v Telstra, the commercial was broadcast widely and carried a definite meaning. The question does not say whether Polex watches are in fact manufactured in London; assuming they are, there is no breach of s 29(1)(k).

Conclusion

Troy is likely liable under s 29(1)(a) and (g).

Issue 3: Consumer guarantees

Issue

Is Troy liable to Daniel for failing to comply with the consumer guarantees in ss 54, 55 and 56 of the ACL?

Law

Under s 55, goods supplied to a consumer must be reasonably fit for any disclosed purpose — a particular purpose the consumer makes known, expressly or by implication, to the supplier (s 55(2)) — unless the consumer did not rely, or it was unreasonable to rely, on the supplier's skill or judgment. Under s 54, goods must be of acceptable quality: fit for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied, acceptable in appearance and finish, free from defects, safe and durable, judged by a reasonable consumer fully acquainted with the goods' state and condition, including hidden defects (Licciardo v Plush Think Sofas [2015] VCAT 2044; Mitchell v Oz Design Furniture [2016] VCAT 108). Under s 56, goods must correspond with their description. Any term excluding these guarantees is void: s 64.

Application

s 55 (strongest). Daniel expressly made known his particular purpose: wearing the watch at cycling competitions in all weather conditions. He relied on Troy, the specialist retailer, who did not correct him and said he was pleased to "help". It was reasonable to rely on an owner of a luxury watch shop. The watch was not fit for that purpose — it stopped working within two weeks of exactly that use. Troy cannot argue that reliance was unreasonable, because he had the pamphlet showing the watch was unsuitable and stayed silent.

s 54 (arguable). Troy will argue that the Elite is of acceptable quality for the purpose for which designer watches are commonly supplied — wearing at indoor occasions — and that the damage was caused by Daniel's unsuitable use. That argument has some strength. However, a watch is commonly supplied to be worn daily, and a reasonable consumer, fully acquainted with its hidden delicacy, may not regard a $25,000 watch that is destroyed by water and scratches within two weeks as durable.

s 56. The commercial described the Polex watches shown as durable and water and scratch resistant. The Elite does not correspond with that description.

Conclusion

Troy is very likely liable under s 55, and arguably under ss 54 and 56.

Remedies and Troy's defences

Daniel may seek a refund of $25,000 (the watch is beyond repair, so repair is not an option) under ss 237–241, damages under s 236 for loss caused by the contraventions, and the ACCC could seek an injunction against the commercial (ss 232–235), pecuniary penalties for the s 29 breach (ss 224–231) and an adverse publicity order (s 247). Troy cannot rely on the defence of reasonable mistake of fact (s 207): he did not rely on misleading information from Polex — Polex's pamphlet was correct, and Troy knew of it. Although Polex is the manufacturer, the guarantees and the ss 18 and 29 contraventions attach to Troy as supplier and advertiser.

Overall conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, Troy is liable to Daniel under ss 18, 29 and 55 of the ACL and is likely to have to refund the $25,000.

Margin note: Bob is a competitor, but he has suffered nothing — don't discuss him as a claimant. His evidence just proves the watch was unsuitable.

Q5 · 2021 S1 Q3 — Tim v Brisbane Truck Hire 15 marks

Question text (click)

Tim is in the process of moving house and decides to hire a medium sized removalist van for 5 days. He searches online and makes a booking for a van through 'Brisbane Truck Hire' (BTH).

As part of the online booking, Tim arranges to collect the van at 9am on the 20th June and to return it by 9am on the 27th June. He pays a $400 deposit after reading through the online terms and conditions of the hire agreement.

At 9am on the 20th June, Tim arrives at BTH. The manager of BTH tells Tim: "We are waiting for your van to be returned by another customer. It won't be too long a wait." After a lengthy wait, Tim leaves BTH with the removalist van at 1pm.

On the 27th June, Tim is on his way back to BTH and is stuck in bad traffic. He returns the van to BTH at 10.00 am (1 hour later than expected). Tim is then informed by the manager of BTH that his deposit of $400 is forfeited. The manager refers Tim to the following terms in the online agreement:

1. BTH will make all reasonable efforts to make sure that their vehicles are available for collection by the customer at the agreed upon time. However, BTH will not be held liable for any delay that may occur.
2. The customer must return the vehicle by no later than the agreed upon time. Any failure for whatever reason to comply with this requirement will result in a complete forfeiture of the deposit price.

Advise Tim as to whether he has any claims against BTH under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL)? (Note: you must only look at the ACL provisions.)

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Preliminary: Tim is a consumer (s 3; moving house = personal/household; $400 deposit).
  2. Issue 1 — Clause 2 (forfeit $400 for any lateness) unfair: ss 23, 24, 25, 27 → void.
  3. Issue 2 — Clause 1 ("not liable for any delay") void under s 64 (s 60) and unfair under s 25.
  4. Differences from Ben: traffic is more within Tim's control than a flood (helps BTH); 1 hour late; BTH's delay caused by another customer, not its own understaffing (BTH: less fault).
  5. One line each: s 21 unlikely (no special disadvantage); s 36 unlikely (supplied same day).
  6. Remedies: declare void, refund $400, s 236 damages for 4-hour delay (eg removalist helpers' wages) if proven.

Whether Tim has claims against BTH under the ACL depends on two main issues: (1) whether clause 2 is an unfair term; and (2) whether BTH can rely on clause 1 to exclude liability for its delay. Under s 3, Tim is a consumer: he hired a van to move house — a personal, household purpose — and the price was well below $100,000.

Issue 1: Clause 2 — unfair term

Issue

Can Tim have clause 2 declared void as an unfair term under s 23 of the ACL?

Law

[Copy the Law paragraphs from Q1 Issue 1: s 23 (three requirements), s 23(3) consumer contract, s 27 standard form, s 24(1) three limbs, s 24(2)–(3) transparency and contract as a whole, s 25 examples — Director v AAPT (2006), Director v Trainstation (2006).]

Application

The hire is a contract for the supply of a vehicle to Tim, an individual, wholly for personal and household use (moving house), so it is a consumer contract (s 23(3)). Tim booked online on BTH's terms with no negotiation, so it is a standard form contract (s 27).

Clause 2 forfeits the entire $400 deposit for any late return "for whatever reason". Clause 1 means BTH bears no consequence when it is late — and it was four hours late, against Tim's one hour. This is a significant imbalance and a s 25 example of a term penalising one party for breach while the other may limit its own performance.

BTH will argue it has a legitimate interest in prompt return so the van can be hired to the next customer — indeed, its own delay was caused by another customer returning late, which shows the problem is real. BTH may also argue that bad traffic, unlike a natural disaster, is foreseeable, so Tim could have allowed extra time. However, forfeiting the whole deposit for a one-hour delay, without regard to whether BTH suffered any loss, goes beyond what is reasonably necessary; a late fee proportionate to the delay would protect BTH. Tim suffers financial detriment of $400. Although the term was transparent — plainly worded and Tim read it (s 24(2)(a)) — the contract as a whole (s 24(2)(b)) places all of the risk of delay on Tim.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, clause 2 is unfair and void under s 23.

Issue 2: Clause 1 — exclusion of liability for delay

Issue

Can BTH rely on clause 1 to avoid liability to Tim for the four-hour delay under the ACL?

Law

Section 60 guarantees that services will be rendered with due care and skill, and s 62 that they will be supplied within a reasonable time where no time was fixed. Under s 64, any term that seeks to exclude, restrict or modify the guarantees is automatically void. Clause 1 can also be tested under ss 23–25, which list terms limiting one party's performance or the other's right to sue.

Application

The collection time was fixed at 9am, so s 62 does not strictly apply. However, BTH knew Tim's van was booked for 9am and allowed it to be hired to another customer whose return it could not guarantee; the manager's assurance that "it won't be too long a wait" proved wrong by four hours. It is arguable that BTH did not render its service with due care and skill (s 60). Clause 1 ("BTH will not be held liable for any delay that may occur") seeks to exclude that liability and is void under s 64 to that extent. It is also likely unfair: it excuses all BTH delay while clause 2 severely punishes Tim's.

Conclusion

BTH cannot rely on clause 1 to exclude its liability. A claim under s 21 (unconscionable conduct) is unlikely, since Tim was under no special disadvantage, and a claim under s 36 is unlikely, since BTH supplied the van on the agreed day.

Remedies

Tim should seek a declaration that clauses 1 and 2 are void and an order for the refund of his $400 deposit (ss 237–241). If the four-hour delay caused him loss — for example, paying removalist helpers to wait — he may claim damages under s 236. On the balance of probabilities, Tim will recover his deposit.

Q6 · 2021 S2 Q3 — Barry v Harty (Fish Delight) 15 marks

Question text (click)

Harty is the owner of a seafood restaurant called "Fish Delight" in Toowong, Brisbane. The restaurant is located across the street from another restaurant called "Seafoods Are Us", which is managed by Barry.

On Harty's birthday, he decides to host a private lunch at his home with all his staff members. At the lunch, Harty says to his staff members that "Seafoods Are Us do not serve fresh fish. Their fish is rotten and they freeze it for months before using it. I've tasted their food, it is absolutely disgusting!"

The day after the lunch, in an effort to drive more customers to his restaurant, Harty places a sign outside Fish Delight, which reads: "Come in and eat the ONLY fresh seafood in Toowong!"

Alex, who attended Harty's lunch and who used to work at "Seafoods Are Us", tells Barry about Harty's comments at the party and also about the sign. Barry, whose restaurant only serves fresh seafood caught within 24 hours, is upset about Harty's actions.

Advise Barry as to whether he has any claims against Harty under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) in relation to Harty's comments and the sign? (Note: you must only look at the ACL provisions.)

Answer plan (6 lines)
  1. Preliminary: Barry is a competitor — can he sue? Yes for ss 18/29 (Gillette v Energiser; Taco Bell). Guarantees ss 54–56 NOT relevant (Tut 9 feedback).
  2. Issue 1 — the lunch comments: fails at "in trade or commerce" (private birthday lunch at home, staff only, not to the public or promoting supply) → no s 18/s 29.
  3. Issue 2 — the sign, s 18: four elements; audience = diners/passers-by in Toowong; Annand; "ONLY fresh" = specific factual comparative claim, false (Barry's is caught within 24 hours); not puffery (contrast "best"); Optus v Telstra; McWilliam's (not mere confusion).
  4. Issue 3 — the sign, s 29(1)(a) (food standard/quality) and (b) (services); Coles "no difference".
  5. Remedies: injunction (remove sign), corrective/adverse publicity (s 247), damages s 236 only if lost customers proven; ACCC penalties.
  6. Conclusion: sign claim likely succeeds; comments claim fails under the ACL (defamation is tort law — outside the question).

Whether Barry has claims against Harty under the ACL depends on three issues: (1) whether Barry, as a competitor, may bring a claim; (2) whether Harty's comments at the lunch breach s 18 or s 29; and (3) whether the sign breaches s 18 and s 29.

Preliminary: can Barry, a competitor, bring a claim?

Although ss 18 and 29 appear in the consumer protection part of the ACL, they are not confined to protecting consumers. Section 18 may be relied on in disputes between businesses and competitors, and many significant s 18 cases involve competitors suing each other: Gillette v Energiser (2002). In Taco v Taco Bell (1982), the existing Sydney restaurant successfully sued its rival. Barry, who manages the competing restaurant across the street, may therefore bring a claim. The consumer guarantees in ss 54–56 are not relevant, because Barry has not acquired any goods from Harty as a consumer.

Issue 1: Harty's comments at the private lunch

Issue

Did Harty's comments to his staff at his birthday lunch breach s 18 or s 29 of the ACL?

Law

Section 18(1) provides that "a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive". "Trade or commerce" means trade or commerce within Australia or between Australia and overseas, and includes any business or professional activity, whether or not for profit: s 2(1); Re Ku-Ring-Gai Co-operative Building Society (1978). Section 29 additionally requires that the representation be made in connection with the supply or promotion of goods or services.

Application

Harty is a person and, by speaking, engaged in conduct (s 2(2)). His statements that Seafoods Are Us serves rotten, months-old frozen fish are false, since Barry serves only seafood caught within 24 hours. However, they were made at a private birthday lunch at Harty's home, to his own staff, not to customers or the public. Barry could argue that Harty was speaking as an employer to his employees, which is connected with his business. On balance, though, a private social conversation is not a business activity directed at anyone in the market, and it was not made in connection with the supply or promotion of Harty's food. The comments were also not directed at a section of the public, so the Taco Bell test has no audience to apply to.

Conclusion

The comments were likely not made "in trade or commerce", so Barry has no claim under ss 18 or 29 in respect of them. (Any claim for the comments would lie in the tort of defamation, which is outside this question.)

Issue 2: The sign — misleading or deceptive conduct

Issue

Can Barry bring an action against Harty for misleading or deceptive conduct under s 18 in relation to the sign "Come in and eat the ONLY fresh seafood in Toowong!"?

Law

[Copy P2 s 18 Law in full — four elements, Weitmann v Katies, Taco Bell two tests, Annand & Thompson, Powerballwin — then P3 Puffery: Given v Pryor, Henderson v Pioneer Homes, McWilliam's Wines v McDonald's, Optus v Telstra.]

Application

Elements. Harty is a person. He placed the sign outside his restaurant "in an effort to drive more customers" to it — promotional business activity in trade or commerce (s 2(1)). Displaying a sign is making a claim, so he engaged in conduct (s 2(2)).

Audience. Applying the first Taco Bell test, the relevant section of the public is people in Toowong looking for somewhere to eat seafood, including passers-by on the street where both restaurants sit. Under the second test and Annand, it includes diners who are not particularly intelligent or well-informed and who would take the sign at its word.

Misleading? Harty may argue the sign is puffery, like "the best French food in Brisbane". However, unlike "best", which is a subjective opinion, "the ONLY fresh seafood in Toowong" is a specific, factual and comparative claim — the capitalised "ONLY" emphasises that every other seafood restaurant in Toowong, including the one directly across the street, does not serve fresh seafood. That claim is false: Barry serves only seafood caught within 24 hours. Like the word "unlimited" in Optus v Telstra, "ONLY fresh" conveys a definite meaning to its audience. This is not mere confusion as in McWilliam's Wines v McDonald's; it leads diners into the error (Weitmann v Katies) of believing Barry's seafood is not fresh. Only a small percentage of diners need be misled (Powerballwin.com), and Harty's intention is irrelevant — although the timing, the day after his comments, suggests the sign was deliberately aimed at Barry.

Conclusion

On the balance of probabilities, the sign is misleading or deceptive in breach of s 18.

Issue 3: The sign — false or misleading representation

Issue

Does the sign breach s 29 of the ACL?

Law

Section 29 prohibits false or misleading representations, in trade or commerce, in connection with the supply or promotion of goods or services, including that goods are of a particular standard or quality (s 29(1)(a); ACCC v Cadbury (2004)) and that services are of a particular standard or quality (s 29(1)(b)). Competitors may rely on s 29, and there is no meaningful difference between "false or misleading" and "misleading or deceptive": ACCC v Coles Supermarkets [2014] FCA 634 at [40].

Application

The sign promotes the supply of Harty's food (goods) and dining (services). It represents that Harty's seafood is of a particular standard and quality — the only fresh seafood available — which is false as a comparison with Barry's restaurant (s 29(1)(a), (b)). For the same reasons as under s 18, it is false or misleading.

Conclusion

Harty has likely breached s 29(1)(a) and (b).

Remedies

Barry is most likely to obtain an injunction (ss 232–235) requiring Harty to remove or correct the sign, and may seek an adverse publicity or corrective order (s 247). He can claim damages under s 236 only if he proves loss caused by the sign, such as lost customers, which may be difficult as the sign was recently placed. He may also report Harty to the ACCC, which may seek pecuniary penalties for the s 29 breach (ss 224–231). Barry is therefore likely to succeed in relation to the sign but not the comments.

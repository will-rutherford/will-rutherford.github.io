Questions (on-syllabus)

Q1 1 mark · T6 Climate & games

Suppose the Government introduces a 10c tax on sugary beverages. Which of the following concepts could be used to argue FOR the tax?

  • (a) Externalities.
  • (b) Tariffs.
  • (c) Monopolies.
  • (d) The invisible hand.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) Externalities.  🟢 confident

A tax is justified when the market over-produces because buyers or sellers ignore a cost borne by others: a negative externality (sugar → obesity → public health costs). A corrective (Pigouvian) tax makes them internalise it (L10). The invisible hand is the argument against intervening.

Q2 1 mark · T1 D&S · T3 Market power

Helen is a digital designer who creates commissioned artwork using advanced graphic software. It costs Helen $30 to create the first design in an artwork series and $10 for each subsequent design. Helen can sell 4 designs in a series for $250 and 5 designs in the same series for $300. Which of the following statements is true?

  • (a) The marginal cost of the fifth design is $10.
  • (b) The average cost for 10 designs is $10 per design.
  • (c) The marginal benefit of the fifth design is $60.
  • (d) The producer surplus of the fifth design is $60.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) The marginal cost of the fifth design is $10.  🟢 confident

  • MC of the 5th = cost of one more design after the first = $10. (a) is true.
  • AC of 10 = (30 + 9 × 10)/10 = 120/10 = $12, not $10. (b) is false.
  • MB of the 5th = 300 − 250 = $50, not $60. (c) is false.
  • Surplus on the 5th = MB − MC = 50 − 10 = $40, not $60. (d) is false.
“Marginal” = the change in the total from one more unit. Don't divide the total by Q (that's the average).

Q3 1 mark · T1 D&S

Three primary reasons for an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded of a normal good are

  • (a) increasing marginal costs, complements and substitutes.
  • (b) decreasing marginal product of labour, scarcity and the willingness to pay.
  • (c) normal goods, inferior goods and the law of diminishing marginal utility.
  • (d) substitution effects, income effects and decreasing additional satisfaction.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) substitution effects, income effects and decreasing additional satisfaction.  🟢 confident

The law of demand (L2): when P↑, buyers switch to substitutes (substitution effect) and can afford less (income effect), and each extra unit is worth less to them (diminishing marginal benefit, i.e. “decreasing additional satisfaction”). (a) and (b) are supply-side ideas.

Q4 1 mark · T1 D&S

Zuli decides to spend the morning developing her BMX bike riding skills at the skatepark instead of studying at the library. The opportunity cost of Zuli's decision is

  • (a) the improvement in her BMX riding skills.
  • (b) the new knowledge she would have gained at the library.
  • (c) the amount of money she has spent on her BMX bike.
  • (d) zero, since she chose to practice BMX bike riding, which reflects her preferences.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) the new knowledge she would have gained at the library.  🟢 confident

Opportunity cost = the value of the next-best alternative given up. She gave up studying, so the cost is the knowledge she would have gained. (a) is the benefit of her choice. (c) is a sunk cost.

Q5 1 mark · T1 D&S · T2 Intervention

The price elasticity of demand for tickets to end of season tennis matches is estimated to be 0.9. In order to increase ticket revenues, an economist would advise

  • (a) decreasing the price of match tickets because demand is elastic.
  • (b) increasing the price of match tickets because demand is elastic.
  • (c) decreasing the price of match tickets because demand is inelastic.
  • (d) increasing the price of match tickets because demand is inelastic.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) increasing the price of match tickets because demand is inelastic.  🟢 confident

|εD| = 0.9 < 1, so demand is inelastic. A 1% price rise cuts Q by only 0.9%, so TR = P × Q rises. To raise revenue, raise the price.

2026 convention: εD is negative (−0.9). Here 0.9 is a magnitude. Classify by |ε|. Inelastic → move P and TR in the same direction.

Q7 1 mark · T3 Market power

Which of the following costs should NOT be considered by a firm in the short-run?

  • (a) Marginal costs.
  • (b) Sunk Costs.
  • (c) Opportunity costs.
  • (d) Average variable costs.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) Sunk Costs.  🟢 confident

A sunk cost has already been paid and cannot be recovered, whatever the firm does next. So it cannot change the best decision. L5 says this directly: “fixed costs are sunk costs … and so should be ignored” (that is why PS = TR − VC, and maximising PS = maximising profit). MC, opportunity cost and AVC all change with the decision, so they matter.

Opportunity cost is always relevant. Don't pick it just because it sounds abstract.

Q8 1 mark · T2 Intervention · T5 Trade

Which of the following does NOT create a deadweight loss in competitive markets?

  • (a) Taxes
  • (b) Shift in demand
  • (c) Import quotas
  • (d) Tariffs
Answer & working

Answer: (b) Shift in demand  🟢 confident

A DWL appears only when something forces Q away from where MB (demand) = MC (supply). Taxes, quotas and tariffs all drive a wedge, so Q ≠ Q*. A demand shift just moves the market to a new equilibrium, where MB = MC still holds, so there is no DWL.

Q9 1 mark · T2 Intervention · T5 Trade

Which of the following creates a deadweight loss in competitive markets?

  • (a) Taxes
  • (b) Import quotas
  • (c) Tariffs
  • (d) All of the above.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) All of the above.  🟢 confident

All three push Q away from the efficient level: a tax cuts trade below Q*, and a tariff or quota raises the domestic price, so domestic over-production plus under-consumption gives two DWL triangles. This is the companion to Q8.

Q10 1 mark · T1 D&S

If the price goes above the market equilibrium, there will be

  • (a) a surplus of production.
  • (b) a shortage of production.
  • (c) Pareto efficiency.
  • (d) excess demand.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) a surplus of production.  🟢 confident

At P > P*: QS rises and QD falls, so QS > QD. That is excess supply (a surplus). Excess demand (shortage) is what happens below P*.

Q11 1 mark · T1 D&S

When there is an increase in suppliers and a decrease in the price of a substitute good, the impact on the market equilibrium will be a/an __ in quantity and a/an __ in price.

  • (a) uncertain change; decrease
  • (b) increase; uncertain change
  • (c) uncertain change; increase
  • (d) decrease; uncertain change
Answer & working

Answer: (a) uncertain change; decrease  🟢 confident

  1. More suppliers: S shifts right, so P↓ and Q↑.
  2. Substitute is cheaper: buyers switch away, D shifts left, so P↓ and Q↓.
  3. Combine: both push P down, so P definitely falls. Q gets ↑ and ↓, so Q is uncertain (it depends on the size of the shifts).

Method: two simultaneous shifts, T1 D&S. The variable the two shifts agree on is certain; the other is ambiguous.

Q13 1 mark · T6 Climate & games

Which of the following has positive consumption externalities?

  • (a) Smoking.
  • (b) Eating food.
  • (c) Getting the flu vaccine every year.
  • (d) Buying a new IKEA desk.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) Getting the flu vaccine every year.  🟢 confident

A vaccine protects other people too (less transmission). That is a benefit to third parties, so it is a positive externality. The market under-consumes it, and a Pigouvian subsidy fixes that. Smoking is a negative externality. Food and desks are private.

Q16 1 mark · T2 Intervention

A price floor set above the equilibrium price will NOT result in a/an

  • (a) surplus due to excess quantity supplied.
  • (b) deadweight loss.
  • (c) efficient outcome.
  • (d) decrease in consumer surplus.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) efficient outcome.  🟢 confident

A floor above P* is binding. QS > QD (surplus), trade falls to QD (so a DWL appears), and buyers pay more for fewer units (CS falls). The one thing it cannot be is efficient.

“NOT” questions: three options are true, so tick off each one against the diagram.

Q17 1 mark · T2 Intervention

A price ceiling set below the equilibrium price will result in a/an

  • (a) surplus due to excess quantity supplied.
  • (b) shortage due to excess quantity demanded.
  • (c) efficient outcome.
  • (d) increase in producer surplus.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) shortage due to excess quantity demanded.  🟢 confident

A binding ceiling (PC < P*) means QD > QS, so there is a shortage. Trade = QS, PS falls and there is a DWL. This is the lecture's rent-control story.

Q18 1 mark · T2 Intervention

Refer to the Figure. What is the area of the deadweight loss created by the government's implementation of a price ceiling?

  • (a) A.
  • (b) B + C.
  • (c) C + E.
  • (d) None of the above.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) C + E.  🟢 confident

  1. Is it binding? The ceiling is below P*, so yes.
  2. Trade falls to QS at the ceiling (the vertical line through C/E).
  3. Surplus before: CS = A + B + C, PS = D + E + F. After: CS = A + B + D, PS = F.
  4. Lost and gained by nobody: C + E (the triangle between D and S from QS to Q*).
B + C is the trap (a rectangle plus a triangle). The DWL is always the triangle(s) pointing at the old equilibrium.

Q19 1 mark · T2 Intervention

Refer to the Figure. What is the area of the deadweight loss (DWL) created by the government's implementation of a price floor?

  • (a) A.
  • (b) B + C.
  • (c) C + E.
  • (d) None of the above.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) None of the above.  🟡 judgement call

  1. Is it binding? A floor is a legal minimum. It binds only if it is set above P*.
  2. Here it's drawn below P*, so it is non-binding. The market stays at P*, Q*.
  3. No change in Q means DWL = 0. None of A, B + C or C + E is right, so the answer is (d).

🟡 Why a judgement call: the examiner copied Q18's diagram and relabelled the line. Reading it literally, as drawn, the floor is non-binding, so (d). If the floor had been above P*, the DWL would be the two triangles to the left of Q* above P*, and those aren't lettered in this diagram either, so (d) holds either way.

Always check binding vs non-binding first. Ceiling below P* binds. Floor above P* binds. The other way round does nothing.

Q20 1 mark · T6 Climate & games

Game theory can NOT be applied to

  • (a) a firm's behaviour in a perfectly competitive market.
  • (b) negotiating your salary when two potential employers have made you a job offer.
  • (c) deciding whether to share your study notes with friends.
  • (d) two neighbouring fuel stations deciding their fuel prices.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) a firm's behaviour in a perfectly competitive market.  🟢 confident

Game theory needs strategic interdependence: my best move depends on what you do. A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker among thousands of firms. Nothing it does affects anyone else, so there is no game. (b), (c) and (d) are all small-number strategic situations; (d) is the classic duopoly pricing game.

Q23 1 mark · T3 Market power

If a monopolist faces a linear downward-facing demand curve, the firm's marginal revenue curve:

  • (a) lies above the demand curve.
  • (b) lies below the demand curve.
  • (c) intersects the supply curve at the point where it is unit elastic.
  • (d) lies below the average total cost curve at all output quantities.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) lies below the demand curve.  🟢 confident

To sell one more unit the monopolist must cut the price on all units, so MR < P at every Q > 0. For linear D: P = a − bQ gives MR = a − 2bQ. It has the same intercept and twice the slope, so it hits the Q-axis halfway to demand's intercept.

Q24 1 mark · T3 Market power

A monopoly can maximise profits by

  • (a) selling the number of goods where price equals marginal cost.
  • (b) selling the number of goods where marginal revenue equals price.
  • (c) selling the number of goods where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.
  • (d) selling the number of goods where average costs intersects with the demand curve.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) selling the number of goods where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.  🟢 confident

Profit-max rule for every firm: produce Q where MR = MC, then read the price off the demand curve at that Q (L5). P = MC is the competitive (efficient) outcome.

Q26 1 mark · T3 Market power

The following diagram depicts the operating conditions for a profit-maximising monopolist. To maximise profit the monopoly should set price to __ and quantity to __.

  • (a) $20; 5
  • (b) $20; 10
  • (c) $30; 5
  • (d) Cannot answer without more information.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) $30; 5  🟢 confident

  1. Find MR = MC: MR (40 − 4Q) meets MC = 20 at Q = 5.
  2. Go up to demand at Q = 5: P = 40 − 2(5) = $30.
(a) $20 is MC at QM, the price taken off the MR = MC point instead of the demand curve. (b) Q = 10 is where D meets MC, the competitive quantity.

Q27 1 mark · T3 Market power

The following diagram depicts the operating conditions for a profit-maximising monopolist. Calculate the deadweight loss created by this monopoly selling at the profit maximising point.

  • (a) $4.25
  • (b) $6.25
  • (c) $8.25
  • (d) None of the above.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) $6.25  🟢 confident

  1. Equations: D: P = 20 − Q; MR = 20 − 2Q; MC = 5 + Q.
  2. Monopoly: MR = MC ⇒ 20 − 2Q = 5 + Q ⇒ QM = 5; PM = 20 − 5 = $15; MC at QM = $10.
  3. Efficient: D = MC ⇒ 20 − Q = 5 + Q ⇒ QC = 7.5 (P = $12.50). The axis labels 7.5 for exactly this reason.
  4. DWL = triangle between D and MC from 5 to 7.5 = ½ × (7.5 − 5) × (15 − 10) = ½ × 2.5 × 5 = $6.25.
The DWL triangle's height is PM − MC(QM) = 15 − 10, not PM − PC.

Q29 1 mark · T5 Trade

If the Domestic Price is greater than the World Price, then

  • (a) an open economy will protect producer surplus.
  • (b) an open economy will increase producer surplus.
  • (c) an open economy will not affect consumer surplus.
  • (d) an open economy will increase consumer surplus.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) an open economy will increase consumer surplus.  🟢 confident

Pdom > PW means the country will import once open (Heard Island case). The domestic price falls to PW, so buyers gain (CS↑) and producers lose (PS↓). Total surplus rises.

Q30 1 mark · T5 Trade

When a country becomes an exporter of a good, domestic consumer surplus ____ and domestic producer surplus ____

  • (a) increases; increases
  • (b) decreases; decreases
  • (c) increases; decreases
  • (d) decreases; increases
Answer & working

Answer: (d) decreases; increases  🟢 confident

A country exports when PW > domestic P*. Opening up raises the domestic price to PW. Buyers pay more, so CS falls. Sellers get more and sell more, so PS rises by more than CS falls, and total surplus rises. (Lecture: McDonald Island, the exporter case.)

Q32 1 mark · T1 D&S · T3 Market power

Which of the following is NOT a feature of a perfectly competitive market?

  • (a) Lots of buyers and sellers.
  • (b) Sellers are price takers.
  • (c) Sellers are price makers.
  • (d) No barriers to entry.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) Sellers are price makers.  🟢 confident

L5 features: many buyers and sellers, identical products, perfect information, free entry and exit. Each firm is a price taker. Price makers (price searchers) are monopolies and firms with market power.

Q33 1 mark · T3 Market power

In the long-run, perfectly competitive markets will NOT have

  • (a) substitutes.
  • (b) economic profits.
  • (c) many suppliers.
  • (d) perfect information.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) economic profits.  🟢 confident

L5: perfect competition has free entry and exit. If firms earn economic profit, new firms enter, supply shifts right and price falls until P = minimum ATC, where economic profit = 0. Many suppliers and perfect information are defining features, and substitutes exist (identical products are perfect substitutes).

Zero economic profit still lets firms cover all costs, including the opportunity cost of the owner's capital. It is not the same as zero accounting profit.

Q34 1 mark · T5 Trade

What would shift the production possibility curve (PPC) to the right?

  • (a) An increase in the number of people producing the goods.
  • (b) An increase in infrastructure (factories and equipment).
  • (c) An increase in technology and knowledge.
  • (d) All of the above.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) All of the above.  🟢 confident

The PPC shows the maximum output from given resources and technology. More labour (a), more capital (b) and better technology (c) each expand what can be produced, so the curve shifts out. (The same three are the sources of growth in T7 Growth.)

Q36 1 mark · T5 Trade

When a tariff is introduced, the sum of consumer surplus and producer surplus is __ than that of a closed economy and __ than that of a fully open economy.

  • (a) greater; less
  • (b) less; less
  • (c) greater; greater
  • (d) less; greater
Answer & working

Answer: (a) greater; less  🟢 confident

Free trade maximises total surplus. A tariff gives back part of the gains from trade (DWL triangles appear), but the country still imports at a price below the closed P*, so it keeps some gains. CS + PS: closed < tariff < free trade, i.e. greater; less.

Careful with the wording: “CS + PS” excludes tariff revenue. Adding revenue (total surplus) gives the same ranking.

Q37 1 mark · T5 Trade

When a tariff is introduced, producer surplus is ____ than that of a closed economy and ____ than that of a fully open economy.

  • (a) greater; less
  • (b) less; less
  • (c) greater; greater
  • (d) less; greater
Answer & working

Answer: (d) less; greater  🟢 confident

Rank the domestic price: closed P* > PW + tariff > PW (free trade). Domestic producers like a high price, so PS: closed > tariff > free trade. With a tariff, PS is less than closed and greater than fully open.

CS runs the other way (closed < tariff < free trade). Read whether the blank asks about PS or CS. The two 2021 papers swap them.

Q38 1 mark · T1 D&S

Refer to the Figure. What is the consumer surplus?

  • (a) $10
  • (b) $15
  • (c) $22.50
  • (d) $45
Answer & working

Answer: (c) $22.50  🟢 confident

  1. Read the intercepts: D hits P = 7 and Q = 35, so P = 7 − Q/5. S starts at P = 1 and reaches (35, 8), so P = 1 + Q/5.
  2. Equilibrium: 7 − Q/5 = 1 + Q/5 ⇒ Q* = 15, P* = $4. (Check on the grid: the lines cross at (15, 4).)
  3. CS = ½ × base × height = ½ × 15 × (7 − 4) = $22.50.
$45 forgets the ½. The grid has minor ticks every 1 unit on Q, so confirm the crossing at exactly Q = 15.

Q39 1 mark · T2 Intervention

Refer to the Figure. If a $2 per unit tax was charged to suppliers, how much tax revenue will the government receive?

  • (a) $20
  • (b) $15
  • (c) $10
  • (d) $30
Answer & working

Answer: (a) $20  🟢 confident

  1. Read the curves: D: P = 7 − Q/5; S: P = 1 + Q/5. No-tax equilibrium: Q* = 15, P* = $4.
  2. A $2 tax on suppliers shifts S up by $2: P = 3 + Q/5.
  3. New equilibrium: 7 − Q/5 = 3 + Q/5 ⇒ QT = 10. Buyers pay PB = 7 − 2 = $5, sellers keep PS = $3.
  4. Revenue = tax × QT = 2 × 10 = $20.

Bonus: DWL = ½ × 2 × (15 − 10) = $5. The curves have equal and opposite slopes, so the burden is split evenly: $1 to buyers and $1 to sellers. Method: T2 Intervention.

$30 = 2 × 15 uses the old quantity. Revenue always uses the quantity after the tax.

Q40 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Refer to the figure. Suppose the economy is at point D. A one-time unanticipated decrease in government spending will shift __ and move the economy along __, meaning that the inflation rate is __ and output is __ in the short run.

  • (a) AD1 to AD2; SRAS1; decreasing; decreasing
  • (b) SRAS2 to SRAS1; AD1; increasing; decreasing
  • (c) LRAS; AD1; decreasing; decreasing
  • (d) AD2 to AD1; SRAS1; increasing; increasing
Answer & working

Answer: (a) AD1 to AD2; SRAS1; decreasing; decreasing  🟢 confident

  1. G is a component of AD (AD = C + I + G + NX). G↓ shifts AD₁ → AD₂ (left).
  2. In the short run SRAS doesn't move, so the economy slides along SRAS₁ from D to C.
  3. At C: lower on the vertical axis (inflation ↓) and left of Y* (output ↓), a recessionary gap.
The 2021 course put the inflation rate on the vertical axis. 2026 uses the price level P. The logic of shifts is identical; just read “inflation ↓” as “P ↓”.

Q41 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Refer to the Figure. If the prices increase, aggregate output in the long run is going to

  • (a) shift to the right.
  • (b) shift to the left.
  • (c) be constant.
  • (d) change slope.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) be constant.  🟢 confident

LRAS is vertical at potential output. In the long run, output is set by resources and technology, not by the price level. So higher prices leave long-run output unchanged.

Q42 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

With the imposition of stricter immigration laws, the amount of foreign workers is expected to decrease. How would this affect the economy in the long run?

  • (a) The short-run aggregate supply curve will shift to the left.
  • (b) The long-run aggregate supply curve will shift to the left.
  • (c) The economy will move down along the short-run aggregate supply curve.
  • (d) The economy will move down along the long-run aggregate supply curve.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) The long-run aggregate supply curve will shift to the left.  🟢 confident

L6: LRAS (potential GDP) is “determined solely by the economy's access to factors of production, e.g. labour force, capital”. Fewer workers mean less potential output, so LRAS shifts left. The question says “in the long run”, so the answer is about LRAS, not SRAS.

In the 2026 lecture, immigration also appears as an AD shifter (2022: population↑ ⇒ C↑). If a question asks about the short-run spending effect, it's AD. If it asks about the labour force or the long run, it's LRAS.

Q43 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Which of the following would NOT cause the Australian AD curve to shift to the right?

  • (a) The U.S. wants to import more iron ore from Australia.
  • (b) Australian Government spending increases.
  • (c) The inflation rate in Australia increases.
  • (d) Australians buy more and save less.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) The inflation rate in Australia increases.  🟢 confident

(a) X↑ (NX↑), (b) G↑ and (d) C↑ are all AD components, so they shift AD right. A change in the inflation rate (or price level) is the variable on the vertical axis. It moves the economy along AD; it does not shift it.

Same rule as micro: a change in the variable on the axis is a movement along the curve, never a shift.

Q44 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Suppose the Australian economy is currently in the expansionary stage of the business cycle. Which policy could the Government adopt to bring the economy back towards long run equilibrium?

  • (a) Decrease income tax rates for all workers.
  • (b) Increase the cash rate by 25 basis points.
  • (c) Delay planned construction projects.
  • (d) Decrease taxes on cigarettes.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) Delay planned construction projects.  🟡 judgement call

Expansionary stage (Y > Y*) calls for contractionary policy (AD left). (a) and (d) cut taxes, which is expansionary, so they're wrong. Both (b) and (c) are contractionary. But (b) is monetary policy, set by the RBA (L7: “The RBA controls the Cash Rate”). The Government controls fiscal policy: delaying construction cuts G. So (c).

🟡 There is no answer key. The word “Government” is the discriminator. If a 2026 question says “policymakers” or “the RBA”, the cash-rate rise is the answer.

Who does what: Government = fiscal (G, T); RBA = monetary (cash rate). 2026 questions use this split in their dropdowns.

Q45 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Which of the following counts towards Australia's GDP?

  • (a) Packaging machines in a factory depreciate.
  • (b) Bob's grandma receives pensions from the government every month.
  • (c) A criminal sells drugs on the black market.
  • (d) Zach sells his second-hand car to Victoria without earning profits.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) A criminal sells drugs on the black market.  🟡 judgement call

GDP = market value of final goods and services newly produced in the period (L6). (a) Depreciation is wear on existing capital, not new production. (b) A pension is a transfer (no good or service is produced in exchange). (d) A second-hand car was counted when it was produced. Only (c) is a new final good sold in a market. The ABS does estimate illicit drug production in the national accounts (since 2014, under the 2008 SNA).

🟡 Many textbooks say the underground economy is hard to measure (a limitation of GDP). But it is the only option that is new production, so (c) is the intended answer by elimination.

Q46 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

If nominal GDP rises we can conclude that

  • (a) prices have risen and production remains constant.
  • (b) production has risen and prices remain constant.
  • (c) production has risen or prices have risen, or both have risen.
  • (d) prices have fallen and production has risen.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) production has risen or prices have risen, or both have risen.  🟢 confident

Nominal GDP = Σ P × Q at current prices. A rise can come from P↑, Q↑ or both. You can't separate them without real GDP or a price index. (Strictly, one could even fall if the other rises by more, but (c) is the only safe conclusion offered.)

Q47 1 mark · T4 AD-AS · T7 Growth

One reason why economists do NOT consider GDP the perfect measure of well-being of a society is because

  • (a) GDP is not adjusted for pollution.
  • (b) GDP is adjusted for the increase in crime rates.
  • (c) GDP includes the value of leisure.
  • (d) GDP cannot account for inflation.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) GDP is not adjusted for pollution.  🟢 confident

GDP counts market production. It ignores environmental damage (pollution), leisure, home production and distribution. (b) and (c) are false statements (GDP makes neither adjustment). (d) is false because real GDP does adjust for inflation.

Q49 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Which of the following is NOT true about monetary policy?

  • (a) Conventional monetary policy may be ineffective in some specific economic situations.
  • (b) The effects of monetary policy have time lags.
  • (c) Monetary policy can change the interest rate in the mortgage market directly.
  • (d) Monetary policy cannot be directed to boost an industry or region.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) Monetary policy can change the interest rate in the mortgage market directly.  🟢 confident

L7: the RBA sets the cash rate (the overnight interbank rate). That changes banks' funding costs, which in turn influences mortgage and loan rates. This is indirect, not direct, so (c) is the false statement. (a) is true (e.g. near-zero rates), (b) is true (lags), and (d) is true (one rate for the whole country).

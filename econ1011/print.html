Past-paper patterns: the slot-by-slot checklist

From the practice exam (official key) and the S1 2026 final (lecturer-solved in L13), plus our solutions to the S2 2025 and S1 2025 finals. Area letters change between papers, so always rebuild areas from the price lines rather than memorising letters.

§1 D&S / intervention (T1, T2) · 14–20 marks
  1. Read P*, Q* off the grid. Count the minor gridlines and double-check the scale.
  2. Story → which curve. Buyers' circumstances (people, income, mortgage help, a substitute's price ↑, a ban on a substitute) shift D. Costs, inputs, building materials and rates (for landlords) shift S. "Production cost unaffected" means S doesn't move.
  3. Both shift. D↑ and S↑ give Q↑ with P uncertain; D↑ and S↓ give P↑ with Q uncertain.
  4. Ceiling/floor. A ceiling binds only below P*, and a floor binds only above P*. Quantity traded = the short side (QS under a ceiling). Black-market price = the D height at QS. Excess supply/demand = QS − QD at the controlled price. The decoy is the old P* or the long side.
  5. Tax diagram (P1–P4). Buyers pay the top price, sellers get the price on the original S at the new Q, and the consumer burden is PB − old P*. The drag label "upper line" means S + tax.
  6. Elasticity, point method. ε = (1/slope) × (P/Q), kept negative. The inverse value is the decoy (−0.8 vs −1.25).
  7. Two markets, same shock. The more elastic side takes more of the change in Q; the less elastic side takes more of the change in P. Whoever is more elastic bears less of a tax.
  8. "Reduce consumption" policy. A floor above P* works by reducing quantity demanded, not demand.
§2 Monopoly (T3) · 8–12 marks
  1. QM from MR = MC, then go up to D for PM. The decoy is the D = MC point.
  2. Competitive Q is where D = MC. Under-production = QC − QM.
  3. Mark-up = PM − MC(QM).
  4. Click the DWL. It's the triangle between D and MC from QM to QC, never anything involving MR.
  5. Elasticity at the monopoly point. Use the point method and keep the sign; it is always elastic (|ε| > 1).
  6. "Is demand elastic there?" Yes. When demand is inelastic, MR < 0, while MC ≥ 0, so MR = MC can't happen there.
  7. "Natural monopoly" = economies of scale. The DWL can be worded as "units with positive social surplus not produced".
§3 AD-AS (T4) · 12–20 marks
  1. Click the SR equilibrium at AD ∩ SRAS, not on LRAS.
  2. Gap type. Left of LRAS means below potential, which needs expansionary policy. Right of LRAS means above potential, which needs contractionary policy.
  3. Policy pick: filter by TYPE first, then direction. Fiscal = G, taxes, transfers. Monetary = interest/cash rate, money supply.
  4. Single event → curve:
    • G↑ or tax cut → AD.
    • Confidence, export demand, immigration → AD.
    • Oil or input costs → SRAS.
    • Immigration of workers → also SRAS↑.
    • Income tax↑ → AD↓.
    • The trap is answering "SRAS" for a G or tax change.
  5. Two shocks at once:
    • AD↓ and SRAS↓ → Y↓, P uncertain.
    • AD↑ and SRAS↓ → P↑, Y uncertain.
    • AD↑ and SRAS↑ → Y↑, P uncertain.
  6. Written "which dominates". Name the bigger shift, then state its effect on Y and P. If a policy is asked for, name the objective (Y or P), the tool and its cost.
  7. Self-correction. Below potential, wages fall and SRAS rises (shifts right).
§4a Comparative advantage (T5) · ~12 marks
  1. The table is LABOUR HOURS per unit (input table).
    • Absolute advantage = fewer hours.
    • OC of X = hrsX / hrsY.
    • Comparative advantage = lower OC.
    • Each country usually has one comparative advantage.
  2. PPC intercepts = total hours ÷ hours per unit.
  3. Terms of trade must lie STRICTLY between the two OCs of the same good. Convert options like "2 for 5" to a per-unit rate first. An option outside the range means no trade.
  4. Bundle checks:
    • Without trade: is it within the hours? If not, it's above the PPC.
    • With trade: does it fit the CPC from the specialised intercept at the ToT?
    • Exclude bundles that were already feasible without trade.
  5. Exports/imports. Specialise fully, then exports = production − consumption. Check that exports ÷ imports = the ToT.
§4b Tariff (T5) · 8–12 marks
  1. Imports = QD − QS at the domestic price, which is PW (free trade) or P1+t (with tariff).
  2. This is a large country: the world price FALLS with a tariff (P0 → P1).
    • Domestic price = P1 + t.
    • Revenue = t × imports; part of it is the terms-of-trade gain (the rectangle below P0).
  3. CS loss = everything between P0 and P1+t, left of D.
  4. PS after = the area above S and below P1+t.
  5. Net welfare = ToT gain − the two DWL triangles.
    • "Can it gain?" Yes, if the ToT rectangle exceeds the triangles.
    • "No regardless" is wrong.
    • Remove the tariff if the DWL is bigger than the gain.
§5a Externality (T6) · ~10 marks
  1. Identify the type.
    • SMC above PMC = negative, which over-produces.
    • SMB above PMB = positive, which under-produces.
  2. Find the two quantities.
    • Market Q is where the private curves cross.
    • Optimum Q is where the social curves cross.
  3. Pigouvian tax/subsidy = the gap between social and private curves AT THE OPTIMAL Q. The decoy is the gap at the market Q (e.g. $6 vs $5, $13 vs $12, 400 vs 600), which differs when the curves aren't parallel.
  4. DWL = ½ × (Q gap) × (vertical gap at the market Q).
  5. "Subsidise if DWL > admin cost": compute the DWL and compare.
§5b Game theory (T6) · 10 marks, always
  1. Row player: use the 1st numbers and compare down each column. Column player: use the 2nd numbers and compare across each row.
  2. Written template (4 marks): "If B plays X, A gets a from P and b from Q, so A's best response is __. If B plays Y, A gets c from P and d from Q, so A's best response is __. Since __ is the best response to every strategy of B, it is A's dominant strategy." Or: "Since A's best response depends on B's choice, A has no dominant strategy."
  3. Nash = the cell where both payoffs are starred. Write it as (row, column). If one player has a dominant strategy, the NE is that strategy plus the other player's best response to it.
§6 Solow (T7) · 10–20 marks
  1. Click the steady state at sf(k) ∩ (n+δ)k, not the tangency and not f(k).
  2. Golden Rule. Find where the tangent to f(k) is parallel to (n+δ)k.
    • k* right of kG means saving too much.
    • k* left of kG means saving too little, so raise s.
  3. SS consumption = f(k*) − sf(k*), the vertical gap at k*. The decoy is the gap at kG.
  4. Parameter changes:
    • n↑ or δ↑ → "maintenance line, up" → k* lower.
    • n↓ → down → k* higher.
    • s↑ → saving curve up → k* higher.
    • A↑ → output (and saving) up.
  5. Starting from k₀ > k*: required investment exceeds actual investment, so k falls to k* and y falls to y*.
Sometimes: GDP calc (2024) and drag-the-labels
  • Nominal GDP = Σ (this year's price × this year's quantity).
  • Real GDP = Σ (base-year price × this year's quantity). In the base year, real = nominal.
  • "Excluding inflation" means use real growth = (realt − realt−1) / realt−1. The decoy is nominal growth.
  • GDP deflator = nominal/real × 100 (only in 2024, not lectured in 2026).
  • Drag labels: on the AD-AS diagram the axes are Price level and Real output (decoys: interest rate, capital per capita). On the Solow diagram the axes are k and y, the curves are f(k), sf(k) and (n+δ)k, and c = f − sf at k*.
  • Poverty trap (2024 S2) = high n and low y. To escape, lower n (the break-even line pivots down) or raise s.

Definitions
  • QD: qty buyers want to buy at a price (per period). Demand: the whole P–QD relationship.
  • QS: qty sellers want to sell. Supply: the P–QS relationship.
  • Law of demand: P↑ → QD↓ (substitution effect, income effect, buyers exit).
  • Law of supply: P↑ → QS↑ (intensive margin: more profitable; extensive margin: entry).
  • Increase in D = right = up (pay more for the same Q). Increase in S = right = down (accept less for the same Q). Decrease in S = left = up.
  • Competitive equilibrium: no one wants to change decision given P. QD* = QS* at P*. Also called market clearing.
  • Perfect competition: many buyers and sellers (price-takers), homogeneous good, perfect information, free entry/exit.
  • Opportunity cost: value of the next-best alternative forgone.
  • Economic surplus = Benefits − Explicit − Implicit costs.
  • Stock (at a point in time: house) vs flow (per period: rent). Assets = stocks generating flows. Rent = opportunity cost of housing.
  • Housing affordability = housing costs/household income × 100%. Stress > 30% (bottom 40% of income).

Definitions
  • Price control: legally binding restriction on price. Ceiling = max (rent cap/freeze). Floor = min (min wage $26.44/hr; employers = buyers of labour).
  • Binding = constrains the price ("effective"). Non-binding has no economic impact.
  • Unit tax: $ per unit (tobacco excise $1.53/cigarette). Analytically a negative subsidy.
  • Subsidy: govt payment to encourage production/consumption. Unit = $ per unit (course uses this). Ad-valorem = % of price. Paying it to sellers or buyers gives the same market outcome.
  • Quota: cap on quantity, so S becomes vertical at the quota.
  • CS = area under D, above PB, up to Q. PS = area above S, below PS, up to Q.
  • SS = CS + PS (+ tax rev − subsidy cost) = total benefit (area under D) − production cost (area under S).
  • DWL = SS lost vs competitive Q*. Comes from under- OR over-production.

Elasticity (lecturer's method)
  • εD = %ΔQD/%ΔP, negative. Use |εD| only if told.
  • Point formula: εD = (P/Q) ÷ (ΔP/ΔQ) = slope of ray ÷ slope of D = (1/slope)·(P/Q).
  • Linear D: slope = −(P-int)/(Q-int). Midpoint → ε = −1, MR = 0, TR max.
  • %ΔQD = εD × %ΔP. %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ.
  • Elastic: P ↓ gives TR ↑. Inelastic: P ↓ gives TR ↓.
  • Vertical = perfectly inelastic (0). Horizontal = perfectly elastic (∞).
  • More elastic D: more substitutes, longer time, luxury, big budget share.
  • εS = %ΔQS/%ΔP > 0 ("don't worry about calculating"). Housing S is inelastic in the short run.
  • Examples: (Q 6, P 8), slope −2 → (8/6)/(−2) = −2/3. (Q 8, P 12), slope −1 → −3/2. (Q 36, P 64), slope −1 → −16/9.
Incidence rule: the LESS elastic side bears MORE of a tax and gets MORE of a subsidy. Perfectly inelastic side bears it all. Check: buyers' share ≈ εS/(εS + |εD|) (textbook, not on slides).

Game theory — payoff matrix procedure (M6)

Definitions (L11)
Game = players, actions, payoffs. 2×2 simultaneous-move.
Convention: Player 1 = rows, Player 2 = columns; cell = outcome; 1st number → row player, 2nd → column player.
Best response: action with highest payoff given the opponent's action (mark with *).
Dominant strategy: action that is always a best response to all of the opponent's actions.
Nash equilibrium: outcome where all players play best responses to each other's chosen actions (self-enforcing; nobody wants to change). = cell with two stars. Can be more than one.
Prisoner's Dilemma: both have dominant strategy + resulting outcome collectively inefficient.
Coordination game: 2 NE (good & bad); need trust/coordination device.
IEDS: remove dominated action, best-respond to what's left → NE.
Steps
  1. Write: Row = __ = 1st no.; Col = __ = 2nd no.
  2. Row player: for each column, compare 1st numbers up/down; star larger.
  3. Column player: for each row, compare 2nd numbers left/right; star larger.
  4. Row's stars in the same row → dominant = that row. Col's stars in the same column → dominant. Else "no dominant strategy".
  5. NE = every cell with both numbers starred; name it (Row action, Col action), never by payoffs.
  6. Check: one dominant → NE = (that action, other's BR to it). Better cell for both? → PD inefficiency.
  7. Ties: star both.
"Row looks DOWN a column at 1st numbers; Column looks ACROSS a row at 2nd numbers."
Written answer (4 marks)
"Yes/No. If B plays X: R1 gives A __, R2 gives A __ → __ is A's best response to X. If B plays Y: R1 gives A __, R2 gives A __ → __ is A's best response to Y. Since __ is the best response to each and every one of B's actions, it is A's dominant strategy. / Since A's best response differs with B's action, A has no dominant strategy."

Definitions
  • GDP per capita = GDP / Population = average income (ignores distribution).
  • Economic growth = long-run growth of real GDP per capita.
  • Labour productivity y = Y/L (output per worker or per hour).
  • Capital = physical, durable produced goods (machines, buildings, roads), not human or financial.
  • Technology = intangible methods turning inputs into outputs. Progress = more output from the same inputs (need not need new machines).
  • TFP (A) = catch-all efficiency: technology, human capital, rainfall, legal system.
  • α = capital share of income; 1−α = labour share.
  • Break-even / required investment / maintenance = (δ+n)k.
  • Steady state: Δk = 0 ⇒ Δy = 0.
  • Golden Rule kG*: the SS k that maximises SS consumption per worker. sG is the saving rate that delivers it.
Formulas
  • Y/Pop = (Y/Hours) × (Hours/L) × (L/Pop)
  • Cobb-Douglas: Y = A KαL1−α ⇒ y = A kα, k = K/L
  • Saving = investment = sY; C = (1−s)Y; s = 1 ⇒ C = 0
  • ΔK = sY − δK; %Δk = %ΔK − %ΔL; %ΔL = n
  • Δk = s·y − (δ+n)k (δk = depreciation, nk = dilution)
  • SS: s·f(k*) = (δ+n)k*; y* = f(k*)
  • c = y − sy; c* = f(k*) − (δ+n)k* = y* − sy*
  • Δc* = (MPK − (δ+n))·Δk*, MPK = slope of f
  • Golden Rule: MPK = δ + n (f(k) parallel to (δ+n)k)
  • If y = A√k: k* = (sA/(δ+n))²; kG = (A/(2(δ+n)))²; sG = 0.5 (in general sG = α, a derived check)
  • In SS, aggregate K and Y grow at rate n (per-worker growth = 0)
  • n = birth rate − death rate

Transition rules
  • k < k*: sf(k) > (δ+n)k ⇒ Δk > 0 ⇒ k↑, y↑ towards k*, y*.
  • k > k*: (δ+n)k > sf(k) ⇒ Δk < 0 ⇒ k↓, y↓ towards k*, y*.
  • Growth slows as k → k* (diminishing returns): further below SS means faster growth, which is how late-comers catch up.
Golden Rule rules
  • At k*: f steeper than maintenance (MPK > δ+n) ⇒ k* < kG ⇒ too little saving ⇒ raise s.
  • f flatter (MPK < δ+n) ⇒ k* > kG ⇒ too much ⇒ cut s.
  • Equal slopes ⇒ Golden Rule ⇒ optimal.
  • Too low s traps poor countries; too high s means too little consumption (China, "Is thrift a virtue?").
Poverty trap / Malthus
  • Malthus: income gains are diluted by population growth ⇒ poverty inevitable (may hold in under-developed countries).
  • Low y: high births and high deaths ⇒ low n. Middle y: deaths fall fast, births steady ⇒ high n. High y: births fall (opportunity cost of children) ⇒ low n.
  • Break-even n(k)·k is S-shaped ⇒ 3 equilibria. A = trap (high n, low y), C = ideal (low n, high y), B = unstable threshold.
  • Escape: (1) raise s, temporarily if needed (s·f(k) up, removes A); (2) population control (n·k down).
  • Institutions: poor property rights (corruption, war, bureaucracy) and broken financial markets mean low investment and low growth.
Technology & middle-income trap
  • A↑ ⇒ k*↑ ⇒ incentive for more R&D: a positive feedback loop sustains growth.
  • Middle-income trap: growth from capital accumulation alone stalls. Caused by protectionism (no foreign capital goods, no competition) and centralised projects instead of innovation.
  • Fix: trade (import the latest tech) + domestic R&D incentives.
  • Ford assembly line = technology without new capital (12 h → 90 min; $850 → $300).
Mini procedure: numeric SS
  1. Δk at a given k: y = f(k); s·y; (δ+n)k; subtract.
  2. SS: s·f(k) = (δ+n)k. With A√k: k* = (sA/(δ+n))².
  3. y* = f(k*); c* = (1−s)y*; check s·y* = (δ+n)k*.
  4. Golden: slope A/(2√k) = δ+n.

Ex: s=0.3, δ+n=0.15, A=1 ⇒ k*=4, y*=2, c*=1.4; kG=11.1, cG=1.67.

The big idea

In a competitive market, buyers and sellers are price-takers and the price is set by the interaction of demand (buyers' willingness and ability to buy) and supply (sellers' willingness and ability to sell). The equilibrium price P* is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. When something other than the good's own price changes, a curve shifts and P* and Q* move to a new intersection. Predicting the direction of those moves (comparative statics) is the main point, not forecasting exact numbers. The exam tests this on grid diagrams: read values off the graph, pick which curve shifts and which way, and work out the price elasticity at a point.

The lecture's story: Why did Australian rents surge from 2020 to 2023? Demand for rentals rose (smaller households after COVID work-from-home, about 120,000 extra households, plus migrants returning: about 513,000 net in the year to Mar 2023). Supply of rentals fell (RBA rate rises from 0.1% to 4.35% raised landlords' opportunity cost, and construction costs rose 11.9% in 2022). Both push rent up. The effect on quantity is ambiguous. Housing supply is inelastic in the short run, which is why the price moves were so big.

0 · Foundations from Lecture 1 (examinable ideas)

Scarcity, opportunity cost, micro vs macro. You know these already. Skim them, but learn the lecturer's surplus formula exactly.
  • Scarcity principle: unlimited wants and finite resources, so every choice is a trade-off.
  • Microeconomics studies one market or a few markets (individual households and firms, how buyers and sellers interact, government intervention in single markets). Macroeconomics studies the whole economy (GDP, unemployment, inflation, growth).
  • Opportunity cost = the value of the next-best alternative forgone (only the best alternative, not all of them added up).
  • Cost–benefit principle: do something only if Benefits > Costs.
  • Economic surplus = Benefits − (Explicit costs + Implicit costs). Explicit costs involve money changing hands. Implicit costs are the opportunity cost of resources you use (for example your time), with no money changing hands.
Worked example (lecture-style): ship routes. Profits: Singapore $13m, Bangkok $15m, Perth $14m.
Opp. cost of Singapore = best alternative = Bangkok $15m. Opp. cost of Bangkok = Perth $14m. Opp. cost of Perth = Bangkok $15m.
Bangkok is the only route where profit > opportunity cost, so it is the best choice. The best choice is the one with the lowest opportunity cost.
Worked example: TV in the city. Save $150 by buying in the city. Train $20 (explicit). 2 hours × $70/h = $140 of time (implicit).
Economic surplus of going to the city = 150 − 20 − 140 = −$10, so buy locally. If you ignored the implicit cost you would wrongly get +$130.

1 · Markets & perfect competition

Market = buyers and sellers of a particular good or service, plus the arrangements by which they trade. Prices are set by a combination of producers' costs and buyers' value. Neither one alone explains them: Banksy's shredded painting sold far above its cost, and salt sells far below its value to buyers.

Perfect competition features (4)Meaning
1. Many buyers & many sellerseach one is small relative to the market, so all are price-takers
2. Homogeneous goodsidentical products, no quality differences
3. Perfect informationeveryone knows price and quality
4. Free entry & exitno patents blocking entry, no clean-up costs blocking exit

For the application, the lecturer says the defining test is whether participants are price-takers. The rental market is treated as competitive "as a first pass". Example competitive markets: foreign exchange, coffee, pens. Not competitive: supermarkets, banks.

Housing-specific ideas from Lecture 2:
  • Two related markets: residential properties (buying and selling houses and land) and residential rentals (buying and selling the right to use a dwelling).
  • Stock vs flow: a stock is measured at a point in time (balance sheet: savings balance, a house). A flow is measured over a period (income statement: savings per month, rent per week). Assets are stocks that generate flows. A house's value depends on the rental flow it can generate.
  • Rent is the opportunity cost of housing, even for an owner with no mortgage, because they give up the rent they could earn. So the analysis focuses on the rental market.
  • Housing affordability measure = Housing costs ÷ Household income × 100% (really an unaffordability measure). Housing stress means spending >30% of gross income on housing, most relevant for the bottom 40% of households. Brisbane 2020–23: income +10%, median rent +38%, house prices +56%.
Example: rent $600/wk, gross income $1,800/wk → 600/1800 × 100% = 33.3% > 30%, so the household is in housing stress.

2 · Demand

Quantity demanded (QD) = the quantity buyers want to buy at a given price over a period. Demand = the whole relationship between price and quantity demanded (shown as a table, an equation or a graph).

Law of demand: P↑ ⇒ QD↓ (demand curves slope down). The lecture gives three reasons:

  • Substitution effect: buyers switch to other, relatively cheaper products.
  • Income effect: buyers can afford less of it (for normal goods).
  • Market level: fewer buyers, because some exit the market.

Shift in demand: at the same price, buyers want a different quantity. An increase in demand is a rightward shift. Equivalently it is an upward shift: buyers will pay more for each quantity. Because D slopes down, "right" and "up" describe the same shift.

Shifter (non-price factor)Increase in demand (D → right/up) when…
Number of buyers / populationpopulation rises (e.g. migration, more households)
Preferences / desire for the goodstronger desire (e.g. work-from-home → want more space)
Price of a substitute in consumptionthe substitute's price rises
Price of a complement in consumptionthe complement's price falls
Income (lecture mentions "tastes, incomes etc.")income rises, for a normal good
The QCAA list also includes expectations and inferior goods. The lecturer only names population, preferences, related goods and (in passing) income. If an exam option relies on income, assume the good is normal unless told otherwise.

Substitutes & complements in consumption (buyers' view)

  • Substitutes serve the same or a similar purpose. Examples: Coke and Pepsi, rentals in St Lucia and in Toowong, coffee and bubble tea. If P of the substitute rises, demand for X increases.
  • Complements are used together. Examples: forks, spoons and knives, St Lucia rentals and the St Lucia–Toowong bus. If P of the complement rises, demand for X decreases.
Example (S1 2026 exam style, fresh numbers): a bad coffee-bean harvest raises the price of coffee. Coffee and bubble tea are substitutes in consumption, so the demand curve for bubble tea shifts right. The equilibrium price of bubble tea increases and quantity increases. Bubble tea's own supply does not move, because its production costs are unaffected.

3 · Price elasticity of demand (εD)

Elasticity measures how responsive QD is to price. Slope is not used for this because slope depends on units: switching from kg to g makes the curve 1000× flatter. Elasticity is unit-free.

εD = %ΔQD / %ΔP, where %ΔQD = ΔQD/QD × 100% and %ΔP = ΔP/P × 100%.
Point formula used in this course: εD = (P/QD) ÷ (ΔP/ΔQD) = slope of ray from origin ÷ slope of demand curve = (P/Q) × 1/slope.
Rearranged: %ΔQD = εD × %ΔP.
Sign convention: εD is negative (it is <0 by the law of demand). The lecturer sometimes uses |εD| but says "you'll need to clearly indicate whether you are using the absolute value". Past exams say "do not use the absolute value" and offer both −1.25 and 1.25, so give the negative number unless told otherwise. The slope is ΔP/ΔQ (rise over run on the P–Q diagram), so it is negative for D. Answers can be left as fractions (e.g. −16/9). The course uses the point method throughout and never uses the midpoint formula.
|εD|NameMeaning (1% price rise…)Curve
0Perfectly inelasticQD doesn't changevertical
<1InelasticQD falls by <1%; buyers not price-sensitivesteeper (at a given point)
=1Unitarily elasticQD falls by exactly 1%midpoint of linear D
>1ElasticQD falls by >1%; buyers price-sensitiveflatter (at a given point)
∞Perfectly elasticany price rise kills QDhorizontal

"The steeper the demand curve, the relatively more inelastic is demand." This holds when you compare curves through the same point. Along a single straight line the slope is constant but ε still changes.

Example: εD = −0.5 and price rises 10% → %ΔQD = −0.5 × 10% = −5% (inelastic).
εD = −3 and price rises 1% → QD falls 3% (elastic).

4 · Supply

Quantity supplied (QS) = the quantity sellers want to produce and sell at a given price over a period. Supply = the relationship between P and QS. Law of supply: P↑ ⇒ QS↑ (supply curves slope up). There are two reasons:

  • Intensive margin (each supplier): it is more profitable to sell a higher quantity.
  • Extensive margin (market): new suppliers enter.

Price elasticity of supply εS = %ΔQS/%ΔP (positive). The categories mirror demand: perfectly inelastic is vertical, perfectly elastic is horizontal, and the boundary is 1. The lecturer said "don't worry about calculating it". You do need the idea for comparative statics (see §6).

Shift in supply: at the same price, firms want to sell a different quantity. An increase in supply is a rightward shift. Equivalently it is a downward shift: firms accept a lower price for each quantity because the cost of producing each quantity has fallen. A decrease in supply is a leftward shift, which is the same as an upward shift.

ShifterIncrease in supply (S → right/down) when…
Number of sellersmore sellers
Production cost (input prices, wages, materials, interest)costs fall
Price of a substitute in productionits price falls
Price of a complement in productionits price rises
The QCAA list also includes technology, weather and expectations. All of these work through "production cost" or "number of sellers".

Substitutes & complements in production (sellers' view)

QCAA usually only covers consumption substitutes and complements. The course adds the production versions, and the direction is reversed for substitutes.
  • Substitutes in production use the same or similar inputs. Examples: liquor (vodka, whisky) and hand sanitiser, long-term rentals and short-term rentals (Airbnb). If P of the production substitute rises, supply of X decreases, because producers move inputs to the more lucrative good.
  • Complements in production are produced together. Example: chicken breasts and chicken wings. If P of the production complement rises, supply of X increases, because more chickens get processed.
Example: Airbnb nightly rates soar. Short-term and long-term rentals are substitutes in production, so the supply of long-term rentals decreases (shifts left). Long-term rent rises and quantity falls.
Example: the price of chicken wings jumps. Wings are a complement in production to breasts, so the supply of breasts increases. The price of breasts falls and quantity rises.

Why rental supply fell: opportunity cost of renting out (Lecture 3)

Instead of renting the property out for a year, a landlord could sell it, invest the proceeds at interest rate r, and buy it back a year later.
Opportunity cost of renting out = Pcurrent(1 + r) − Pfuture
This cost is higher when Pcurrent is higher, when r is higher, or when the expected Pfuture is lower. A higher opportunity cost means a decrease in rental supply (S shifts left). Interest-rate rises therefore show up here as a supply shock to rentals. Higher construction costs reduce the supply of new houses, which raises Pcurrent, which raises the opportunity cost, which reduces rental supply.
Example: Pcurrent = $800,000, Pfuture = $820,000.
At r = 5%: 800,000 × 1.05 − 820,000 = 840,000 − 820,000 = $20,000.
At r = 6%: 800,000 × 1.06 − 820,000 = 848,000 − 820,000 = $28,000. The higher opportunity cost means the supply of rentals decreases, so rent ↑.

5 · Equilibrium

Equilibrium (in economics): nobody wants to change their decision, given the other decision makers' choices. Competitive equilibrium: no buyer or seller wants to change their buying or selling decision given the market price. It is described by P* and Q* = QD* = QS* at the intersection of D and S. This is also called market clearing: everything offered for sale gets bought.

Price is…SituationWhat happens
above P*Excess supply (QS > QD)sellers can't sell all they want, so they undercut each other and P falls
below P*Excess demand (QD > QS), a shortagefrustrated buyers bid more, so P rises
= P*QD = QSno incentive to change, so P stays at P* until there is a shock

The amount of excess demand or supply is the horizontal gap |QD − QS| at that price.

Algebra example. QD = 120 − 2P, QS = 4P − 30.
1. Set QD = QS: 120 − 2P = 4P − 30 → 150 = 6P → P* = 25.
2. Substitute back: Q* = 120 − 2(25) = 70. Check: 4(25) − 30 = 70 ✓.
3. At P = 30: QD = 60, QS = 90, so excess supply of 30. At P = 20: QD = 80, QS = 50, so excess demand of 30.

Why care? The lecturer says the purpose is not to forecast exact numbers, because the curves can't be observed directly and keep shifting. The purpose is to predict the direction P and Q move when circumstances change.

Simultaneous shifts

When both curves shift, one of P or Q has a definite direction and the other is uncertain. Which one is uncertain depends on the relative sizes of the shifts.

Both shiftP*Q*Lecture / exam example
D↑ and S↓↑uncertainAustralian rentals 2020–23 (lecture)

D↓ and S↓uncertain↓
D↓ and S↑↓uncertain
Trick: work out each shift's effect on P and on Q separately. Where the two effects agree, that variable's direction is certain. Where they conflict, it is uncertain. If the question gives you the new curves on a grid, it is no longer uncertain. Read off the new intersection.
Numeric example (grid D: P = 10 − 0.01Q, S: P = 2 + 0.01Q; E0: P = 6, Q = 400).
D shifts up by $2 (P = 12 − 0.01Q) only: 12 − 0.01Q = 2 + 0.01Q → Q = 500, P = 7.
S shifts up by $2 (P = 4 + 0.01Q) only: 10 − 0.01Q = 4 + 0.01Q → Q = 300, P = 7.
Both shift: 12 − 0.01Q = 4 + 0.01Q → Q = 400 (unchanged here), P = 8. Price definitely rose. Quantity happened to stay the same because the two shifts were equal in size.

Role of elasticity in how the adjustment splits

  • When demand shifts, the market moves along the supply curve. If S is inelastic, most of the adjustment is in price. Short-run housing supply is inelastic, which is why rents jumped.
  • When supply shifts, the market moves along the demand curve. If D is less elastic, there is a bigger price change and a smaller quantity change. If D is more elastic, there is a smaller price change and a bigger quantity change. Past exams test this as "which of two markets has the bigger change".

7 · Consumer, producer & social surplus

Surplus areas are taught in Lecture 5 but are built on D and S, and the exam reads them off grids. The lecturer derives them from marginal benefit and marginal cost.
  • Consumer surplus CS = Total consumer benefits − P×Q. The demand curve is the marginal benefit (MB) curve, because MB of a unit is the most a buyer would pay for it. Buy every unit with MB ≥ P. Area under D up to Q = total benefit. CS = area below D and above the market price.
  • Producer surplus PS = P×Q − (variable) cost of production = TR − VC. The supply curve is the marginal cost (MC) curve. Sell every unit with P ≥ MC. Area under S up to Q = variable cost. PS = area above S and below the market price.
  • Social surplus SS = CS + PS = Total benefits − Production cost. It is maximised at the competitive equilibrium Q*, because every unit with MB > MC gets traded. Producing Q1 < Q* loses the triangle between D and S from Q1 to Q*. That lost triangle is the deadweight loss (DWL) (more in T2/T3).
Producer side (L5 table): MC = 1, 2, 4, 6 and P = $3. Sell units 1 and 2 (MC ≤ 3). PS = (3−1) + (3−2) = $3. Selling unit 3 would add 3 − 4 = −1, so stop at 2.
After a demand shift (grid D → P = 12 − 0.01Q, new E: P = 7, Q = 500): CS = ½ × 500 × (12 − 7) = 1,250 and PS = ½ × 500 × (7 − 2) = 1,250.

Step-by-step methods

M1 · Read equilibrium off a grid
  1. Find where the D and S lines cross. Run a ruler/finger to both axes.
  2. Use the gridline spacing to read the values exactly. Check what one grid square equals (e.g. 100 units, $1).
  3. If the crossing is not on a gridline, use the line equations (M2).
M2 · Equilibrium from equations
  1. If given QD = a − bP and QS = c + dP: set them equal and solve for P*, then substitute into either equation to get Q*.
  2. If given inverse form P = a − bQ and P = c + dQ: set the P's equal, solve for Q*, then substitute to get P*.
  3. To build a line from a grid: slope = ΔP/ΔQ between two points, then P = intercept + slope × Q.
  4. Always check by substituting into the other equation.
M3 · Excess demand / supply at a fixed price
  1. Draw a horizontal line at the given price.
  2. Read QD (where it hits D) and QS (where it hits S).
  3. If P > P*: excess supply = QS − QD. If P < P*: excess demand = QD − QS.
M4 · An event → which curve, which way
  1. Identify the market in the question (whose price is on the axis?).
  2. Did the good's own price change? Then it is a movement along a curve, not a shift.
  3. Does the event change buyers' willingness or ability (population, tastes, income, price of a consumption substitute or complement)? Then D shifts.
  4. Does it change sellers' costs, the number of sellers, the price of a production substitute or complement, or the landlord's opportunity cost (interest rate)? Then S shifts.
  5. Choose the direction using the tables, then read P and Q from the single-shift table.
M5 · Two simultaneous shifts
  1. Do each shift separately and write down its effect on P and on Q.
  2. Same sign → that direction is certain. Opposite signs → "uncertain / indeterminable".
  3. If magnitudes or new curves are given, compute the new intersection instead.
M6 · Point price elasticity of demand (signed)
  1. Get (P, Q) for the point. In a monopoly question, use the monopoly P and Q on the demand curve.
  2. Slope of D = ΔP/ΔQ using two points on D, usually the intercepts: (P-intercept − 0)/(0 − Q-intercept). This is negative.
  3. Ray slope = P/Q.
  4. εD = (P/Q) ÷ slope. Equivalently, εD = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q). For Q = a − bP, ΔQ/ΔP = −b.
  5. Report the negative number, as a fraction if allowed. Classify using |ε|: >1 elastic, <1 inelastic, =1 unit elastic.
M7 · Percentage-change prediction
  1. %ΔQD = εD × %ΔP. Keep the signs: a price rise is +, so ε<0 gives a fall in Q.
  2. To get the price change needed for a target quantity change: %ΔP = %ΔQD ÷ εD.
M8 · Surplus areas
  1. CS = the region under D, above the price line, from Q = 0 to the quantity traded. For linear D it is a triangle: ½ × Q × (P-intercept of D − P).
  2. PS = the region above S, below the price line, up to the quantity traded: ½ × Q × (P − P-intercept of S). If S starts at the origin, the height is just P.
  3. If S hits the Q-axis rather than the P-axis, PS is a trapezoid. Split it into a rectangle plus a triangle.
  4. For stepwise tables: CS = Σ(MB − P) over units bought, and PS = Σ(P − MC) over units sold.
  5. SS = CS + PS = (area under D) − (area under S) up to Q.
M9 · Comparing two markets with different elasticities
  1. Identify which curve shifts and which curve the market moves along.
  2. The curve you move along is the one whose elasticity matters. If it is less elastic (steeper through the same point), P changes more and Q changes less.
  3. If it is more elastic, P changes less and Q changes more.
M10 · Opportunity cost of renting out
  1. OC = Pcurrent(1 + r) − Pfuture. Use r as a decimal.
  2. If OC rises (r↑, Pcurrent↑, expected Pfuture↓), the supply of rentals decreases, so rent rises and the quantity of rentals falls.

How it's examined

Calibrated from the S2 2026 practice exam Q1, the S1 2026 final (bubble tea, Q1–2) and the S1/S2 2025 finals (housing and cigarettes, Q1–3). The examples below are fresh and not copied from past papers.

Type 1 · Grid → equilibrium + excess at a fixed price ~2–8 marks

Use the grid in §5 (D: P = 10 − 0.01Q, S: P = 2 + 0.01Q). "The equilibrium price is [$4 / $6 / $8 / indeterminable] and quantity [200 / 400 / 600]. If the price is fixed at $4 there will be a(n) [government injection / excess demand / excess supply / equilibrium] of [200 / 400 / 600] units."

Answer: P* = $6, Q* = 400. At $4: QD = 600 and QS = 200, so there is excess demand of 400. Because $4 is below P*, it must be excess demand. "Government injection" is a distractor.

Type 2 · Event → which curve shifts, which way, P and Q ~4–6 marks

"Tea and coffee are [complements in consumption / substitutes in consumption / derived demand of each other]. A tariff raises the price of imported tea leaves. The [demand / supply] curve in the coffee market will [shift left / shift right / remain unchanged]. The equilibrium price of coffee will [increase / decrease / be uncertain] and quantity will [increase / decrease / be uncertain]."

Answer: substitutes in consumption. Tea becomes dearer, so buyers switch to coffee and coffee demand shifts right. Coffee P increases and Q increases. Coffee's supply is unaffected because the tariff hits tea inputs, not coffee.

A variation the exam uses: a ban on a close substitute (e.g. vapes banned → cigarettes). The substitute disappears, so demand for cigarettes increases, and P↑ and Q↑. Distractor words like "more volatile" and "more competitive" are never the answer for a simple shift.

Type 3 · Two simultaneous events (MCQ, 4 options) ~4–8 marks

"Young families move to Coastville (more people needing rentals). At the same time, interest rates are cut. Effect on equilibrium rent? (a) increase (b) decrease (c) unchanged (d) uncertain. Effect on quantity?"

Answer: People arriving means D↑ (P↑, Q↑). A rate cut lowers the opportunity cost of renting out, so S↑ (P↓, Q↑). Price: the effects conflict, so (d) uncertain. Quantity: both effects raise it, so it increases. This matches the practice exam pattern (sailors + building materials gives the same D↑ S↑ answers).

Type 4 · Two markets, different elasticity → which changes more? ~4 marks

"Market A and market B have identical supply curves. Demand in A is much less price-elastic than in B. A drought reduces supply equally in both. Which market has the bigger change in equilibrium price? Which has the bigger change in quantity?"

Answer: Supply shifts, so each market moves along its demand curve. Less elastic demand gives a bigger price change and a smaller quantity change, so A has the bigger price change and B has the bigger quantity change.

Check with numbers: S: P = 2 + 0.01Q shifts to P = 4 + 0.01Q. A: D P = 14 − 0.02Q (ε at E0 = −0.75). B: D P = 8 − 0.005Q (ε at E0 = −3). Both start at P = 6, Q = 400. After the shift, A: P = 7.33, Q = 333.3 (ΔP = +1.33, ΔQ = −66.7). B: P = 6.67, Q = 266.7 (ΔP = +0.67, ΔQ = −133.3).

A mirror-image version: identical demand, but supply in market U is more elastic, and demand rises equally in both. U has the smaller price change and the bigger quantity change.

Type 5 · Elasticity at a point on the grid ~3–4 marks

"At the market equilibrium, the price elasticity of demand is [−1.5 / −0.67 / 0.67 / 1.5] (do not use the absolute value) and demand is [elastic / unit elastic / perfectly inelastic / inelastic]." (Grid in §5.)

Answer: E = (400, $6). Slope of D = (10 − 0)/(0 − 1000) = −0.01. Ray = 6/400 = 0.015. ε = 0.015 ÷ (−0.01) = −1.5, so demand is elastic.

In the monopoly version (T3), the same method is applied at the monopoly (Q, P) on the demand curve, not on MR. Example: D from P-intercept 100 to Q-intercept 100, monopoly at Q = 30 and P = 70. ε = (70/30) ÷ (−1) = −7/3.

Type 6 · Equilibrium and surplus with algebra ~2–4 marks

"QD = 120 − 2P, QS = 4P − 30. Find P*, Q*, CS and PS. Then demand rises by 30 units at every price. Find the new P* and Q*."

Answer: P* = 25 and Q* = 70. The D intercept on the P-axis is 60 (where QD = 0). The S intercept on the P-axis is 7.5. CS = ½ × 70 × (60 − 25) = 1,225. PS = ½ × 70 × (25 − 7.5) = 612.5.
New demand QD = 150 − 2P: 150 − 2P = 4P − 30 → P* = 30, Q* = 90. P and Q both rise, consistent with D↑.
Elasticity at the original E: (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q) = −2 × 25/70 = −5/7 ≈ −0.71 (inelastic).

Type 7 · Short explanation (written box) ~2 marks

"Explain why rents rose sharply even though the number of rental units barely changed."

Model answer: Demand for rentals increased (more households, return of migrants) while supply decreased (higher interest rates raised the landlords' opportunity cost of renting out, and construction costs rose). Both shifts raise equilibrium rent. Their effects on quantity offset each other, so quantity changed little. Short-run supply is also inelastic, so the adjustment shows up mainly in price.

Traps & misconceptions

A change in the good's own price never shifts its own D or S. It is a movement along the curve, a change in quantity demanded/supplied, not in demand/supply.
Production substitutes work the opposite way to consumption substitutes. If the price of a consumption substitute rises, D for X increases. If the price of a production substitute rises, S of X decreases.
"Derived demand" appears as a distractor when you are asked to classify two goods. Coffee and bubble tea are substitutes in consumption.
Elasticity sign: if the question says "do not use the absolute value", the answer is negative. The positive version is offered as a trap.
Slope is not elasticity. A straight-line demand curve has a constant slope but a changing elasticity: elastic at high prices, inelastic at low prices. Use slope = ΔP/ΔQ (P on the vertical axis) in the formula, not ΔQ/ΔP, unless you also flip to ×P/Q.
Price below P* gives excess demand (a shortage). Price above P* gives excess supply. Students often swap these.
"Increase in supply" is a rightward and downward shift. A "supply curve shifting up" is a decrease in supply.
With two simultaneous shifts and no magnitudes, one variable is "uncertain". Don't guess "unchanged". But if the new curves are drawn on a grid, read the actual answer.
Interest rate rises are treated in the lecture as reducing rental supply (landlords' opportunity cost), not as reducing rental demand.
Elasticity in a two-market question: when supply shifts, look at demand elasticity, and vice versa. Less elastic means a bigger price change and a smaller quantity change.
Opportunity cost is the value of the single best forgone alternative, not the sum of all alternatives. Implicit costs (your time) count in economic surplus.
Producer surplus uses variable costs only. Fixed costs are sunk.

Self-check

1. The price of Pepsi rises. What happens in the Coke market?

Pepsi is a substitute in consumption, so demand for Coke shifts right. P↑ and Q↑.

2. The price of whisky rises sharply. What happens in the market for hand sanitiser made by distillers?

They are substitutes in production, so the supply of sanitiser decreases (shifts left). P↑ and Q↓.

3. The price of bus trips from St Lucia to Toowong rises. Effect on demand for St Lucia rentals?

The bus is a complement in consumption, so demand for St Lucia rentals decreases. Rent ↓ and Q ↓.

4. εD = −0.4 and price rises 5%. What is %ΔQD? Elastic or inelastic?

%ΔQD = −0.4 × 5% = −2%. |ε| < 1, so demand is inelastic.

5. Demand P = 50 − 2Q. Find εD at Q = 10.

P = 50 − 20 = 30. Ray = 30/10 = 3. Slope = −2. ε = 3 ÷ (−2) = −1.5 (elastic).

6. On the §5 grid, the price is fixed at $8. What happens?

QD = 200 and QS = 600, so there is excess supply of 400. Sellers would want to cut prices.

7. MB of successive units: $10, $8, $5, $2. Price is $4. How many are bought, and what is CS?

Buy units with MB ≥ 4, which is 3 units. CS = (10−4) + (8−4) + (5−4) = 6 + 4 + 1 = $11.

8. Population grows AND construction costs fall. Effect on rent and on quantity?

D↑ (P↑, Q↑) and S↑ (P↓, Q↑). Quantity increases. Rent is uncertain.

9. The RBA cuts the cash rate. What happens in the rental market (lecture logic)?

r↓ means OC = Pcurrent(1+r) − Pfuture falls, so the supply of rentals increases. Rent ↓ and quantity ↑.

10. Chicken wing prices rise. What happens in the market for chicken breasts?

Wings are a complement in production, so the supply of breasts increases. P↓ and Q↑.

Definitions
  • QD: qty buyers want to buy at a price (per period). Demand: the whole P–QD relationship.
  • QS: qty sellers want to sell. Supply: the P–QS relationship.
  • Law of demand: P↑ → QD↓ (substitution effect, income effect, buyers exit).
  • Law of supply: P↑ → QS↑ (intensive margin: more profitable; extensive margin: entry).
  • Increase in D = right = up (pay more for the same Q). Increase in S = right = down (accept less for the same Q). Decrease in S = left = up.
  • Competitive equilibrium: no one wants to change decision given P. QD* = QS* at P*. Also called market clearing.
  • Perfect competition: many buyers and sellers (price-takers), homogeneous good, perfect information, free entry/exit.
  • Opportunity cost: value of the next-best alternative forgone.
  • Economic surplus = Benefits − Explicit − Implicit costs.
  • Stock (at a point in time: house) vs flow (per period: rent). Assets = stocks generating flows. Rent = opportunity cost of housing.
  • Housing affordability = housing costs/household income × 100%. Stress > 30% (bottom 40% of income).

Elasticity of demand
εD = %ΔQD/%ΔP   (<0)
Point: εD = (P/Q) ÷ (ΔP/ΔQ) = ray slope ÷ D slope = (ΔQ/ΔP)×(P/Q)
%ΔQD = εD × %ΔP
|ε|: 0 perfectly inelastic (vertical) · <1 inelastic · 1 unit · >1 elastic · ∞ perfectly elastic (horizontal)
Linear D: upper half elastic, midpoint unit, lower half inelastic. Through the same point, steeper = less elastic.
More elastic if: many close substitutes (narrowly defined good) · longer time frame · luxury · large budget share.
Elastic → P↓ raises TR. Inelastic → P↓ lowers TR.
εS = %ΔQS/%ΔP (>0). Not calculated in this course.
Surplus
D = MB curve; S = MC curve.
CS = Total benefit − P·Q = area below D, above P.
PS = P·Q − VC = area above S, below P.
SS = CS + PS = TB − cost. Maximised at competitive Q*. Producing Q < Q* loses the D–S triangle (DWL).
Triangle = ½ × base × height. Stepwise: CS = Σ(MB−P), PS = Σ(P−MC) over traded units.
Elasticity & shifts
D shifts → move along S: inelastic S → big ΔP, small ΔQ (short-run housing). Elastic S → small ΔP, big ΔQ.
S shifts → move along D: inelastic D → big ΔP, small ΔQ. Elastic D → small ΔP, big ΔQ.

OC = Pcurrent(1+r) − Pfuture
↑ when Pcurrent↑, r↑, expected Pfuture↓ → rental supply ↓.
e.g. 800k×1.05 − 820k = 20k; at r = 6%: 28k.

1 · The big idea

A firm with market power (a "price-searcher") can raise its price by selling less. To sell one more unit it has to cut the price on every unit, so its marginal revenue is below the price (MR < P). It still maximises profit where MR = MC, but then charges the highest price the demand curve allows for that quantity. The result is a price above marginal cost (a mark-up), less output than a competitive market would produce, consumer surplus moved to the firm, and a deadweight loss: units that consumers value above their cost don't get made.

Lecture story: food inflation (9.6%) ran ahead of general inflation (7%) in 2022–23. Are Coles & Woolworths price gouging? The ACCC (2025) found the industry is an oligopoly (Woolworths 38%, Coles 29%, ALDI 9%, Metcash 7%) with high barriers to entry, rising margins, and monopsony power over fresh-produce suppliers. The economics says that market power means higher prices for consumers, lower prices for suppliers, and an inefficient market.

What actually gets examined (calibrated from the practice exam & the S1 2026 final walkthrough in L13): a grid diagram with D, MR, MC. You type QM, PM and the mark-up (P − MC), choose "produces less / charges more" from a dropdown, click the DWL area, calculate elasticity at the monopoly point, and write a short answer: "is demand elastic at the monopoly price?" (yes, always). Learn that pattern cold.

2 · Concepts, one bite at a time

2.1 Perfect competition: price takers

The four assumptions: many small buyers and sellers, perfect information, identical products, and free entry and exit in the long run.

Market power = the ability of an individual buyer or seller to unilaterally change the market price through its own actions. In perfect competition nobody has any. Every buyer and seller is a price-taker.

Demand facing ONE competitive firm is perfectly elastic (horizontal) at P*, even though market demand slopes down:

  • If PFirm > P*, QDFirm = 0. With perfect information nobody buys from the most expensive seller.
  • If PFirm = P* − dP (a tiny undercut), the whole market wants to buy from you. So you can sell as much as you can produce at P* and have no reason to change the price.

So for a price-taker: MR = ΔTR/ΔQ = P*. Every extra unit brings in exactly P*.

2.2 Price-searchers and market power is a matter of degree

"Big players" can influence the price through the quantity they sell or buy. They search for the profit-maximising price, then sell (or buy) the quantity that achieves it. Examples: Apple (iPhones), TikTok (rates paid for music).

MarketPrice-searching side
Monopolyone seller
Oligopolyfew sellers (e.g. Coles + Woolworths)
Monopsonyone buyer
Oligopsonyfew buyers (supermarkets buying from farmers)
The lecturer uses "monopoly" (and "monopsony") even when there is more than one price-searcher, because the number doesn't matter much for this analysis. So the monopoly model is the course's model of an oligopoly like the supermarkets.

A monopoly's demand curve = the market demand curve (it is the only seller). It can pick any point on it: a high price with a small Q, or a low price with a big Q.

The steeper (less elastic) the demand facing the seller, the more market power it has: the same change in Q moves the price a lot.

2.4 Cost curves, economies of scale and natural monopoly

FC (fixed cost, e.g. factory rent) doesn't change with Q. VC (variable cost, e.g. materials) rises with Q. TC = FC + VC. ATC = TC/Q, AFC = FC/Q.

  • Economies of scale: ATC ↓ as Q ↑.  Diseconomies of scale: ATC ↑ as Q ↑.  Constant returns: ATC unchanged.
  • Minimum efficient scale: the output where ATC is first at its minimum. A natural monopoly arises when this is large relative to the size of the market.
  • It is most likely when FC is very high and VC is low relative to FC. If VC = 0, then ATC = AFC = FC/Q, which falls forever as Q rises: economies of scale at every output level.

Profit: Π = TR − TC = P×Q − ATC×Q = (P − ATC) × Q.

Why big firms drive out small ones: Firm A produces more (QA > QB), so ATC(QA) < ATC(QB). A can set ATC(QA) < PA < ATC(QB). If B matches the price it makes losses. If B charges more, it sells nothing and still pays its fixed costs. B eventually exits and A is left as a natural monopoly.

Worked example. ATC = 400/Q + 2. Firm A makes QA = 80, so ATC = 400/80 + 2 = 7. Firm B makes QB = 20, so ATC = 400/20 + 2 = 22. A prices at PA = 12.
ΠA = (12 − 7) × 80 = +$400.  If B matches the price: ΠB = (12 − 22) × 20 = −$200. B exits.

2.5 Why competitive markets are efficient: marginal benefit, marginal cost and surplus

CS = the area below demand and above price. PS = the area above supply and below price. You know these. The lecture explains why using marginal thinking.

Marginal benefit: MB(Q) = TB(Q) − TB(Q−1). The demand curve IS the MB curve: the MB of a unit is the most a consumer will pay for it. A consumer buys every unit with MB ≥ P (marginal consumer surplus MCS = MB − P ≥ 0). The area under demand up to Q = total benefit (TB).

Worked example (consumer). TB(3) = 24, spending = 3 × 5 = 15, so CS = 24 − 15 = 9 (the same as 5 + 3 + 1 unit by unit).

Marginal cost: MC(Q) = VC(Q) − VC(Q−1). The supply curve IS the MC curve (in competitive markets). A firm sells every unit with P (= MR) ≥ MC. The area under supply up to Q = variable cost (VC). PS = TR − VC. Fixed costs are sunk, so maximising PS is the same as maximising profit.

Worked example (producer). VC = 0, 2, 6, 12, 20 gives MC = 2, 4, 6, 8. At P = 7: unit 1 (MC 2) ✓, unit 2 (4) ✓, unit 3 (6) ✓, unit 4 (8) ✗, so QS = 3. TR = 21, VC = 12, PS = 9 (= 5 + 3 + 1).

Optimal decisions are made at the margin. If marginal surplus > 0, increase Q. If it is < 0, decrease Q. The maximum is where marginal surplus = 0.

Social surplus SS = CS + PS = TB − VC (the payment P×Q cancels out). It is maximised where MB = MC, i.e. at the competitive equilibrium Q*.

Producing Q1 = 20 < Q* = 40: TB = A + B, VC = B, so SS = A = ½(80 + 40)×20 = 1200. Moving to Q* adds benefits C + D and costs D, so SS rises by C = ½ × 20 × 40 = 400, giving 1600 = ½ × 40 × 80 ✓. Under-producing → DWL = C = 400.
Over-producing Q2 = 60: the units from 40 to 60 have MB < MC. DWL = ½ × 20 × (80 − 40) = 400.

Takeaway: both under- and over-production create a DWL (an opportunity cost: surplus society could have had). Self-interested buyers and sellers in a perfectly competitive market reach the efficient Q* with no government needed.

2.6 Marginal revenue for a monopoly: MR < P

QCAA mostly skips this. To sell one more unit a monopolist must cut the price on all its units, not just the extra one. So MR < P for every unit after the first.

Lecture example: 2 units sell at $8 (TR = 16). To sell a 3rd it must charge $7 on all 3 (TR = 21). It gains +$7 from the 3rd unit but loses ($8 − $7) × 2 = $2 on the first two. MR(3) = 21 − 16 = $5 < $7. The 4th unit: P = 6, TR = 24, MR = $3.

2.7 Elasticity, total revenue and MR

Course convention (L2 & L13): εD = %ΔQD / %ΔP, kept negative. "Elastic" means |εD| > 1. At a point on a linear demand curve:

εD = (ΔQD/ΔP) × (P/QD) = (1/slope) × (P/QD) = slope of ray from origin ÷ slope of demand

The two tools to remember (the lecturer says these are all you need):

Worked: εD = −4. %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + (−4 × %ΔP) = −3 × %ΔP. So TR rises only if %ΔP < 0: cut the price. A 2% price cut gives %ΔQ = +8% and %ΔTR ≈ −2 + 8 = +6%.
Worked: εD = −0.5 and the price rises 4%. Then %ΔQ = −2% and %ΔTR ≈ 4 − 2 = +2% (inelastic, so a price rise increases TR).
Key exam fact: a profit-maximising monopolist never operates on the inelastic part of demand. There MR < 0, but MC ≥ 0, so MR < MC. Cutting output and raising the price would increase profit.

2.8 Profit maximisation: MR = MC, then price off the demand curve

Π = TR − TC, so marginal profit MΠ = ΔΠ/ΔQ = MR − MC.

  • MR > MC: MΠ > 0, so increase Q (Q0 on the lecture slide).
  • MR < MC: MΠ < 0, so decrease Q (Q1).
  • MR = MC: profit is maximised at QM. This is the same rule as for a competitive firm. The only difference is that for a monopolist MR < P.

Price: once QM is set, go up to the demand curve. PM is the highest price at which consumers buy exactly QM.

Mark-up = P − MC (measured at QM: PM − MC(QM)). Market power shows up as P(QM) > MC(QM). Under perfect competition P = MC.

A competitive firm has MR = P*, so MR = MC gives P* = MC. That is why the competitive supply curve is the MC curve, and the competitive outcome is where D meets MC.

2.9 Monopoly vs perfect competition: surplus and deadweight loss (lecture labels A–G)

Competition: D = MC gives 120 − Q = 45 + 0.5Q, so QC = 50, PC = 70.

Why there is a DWL: units between QM and QC have MB (the demand height) > MC, so they would add social surplus, but the monopolist doesn't make them. In general, firms with market power create a DWL.

Elasticity at the monopoly point: slope of D = −1, P/Q = 90/30 = 3, so εD = (1/−1) × 3 = −3. That is elastic, as it must be (QM = 30 is left of the midpoint, 60).

2.10 Welfare summary, social cost and regulation

  1. Monopoly reduces consumer surplus.
  2. Monopoly increases producer surplus.
  3. Monopoly causes a deadweight loss, i.e. a reduction in economic efficiency.

How big? The DWL from true monopolies is small relative to the whole economy because there are few of them. But the social cost can be higher: firms spend resources trying to gain monopoly power (lobbying for entry restrictions, advertising for market share), which is socially unproductive. Also, most firms have some market power, so P > MC and some DWL exist in almost every market. That is why governments have competition policy.

Regulating monopolies:

  1. Eliminate the monopoly: reduce barriers to entry (time limits on patents let generics in), force a breakup (the US Bell System), or regulate mergers to stop monopolies forming.
  2. Regulate the prices monopolies charge.

ACCC supermarket recommendations: publish prices online, dynamic pricing via API, streamline planning and zoning, new merger laws covering supermarket acquisitions, stronger Food & Grocery Code, and transparency of tendering and rebates for suppliers.

Lecture conclusion: does supermarket market power worsen the cost of living? Market power leads to higher prices and economic inefficiency. On the supplier side, monopsony power pushes down the prices paid to farmers (although the ACCC noted this can help consumers if the savings are passed through).

Price discrimination is not in the 2026 Lecture 5 slides, but old (2021) exams asked: "DWL when the monopolist practises perfect (first-degree) price discrimination?" Answer: zero. The firm charges each buyer their MB, so it produces up to where D meets MC (= QC), and all surplus becomes producer surplus (CS = 0). Low priority, but now you know it.

3 · Step-by-step methods

M1 · Monopoly outcome from a grid diagram (D, MR, MC drawn)
  1. Find where MR crosses MC. Read down to the Q-axis: QM.
  2. From QM go straight UP to the demand curve, then across to the P-axis: PM. (Not the height of the MR = MC point!)
  3. The height of the MR = MC point is MC(QM). Mark-up = PM − MC(QM).
  4. Competitive outcome: where D crosses MC gives QC and PC.
  5. Compare: the monopolist produces less by QC − QM and charges more by PM − PC.
M2 · Monopoly outcome from equations
  1. Write demand as P = a − bQ. Then MR = a − 2bQ.
  2. Set MR = MC and solve for QM.
  3. Substitute QM into demand to get PM, and into MC to get MC(QM).
  4. Set demand = MC to get QC, PC.
If demand is given as Q = c − dP, rearrange first: P = c/d − (1/d)Q.
M3 · Surplus and DWL areas (monopoly)
  1. Mark four horizontal levels: the D intercept, PM, MC(QM) (and PC if needed), and the MC intercept.
  2. CSM = triangle under D, above PM, from 0 to QM = ½ × QM × (D-intercept − PM).
  3. PSM = area between PM and the MC curve, from 0 to QM = rectangle (PM − MC(QM)) × QM + triangle ½ × QM × (MC(QM) − MC-intercept). With constant MC it is just (PM − MC) × QM.
  4. DWL = triangle between D and MC from QM to QC = ½ × (PM − MC(QM)) × (QC − QM) = ½ × mark-up × lost units.
  5. Check: CSC + PSC − (CSM + PSM) = DWL.
M4 · Point elasticity at the monopoly point (the course's method)
  1. Slope of demand = ΔP/ΔQ = −(P-intercept)/(Q-intercept). E.g. intercepts 100 and 100 give −1.
  2. Ray from the origin = PM/QM.
  3. εD = (1/slope) × (P/Q). Keep the minus sign. Fractions are OK ("−16/9").
  4. Sanity check: it must be < −1 (elastic) at a monopoly point.
M5 · MR (and profit-max Q) from a table
  1. TR = P × Q for each row.
  2. MR(Q) = TR(Q) − TR(Q−1). (If Q goes up in steps of more than 1, divide by ΔQ.)
  3. MC(Q) = TC(Q) − TC(Q−1) (or VC differences).
  4. Keep producing while MR ≥ MC. Stop at the last unit where MR ≥ MC. The price is P in that row.
M6 · Elasticity → does total revenue rise or fall?
  1. %ΔQ = εD × %ΔP.
  2. %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ.
  3. Shortcut: elastic means TR moves the opposite way to P. Inelastic means TR moves the same way as P.
M7 · Consumer and producer choices from TB / VC tables
  1. MB = ΔTB per unit, and MC = ΔVC per unit.
  2. The consumer buys every unit with MB ≥ P. CS = TB(Q) − P×Q (= Σ(MB − P)).
  3. The firm sells every unit with MC ≤ P. PS = P×Q − VC(Q) (= Σ(P − MC)).
M8 · Profit with ATC / natural monopoly
  1. ATC = TC/Q = FC/Q + AVC.
  2. Π = (P − ATC) × Q. If P < ATC, the firm makes a loss.
  3. The bigger firm has the lower ATC (economies of scale), so it can price between the two ATCs. The smaller firm can't match it without a loss.

4 · How it's examined (fresh worked examples)

Format seen in the practice exam and the S1 2026 final (L13): one monopoly question worth ~10–13 marks. Part (a) has numeric boxes plus a dropdown, (b) is "click the area", (c) asks for elasticity at the monopoly price, (d) is a short written answer. Older-style MCQs test concepts.

QT1 · Fill-in boxes and dropdown. "The monopolist will choose a quantity of [__] and charge a price of $[__]. The monopolist enjoys a mark-up of [__]. Compared with a perfectly competitive market, the monopolist [produces less / produces more / charges less…] by [__] units."
Answer & reasoning MR crosses MC at Q = 60 (MR = 80 − Q = 20). Up to demand: P = 80 − 0.5(60) = $50. Mark-up = P − MC = 50 − 20 = 30. Competitive: D meets MC at 80 − 0.5Q = 20, so QC = 120. The monopolist produces less by 60 units.
Trap answers: P = 20 (the height of MR = MC: that is MC, not the price). Q = 80 (where MR hits zero). "By 30" (that is the price gap, not units).
QT2 · "Click the area representing the deadweight loss due to monopoly power." (Areas W, X, Y, Z on the grid above.)
Answer & reasoning The DWL is the wedge between D and MC from QM = 60 to QC = 120: Y. Also: W = CS under monopoly, X = PS (= profit here, since there is no FC), Z = variable cost of production. Under competition CS = W + X + Y and PS = 0 (MC is flat), so X is the transfer from consumers to the monopolist.
QT3 · Numeric surplus. "Calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus and the deadweight loss under monopoly."
Answer & reasoning CS = ½ × 60 × (80 − 50) = 900. PS = (50 − 20) × 60 = 1800. DWL = ½ × (50 − 20) × (120 − 60) = 900.
Check: competitive CS = ½ × 120 × (80 − 20) = 3600 = 900 + 1800 + 900 ✓.
QT4 · "Calculate the price elasticity of demand at the monopoly price and quantity. You can leave your answer as a fraction."
Answer & reasoning Slope of D = ΔP/ΔQ = −80/160 = −0.5, so 1/slope = −2. Ray from origin = P/Q = 50/60 = 5/6.
εD = (1/slope) × (P/Q) = −2 × 5/6 = −5/3 ≈ −1.67. Elastic ✓ (QM = 60 is left of the midpoint, 80).
Write the working (formula → slope → ray → answer). That's how the lecturer's model answers are set out.
QT5 · Written: "Another monopolist in an unrelated market asks whether demand is elastic at the price he charges. Can you answer with no further information? Explain."
Model answer Yes. Demand is elastic. When demand is inelastic (|εD| < 1), marginal revenue is negative (MR < 0). Firms, including monopolies, have non-negative marginal costs (MC ≥ 0), so on the inelastic part MR < MC. A profit-maximising firm never produces where MR < MC, because marginal profit is negative: it could raise profit by cutting output and raising its price. So a monopolist always operates on the elastic part of demand.
QT6 · Full surplus with an upward-sloping MC (the harder grid). Use the §2.9 diagram (D: P = 120 − Q, MC = 45 + 0.5Q). "What is producer surplus under monopoly?"
Answer & reasoning PS = area between PM = 90 and the MC curve from 0 to 30 = rectangle (90 − 60) × 30 + triangle ½ × 30 × (60 − 45) = 900 + 225 = 1125 (= B + C + D).
Trap: (P − MC) × Q = 900 ignores the triangle below MC(QM) when MC slopes up.
QT7 · Concept MCQs (select one alternative).
Five quick ones with answers
  1. "A monopoly maximises profit by selling where…" → MR = MC (not P = MC, not MR = P).
  2. "For a monopolist with linear demand, the MR curve…" → lies below the demand curve (same intercept, twice as steep).
  3. "An individual firm in a perfectly competitive market faces a demand curve that is…" → perfectly elastic (horizontal) at the market price.
  4. "Which is most likely to produce a natural monopoly?" → very high fixed costs, low variable costs (e.g. electricity transmission).
  5. "Compared with perfect competition, a monopoly…" → CS ↓, PS ↑, total surplus ↓ (DWL).
QT8 · Elasticity → revenue. "Demand for a supermarket's home-brand milk has εD = −0.4. If it raises the price by 5%, what happens to total revenue?"
Answer & reasoning %ΔQ = −0.4 × 5% = −2%. %ΔTR ≈ 5% − 2% = +3%: TR rises. Inelastic demand, so the price and TR move together. (This also tells you the firm is not at a profit max: MR < 0 there.)
QT9 · MR from a table. "A firm sells 10 units at $30 or 11 units at $28. What is the marginal revenue of the 11th unit?"
Answer & reasoning TR(10) = 300, TR(11) = 308, so MR = $8. Less than the $28 price, because the price cut of $2 applies to the first 10 units too (−$20), offsetting the +$28 from the new unit: 28 − 20 = 8 ✓.

5 · Traps & misconceptions

Reading PM off the MR = MC intersection. That height is MC(QM). The price comes from going up to the demand curve at QM.
Using D = MC for the monopolist. D = MC is the competitive outcome (QC, PC). The monopolist uses MR = MC.
The DWL triangle's height is PM − MC(QM), not PM − PC. The wedge runs from the demand curve down to the MC curve at QM, and its point is at the competitive equilibrium.
Forgetting the triangle under MC(QM) in PS when MC slopes upward. PSM = everything between PM and the MC curve, not just the mark-up rectangle.
Memorising letters. The L5 slide labels the DWL "F + G" (and has a typo, "E + F"). L13's exam diagram calls it "D + E". The letters always change, so identify areas by definition.
MR = 0 at the Q where demand hits the axis. No: for a linear demand MR hits zero at half that quantity (the midpoint).
Dropping the minus sign or using midpoint elasticity. The course uses point elasticity (1/slope × P/Q) at the monopoly point and keeps it negative (e.g. −16/9). Say "|ε| > 1, elastic" if a classification is asked.
"A monopolist charges the highest price possible." No: it charges the profit-maximising price, which is on the elastic part of demand. A higher price lowers profit.
"A competitive firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve." The market demand slopes down. The firm's demand is horizontal at P*, and MR = P*.
Thinking the DWL is the transfer B. Area B (PC→PM × QM) moves from consumers to the producer. It is redistribution, not lost surplus. Only the wedge is DWL.
Slide wording slip. L5 slide 112 says "MR will be positive when demand is Inelastic". That's a typo: it should be Elastic (MR > 0). Inelastic means MR < 0.

"MR curve for linear demand"Same P-intercept, twice as steep; P = a − bQ ⇒ MR = a − 2bQ; MR = 0 at the midpoint
"Raise/cut price to increase revenue?" / "effect on TR"%ΔQ = ε×%ΔP; %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ. Elastic → cut P; inelastic → raise P
"Demand facing an individual competitive firm"Perfectly elastic (horizontal) at P*; MR = P*
"Profit-maximising rule (any firm)"MR = MC (MΠ = MR − MC = 0). Competitive firm: P = MC
"Profit with ATC"Π = (P − ATC) × Q
"Natural monopoly arises when…"Economies of scale so large one firm supplies the market at lower cost; high FC, low VC; MES large vs the market
"Effects of monopoly on welfare"CS ↓, PS ↑, TS ↓ (DWL)
"How can government reduce monopoly inefficiency?"(1) Eliminate it: cut barriers / patent time limits, break up, regulate mergers; (2) regulate prices
"DWL under perfect (1st-degree) price discrimination" (old exams)Zero (Q = QC; CS = 0, all surplus goes to the firm)

Π = TR − TC = (P − ATC)×Q  ·  MΠ = MR − MC
TC = FC + VC  ·  ATC = TC/Q  ·  AFC = FC/Q
PS = TR − VC (FC sunk ⇒ max PS ≡ max Π)
CS = TB − P×Q  ·  SS = CS + PS = TB − VC
MB(Q) = TB(Q) − TB(Q−1)  ·  MCS = MB − P  ·  MPS = MR − MC

Procedure: monopoly grid question
  1. QM: MR ∩ MC → down.
  2. PM: up from QM to D → across.
  3. MC(QM) = height of MR ∩ MC. Mark-up = PM − MC(QM).
  4. QC, PC: D ∩ MC.
  5. "Produces less by" QC − QM.
  6. CS = ½·QM·(D-int − PM).
  7. PS = (PM − MC(QM))·QM + ½·QM·(MC(QM) − MC-int).
  8. DWL = ½·(PM − MC(QM))·(QC − QM).
  9. ε = (1/slope)·(PM/QM); slope = −(P-int)/(Q-int).
Procedure: from equations
  1. Write P = a − bQ (rearrange if Q = …).
  2. MR = a − 2bQ.
  3. Solve MR = MC → QM; PM = a − bQM.
  4. Solve a − bQ = MC → QC, PC.
  5. Areas as on the left.
Procedure: tables
  1. TR = P×Q; MR = ΔTR. MC = ΔTC (or ΔVC).
  2. Produce while MR ≥ MC. The price is P in that row.
  3. Consumer: buy while MB ≥ P; CS = TB − PQ.
  4. Price-taking firm: sell while P ≥ MC; PS = PQ − VC.

Buyer side: monopsony (one buyer) and oligopsony (few buyers) push down the prices paid to suppliers. The lecturer calls all of these "monopoly/monopsony" regardless of the number of firms.
Perfect competition assumptions: many small buyers and sellers; perfect information; identical product; free entry and exit (LR).
Market power = the ability of one buyer or seller to unilaterally influence the market price. It is a matter of degree: the steeper the firm's demand, the more power.
Barriers to entry (4)
  1. Government: patents/copyright (time-limited); public franchise (Taiwan Liquor & Tobacco, China National Tobacco).
  2. Key resource: Alcoa (bauxite), De Beers (DTC).
  3. Network externalities: value ↑ as users ↑ (Facebook vs Google+).
  4. Natural monopoly: economies of scale; high FC, low VC (rail, airports, Powerlink).
Scale: economies = ATC ↓ as Q ↑; diseconomies = ATC ↑ as Q ↑; constant = flat. MES = output that minimises ATC. VC = 0 ⇒ ATC = FC/Q falls forever.
Price war: big A has ATCA < PA < ATCB, so B loses (PA − ATCB)·QB < 0 if it matches and exits.
Efficiency logic
  • D = MB curve; area under D to Q = TB. S = MC curve; area under S to Q = VC.
  • SS = TB − VC is maximised at MB = MC (the competitive Q*).
  • Q < Q*: units with MB > MC are lost ⇒ DWL. Q > Q*: units with MB < MC are made ⇒ DWL.
  • Monopoly units QM→QC have MB > MC but are not produced ⇒ DWL.
  • Marginal rule: MS > 0 ⇒ ↑Q; MS < 0 ⇒ ↓Q; optimum at MS = 0.
Welfare and policy
  • Monopoly: CS ↓, PS ↑, DWL (efficiency ↓).
  • Total DWL from monopolies is small (there are few of them), BUT the social cost is higher because of resources spent gaining power (lobbying, advertising), and most firms have some power ⇒ small DWL everywhere ⇒ competition policy.
  • Fixes: (1) eliminate the monopoly: cut barriers (patent expiry → generics), break up (Bell System), regulate mergers; (2) regulate prices.
  • Supermarkets (ACCC 2025): oligopoly (Woolworths 38%, Coles 29%, ALDI 9%, Metcash 7%), high entry barriers, rising margins, monopsony over fresh produce. Price gouging = raising prices of essentials to an unreasonable level, especially during a crisis.

Block 1 · Cigarettes in Marlborough (T1) · 20 marks

Scenario. This question concerns the perfectly competitive market for cigarettes in the country of Marlborough. The demand-and-supply diagram is below (drawn to scale).

Q1 12 marks

(a) According to the diagram, the equilibrium price in this market is [ ▾ ] ($3 / $5 / $7 / Cannot be determined.) while the equilibrium quantity transacted is [ ▾ ] (500 / 1500 / 1000 / Cannot be determined.).

(b) At the market equilibrium, the price elasticity of demand is [ ▾ ] (−1.25 / 0.8 / −0.8 / 1.25) (do not use the absolute value) and demand is [ ▾ ] (elastic / unit elastic / perfectly inelastic / inelastic).

(c) If the government wishes to reduce the consumption of cigarettes, one suitable policy will be to implement a [ ▾ ] (unit subsidy of $4 per pack / price floor of $3 / price floor of $7 / price ceiling of $7) which will then [ ▾ ] (reduce demand for cigarettes / reduce supply of cigarettes / reduce quantity of cigarettes demanded by smokers / reduce quantity supplied by tobacco companies).

Answer & working

(a) $5 and 1000 · (b) −1.25, elastic · (c) price floor of $7 → reduce quantity of cigarettes demanded by smokers 🟢 confident

  1. Equilibrium: D and S cross at the dashed lines (1000, $5).
  2. Elasticity (T1 methods: the point method, ε = (P/Q) ÷ slope, with slope = ΔP/ΔQ). Slope of D = (3 − 7)/(1500 − 500) = −4/1000 = −0.004. P/Q at equilibrium = 5/1000 = 0.005. ε = 0.005 / −0.004 = −1.25. |ε| > 1, so demand is elastic. (Check: equilibrium is above the midpoint of the linear D, which is the elastic half.)
  3. Policy (T2 methods): to cut consumption you need a binding price floor, i.e. one set above P* = $5. A $7 floor is binding: Qd = 500 < Qs = 1500, so only 500 packs are bought. A $3 floor sits below P*, so it doesn't bind. A $7 ceiling sits above P*, so it doesn't bind either. A subsidy raises consumption.
  4. A price floor doesn't shift any curve. It moves buyers along demand, so it reduces the quantity demanded, not "demand".
Two traps. First, the elasticity answer choices include both −1.25 and its inverse, −0.8 (that's what you get from using ΔQ/ΔP the wrong way round, or from Q/P). Second, in part (c), "reduce demand" sounds right but is wrong. A price control is a movement along the curve, not a shift.

Q2 4 marks

Vapes and cigarettes are close substitutes. Due to increasing concerns over health risks posed by vapes which were heavily marketed to teenagers, the government of Marlborough has banned the sale of vapes in the country.

This policy will [ ▾ ] (increase the demand for / decrease the demand for / increase the supply of / decrease the supply of) cigarettes, leading to a(n) [ ▾ ] (lower / higher / uncertain / more volatile) equilibrium price and a(n) [ ▾ ] (lower / higher / uncertain / more competitive) quantity transacted in the market for cigarettes.

Answer & working

increase the demand for · higher price · higher quantity 🟢 confident

Banning a substitute effectively raises its price to infinity, so former vapers switch to cigarettes. Demand for cigarettes shifts right with supply unchanged, so P↑ and Q↑ (T1 shift table).

The ban is on vapes. It isn't a supply-side policy in the cigarette market, so it doesn't shift cigarette supply.

Q3 4 marks

There are two separate markets for cigarettes: a market for premium cigarettes and a separate market for value cigarettes. Both markets are perfectly competitive. However, demand in the market for premium cigarettes is significantly less price elastic than demand in the value market. The supply curves for the value and premium cigarette markets are identical. Suppose a failure of the tobacco harvest has decreased supply in both markets to the same exact extent.

(a) Which market will experience a bigger change in equilibrium price due to the tobacco crop failure? (The market for premium cigarettes / The market for value cigarettes / The price changes in the two markets are the same / There is insufficient information to determine)

(b) Which market will experience a bigger change in equilibrium quantity? (same four options)

Answer & working

(a) Premium (inelastic demand) · (b) Value (elastic demand) 🟢 confident

Shift the same supply curve left in both markets. If demand is steep (inelastic), buyers barely cut quantity, so the shock comes out mostly as a price rise. If demand is flat (elastic), price hardly moves and the shock comes out as a quantity fall.

Rule: when supply shifts, the more inelastic the demand, the bigger ΔP and the smaller ΔQ. (When demand shifts, the same rule applies to supply elasticity.)

Block 2 · Zapp Co natural monopoly (T3) · 10 marks

Scenario. Zapp Co is a monopoly in the market for electricity transmission services. The market demand, marginal revenue and marginal cost curves are given in the following diagram.

Q4 10 marks

(a) Zapp Co is a natural monopoly. This means that Zapp Co's monopoly over the electricity transmission industry is due to (government policy / significant economies of scale / network externalities / Zapp Co's control of a key input).

(b) According to the diagram, the monopolist will choose a quantity of [ ] and charge a price of $[ ].

(c) Compared to a perfectly competitive market, the monopolist creates a [ ▾ ] (positive externality / negative externality / deadweight loss / higher level of social surplus). This is because at the monopolist's profit-maximising choice of quantity, [ ▾ ] (250 units with positive social surplus are not produced / 1000 units with positive social surplus are not produced / 1000 units with negative surplus are produced / 250 units with negative surplus are produced).

Answer & working

(a) significant economies of scale · (b) Q = 750, P = $120 · (c) deadweight loss; 250 units with positive social surplus are not produced 🟢 confident

  1. Natural monopoly = one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms could, because of economies of scale (T3 barriers table). The other options are other barriers to entry: legal barriers, network effects, and control of a key resource.
  2. Profit-max: set MR = MC, which gives Q = 750. Then go up to the demand curve for the price: $120, not $70 (T3 methods).
  3. Competitive benchmark: P = MC on demand, so Q = 1000, P = $100. The units from 750 to 1000 have value to buyers (D) greater than MC, so they carry positive surplus. Not producing them is the DWL (shaded): 1000 − 750 = 250 units. If asked for its size, DWL = ½ × 250 × (120 − 70) = $6,250.
Price ≠ $70. $70 is where MR = MC, which only sets the quantity. Also, 1000 is the efficient quantity, not the number of units lost.

Block 3 · AD-AS shocks & policy (T4) · 17 marks

Scenario (Q5–Q8). The economy was originally in long-run equilibrium. However, a series of external shocks shifted the economy into a short-run but not long-run equilibrium, labelled A in the AD-AS diagram below. (There are no numbers. A sits to the left of LRAS.)

Q5 3 marks

(a) How could the shocks have moved the economy from long-run equilibrium to the new short-run equilibrium A? Select one or more: (The shocks decreased SRAS / The shocks decreased AD / The shocks increased SRAS / The shocks increased AD)

(b) If the government takes no action, what will naturally cause the economy to return to long-run equilibrium? (Decrease in AD due to lower wages caused by high unemployment / Increase in SRAS due to lower wages caused by high unemployment / Decrease in SRAS caused by higher wages caused by labour shortage / Increase in AD due to higher wages caused by labour shortage)

Answer & working

(a) decreased SRAS and decreased AD · (b) Increase in SRAS due to lower wages caused by high unemployment 🟡 (a) judgement call · 🟢 (b)

(a) The original LR equilibrium was on LRAS. The diagram doesn't show where it was or what P was. Starting on LRAS, a leftward AD shift (Y↓, P↓) or a leftward SRAS shift (Y↓, P↑) can each land you at a point left of LRAS. Since we can't tell whether P rose or fell, both are possible, so tick both "decreased" options. A rightward shift of either curve pushes Y above potential, so neither "increased" option can explain A on its own.

Why 🟡: with "a series of shocks", a big SRAS fall plus a small AD rise could also reach A. The question clearly intends the two "decreased" options, and that's the standard answer (T4 sheet: below-potential gap).

(b) Y < Ȳ means unemployment is above its natural rate. Wages fall, firms' costs fall, and SRAS shifts right until the economy is back on LRAS (T4 self-correction).

Wages shift SRAS, not AD. Every option that says "AD due to wages" is a distractor.

Q6 2 marks

Which of the following are fiscal policies that can return the country's real output to potential GDP? Select one or more: (Increase in transfer payments / Increase in interest rates / Decrease in interest rates / Increase in taxes)

Answer & working

Increase in transfer payments only 🟢 confident

We need AD to move right (expansionary). Fiscal policy = government spending, transfers and taxes. Higher transfers raise disposable income, so C rises and AD shifts right ✓. Higher taxes are contractionary ✗. Either interest-rate option is monetary policy ✗, even though a rate cut would work.

"Decrease in interest rates" is the right direction but the wrong policy type. Read the bold word in the question.

Q7 8 marks

Now consider the country of Cadia. Recent tensions in the Middle East have reduced the global supply of oil.

(a) The resulting increase in energy prices also increased the cost of production inputs in Cadia. This will likely have [ ▾ ] (decreased SRAS / increased SRAS / increased AD / decreased AD) in the Cadian economy.

(b) At the same time, demand for energy exports from Cadia increased as energy importers overseas sought to replace disrupted supplies from the Middle East. This will likely have [ ▾ ] (increased SRAS / increased AD / decreased SRAS / decreased AD) in Cadia.

(c) The overall impact of the two effects is [ ▾ ] (higher / lower / uncertain effect on / volatile effect on) real output and [ ▾ ] (higher / lower / uncertain / volatile) price level.

Answer & working

(a) decreased SRAS · (b) increased AD · (c) uncertain effect on real output, higher price level 🟢 confident

  • Input costs ↑ → SRAS left: Y↓, P↑.
  • Exports (NX) ↑ → AD right: Y↑, P↑.
  • Combined: both push P up, so P is definitely higher. They push Y in opposite directions, so the Y effect is uncertain without knowing the sizes of the shifts (T4 combined-shift method).
Same logic as combined D&S shifts in T1: the variable both shifts agree on is certain, and the other one is ambiguous.

Q8 4 marks · short answer ≤100 words

Continue to consider the shifts in AD and SRAS in part (c). Suppose the shift in SRAS was much greater than the shift in AD. Should the Central Bank of Cadia implement expansionary or contractionary monetary policy if its policy objective is to return real output to potential GDP? State and briefly explain your answer in 100 words or less.

Answer & working

Expansionary monetary policy 🟢 confident

Model answer (about 85 words): Expansionary. Because the SRAS decrease is much larger than the AD increase, the new short-run equilibrium has real output below potential GDP and a higher price level (stagflation). To return output to potential, the central bank should lower the cash rate (or increase the money supply). Lower interest rates raise consumption and investment, shifting AD to the right until it meets SRAS on LRAS. The trade-off is that the price level rises even further, so the bank accepts more inflation to close the output gap.

Mark-earning points: (1) state expansionary; (2) Y is below Ȳ because the SRAS shift dominates; (3) the mechanism: rates ↓ → C, I ↑ → AD right → Y back to Ȳ; (4) the cost: P rises further (T4: policy after a supply shock).

Don't answer "contractionary to fight inflation". The question fixes the objective as output. Contractionary policy would push Y even further below potential.

Block 4 · Comparative advantage & tariffs (T5) · 22 marks

Scenario (Q9–Q11). Each of Country A and Country B produces two goods: Bread and Cookies. The number of labour hours required to produce one unit of each good in each country is given by the following table. Country A has 270 labour hours while Country B has 200 labour hours.

BreadCookies
Country A3 labour hours9 labour hours
Country B2 labour hours4 labour hours

Q9 4 marks

Country A's opportunity cost of producing 1 unit of Bread is [ ▾ ] (1/3 / 3 / 1.5 / 2/3) units of Cookies.
Country A's opportunity cost of producing 1 unit of Cookies is [ ▾ ] (1.5 / 2/3 / 3 / 1/3) units of Bread.
Country B's opportunity cost of producing 1 unit of Bread is [ ▾ ] (4/9 / 2 / 2.25 / 1/2) units of Cookies.
Country B's opportunity cost of producing 1 unit of Cookies is [ ▾ ] (1/2 / 2.25 / 4/9 / 2) units of Bread.

Answer & working

A: Bread 1/3 C, Cookie 3 B · B: Bread 1/2 C, Cookie 2 B 🟢 confident

With hours per unit, OC of good X = hours(X) ÷ hours(Y) (T5 methods). A: 1 bread uses 3 h, which could make 3/9 = 1/3 cookie. 1 cookie uses 9 h, which could make 9/3 = 3 bread. B: 1 bread = 2/4 = 1/2 cookie. 1 cookie = 4/2 = 2 bread.

So A has the comparative advantage in Bread (1/3 < 1/2) and B has it in Cookies (2 < 3). B has the absolute advantage in both goods because it needs fewer hours for each.

The distractors 2.25 and 4/9 come from comparing across countries (9/4, 4/9). OC is always worked out within one country. The distractors 1.5 and 2/3 come from dividing the hours of the same good across the two countries.

Q10 4 marks

(a) If the terms of trade is 4 Bread for 1 Cookie, which 2 statements will be true? Select two: (Country A will sell Cookies / Country A will buy Cookies / Country B will sell Cookies / Country B will buy Cookies)

(b) Which two of the following terms of trade will result in trade between the two countries? Select two: (5 Bread for 2 Cookies / 4 Bread for 1 Cookie / 9 Bread for 5 Cookies / 8 Bread for 3 Cookies)

Answer & working

(a) Country A will sell Cookies; Country B will sell Cookies · (b) 5 Bread for 2 Cookies; 8 Bread for 3 Cookies 🟢 confident

Write every ToT as bread per cookie, then compare with the OCs of a cookie: A = 3, B = 2. Trade happens only if 2 < ToT < 3.

  • (a) At 4 bread per cookie, a cookie sells for more than it costs either country to make (4 > 3 and 4 > 2). Both countries want to sell cookies, nobody wants to buy, and no trade happens.
  • (b) 5/2 = 2.5 ✓ · 4/1 = 4 ✗ · 9/5 = 1.8 ✗ (both would want to sell bread) · 8/3 ≈ 2.67 ✓.
ToT outside the OC range → both countries sell the same good → no trade. That's what part (a) is testing.

Q11 4 marks

Suppose the two countries agree on terms of trade of 2.5 Bread for 1 Cookie.

(a) In this scenario, Country A will produce [ ▾ ] (90 units of Bread / 30 units of Cookies / 90 units of Cookies / 30 units of Bread) while Country B will produce [ ▾ ] (50 units of Bread / 100 units of Cookies / 100 units of Bread / 50 units of Cookies).

(b) Country A will be able to consume 20 units of Cookies and 40 units of Bread by exporting [ ▾ ] (20 units of Cookies / 10 units of Cookies / 50 units of Bread / 40 units of Bread) in exchange for imports of [ ▾ ] (10 units of Cookies / 40 units of Bread / 50 units of Bread / 20 units of Cookies).

Answer & working

(a) A: 90 units of Bread · B: 50 units of Cookies · (b) exports 50 units of Bread, imports 20 units of Cookies 🟢 confident

  1. 2.5 is between 2 and 3, so both countries specialise fully in their comparative-advantage good. A makes bread: 270 ÷ 3 = 90. B makes cookies: 200 ÷ 4 = 50.
  2. A wants 40 bread and 20 cookies. It has 90 bread, so it exports 90 − 40 = 50 bread and imports 20 cookies. Check against the ToT: 50 ÷ 20 = 2.5 ✓. B is left with 30 cookies and 50 bread.
Sanity check: on its own, A could make 20 cookies (180 h) plus only 30 bread (90 h). Trade gives A 10 extra bread.

Scenario (Q12–Q13). Country X produces and consumes widgets and trades widgets in the international market. The government of Country X currently imposes an import unit tariff of $t. The international market price with the import tariff is PWt. If the import tariff is lifted by Country X, the international market price will be PW. Country X's domestic demand and supply are given in the following diagram. (This is the large-country case: the tariff pushes the world price down from PW to PWt.)

Q12 4 marks

According to the diagram, if the import tariff is in place, Country X will import [ ] units of widgets. If the import tariff is removed, Country X will import [ ] units instead.

Answer & working

400 with the tariff · 600 without 🟢 confident

Imports = Qd − Qs at the domestic price. With the tariff, the domestic price is PWt+t, so imports = 700 − 300 = 400. Without it, the price is PW, so imports = 800 − 200 = 600.

Don't read the quantities at the PWt line. Domestic buyers pay world price plus the tariff.

(b) Country X's total surplus without the import tariff is: (same four options)

(c) If area (C+F) is greater than the area representing terms-of-trade gains from the import tariff, then Country X is better off by: (removing the import tariff / keeping the import tariff / banning imports entirely / there is insufficient information to provide an answer)

Block 5 · Rice externality & regulation game (T6) · 21 marks

Scenario (Q14–Q17). The following diagram depicts the private marginal cost, social marginal cost, and marginal benefits of rice production in the arid country of Aridistan.

Q14 1 mark

Based on the diagram, the production of rice in Aridistan involves: (positive externalities / no externalities / negative externalities / answer cannot be determined given information provided)

Answer & working

negative externalities 🟢 confident

SMC lies above PMC, so production imposes an external cost (here, water use in an arid country). SMB = PMB, so there's no consumption externality.

Q15 4 marks

According to the diagram, the equilibrium quantity in the competitive market is [ ]. Meanwhile, the socially optimal quantity is [ ].

Answer & working

Market Q = 700 · Social optimum = 500 🟢 confident

The market ignores the external cost, so it sets PMB = PMC → (700, $600). The social optimum sets SMB = SMC → (500, $900) (T6 methods).

Negative externality → the market overproduces. The larger quantity is the market one.

Q16 3 marks · typed, working required

Calculate the deadweight loss associated with the externality of rice production. Show your working. No marks if working is not shown.

Answer & working

DWL = $40,000 🟢 confident

Type this: The DWL is the triangle between SMC and SMB from the optimal quantity (500) to the market quantity (700). Base = 700 − 500 = 200 units. Height at Q = 700 = SMC − SMB = $1000 − $600 = $400. DWL = ½ × 200 × 400 = $40,000.
The triangle's tip is at (500, $900) and its vertical side is at Q = 700, from $600 up to $1000. Don't use 900 − 600 = 300 as the height. That's the gap at the wrong quantity.

Q17 3 marks

If the government wants to correct the externality, it can impose a per-unit [ ▾ ] (tax / subsidy) of the amount [ ▾ ] ($1000 − $900 = $100 / $900 − $600 = $300 / $900 − $500 = $400 / $1000 − $500 = $500).

Answer & working

tax of $900 − $500 = $400 🟢 confident

Pigouvian tax = marginal external cost at the social optimum = SMC − PMC at Q = 500 = 900 − 500 = $400. That moves PMC up onto SMC, so the market now clears at 500. (The gap is also $400 at Q = 700, because the lines are parallel.)

Scenario (Q18–Q20). Country A and Country B are considering whether to regulate their rice-growing sectors. Each of them can independently choose whether to regulate or to maintain the status quo. The payoff matrix is given below (payoffs are listed as A, B).

Country B
RegulateStatus quo
Country ARegulate100, 8040, 120

Q18 4 marks · typed

Does Country A have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

No, A has no dominant strategy 🟢 confident

Type this: No. A's best choice depends on what B does. If B regulates, A gets 100 from Regulate vs 50 from Status quo, so A should Regulate. If B keeps the status quo, A gets 40 from Regulate vs 80 from Status quo, so A should keep the Status quo. No single strategy is best for A whatever B does, so A has no dominant strategy.

Method (T6 payoff method): fix each column, compare A's first numbers down the column, and underline the best one.

Q19 4 marks · typed

Refer to the same payoff matrix. Does Country B have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

Yes, Status quo is dominant for B 🟢 confident

Type this: Yes, Status quo. If A regulates, B gets 120 from Status quo vs 80 from Regulate. If A keeps the status quo, B gets 100 from Status quo vs 60 from Regulate. Status quo gives B a higher payoff whatever A does, so it is B's dominant strategy.

Q20 2 marks

Which of the following is/are Nash equilibrium/equilibria of the game? Select one or more: ((Regulate, Regulate) / (Regulate, Status quo) / (Status quo, Status quo) / (Status quo, Regulate))

Answer & working

(Status quo, Status quo) only 🟢 confident

B always plays Status quo. A's best response to Status quo is Status quo (80 > 40). Check: neither player gains by deviating alone from (80, 100). A would prefer (Regulate, Regulate), where it gets 100, but B would deviate to Status quo (120 > 80), so that isn't a Nash equilibrium.

If one player has a dominant strategy, play it, then take the other player's best response. That gives exactly one NE.

Block 6 · Solow growth model (T7) · 10 marks

Scenario. Consider an economy in which per-capita output y depends only on the capital-labour ratio k; the saving rate is s; the population growth rate is n; and the depreciation rate is δ. The diagram depicts the per-capita output line y = f(k), the saving line s·f(k) (labelled sy), and the maintenance line (n + δ)k. A thin grey line is drawn tangent to f(k) at k2, parallel to (n + δ)k.

Q21 4 marks

(a) According to the diagram, the steady-state level of consumption is: (y₂ − y₁ / y₄ − y₂ / y₅ / y₃ − y₁)

(b) According to the diagram, this country can increase steady-state per-capita consumption by: (decreasing its saving rate / increasing its saving rate / increasing its population growth rate / doing nothing, since per-capita consumption is already maximised)

Answer & working

(a) y₃ − y₁ · (b) increasing its saving rate 🟢 confident

  1. Steady state is where sy (investment) = (n+δ)k, which is at k₁, not where f(k) crosses the line (k₃) (T7 methods). At k₁, output = f(k₁) = y₃ and saving/investment = y₁. Consumption = output − saving = y₃ − y₁.
  2. Golden Rule k is where the slope of f equals the slope of (n+δ)k. That's the grey tangent at k₂, with maximum consumption y₄ − y₂. The current k₁ < k₂, so the country is saving too little. Raising s moves k* toward k₂ and raises steady-state consumption.
y₄ − y₂ is the Golden Rule consumption, not the current steady state. y₅ is an output level, and it isn't a steady state at all unless s = 1.

Q22 6 marks

As educational and career opportunities increase, the opportunity costs of motherhood and child rearing increase as well. This often results in lower fertility and population growth rates.

This will shift the [ ▾ ] (Saving line / Maintenance line / Per capita output line / Tangent to the output line) [ ▾ ] (up / down / first up then down / first down then up). Compared to the original steady state, the new steady-state capital-labour ratio will be [ ▾ ] (higher / lower / the same as / uncertain).

Answer & working

Maintenance line · down · higher 🟢 confident

n is in the maintenance line (n+δ)k. A lower n makes the line flatter, so it rotates down. It now meets sy further right, so k* rises (and with it y*). The saving line and f(k) don't change, because they depend on s and technology, not n.

Shift table: s↑ → saving line up → k*↑ · n↑ or δ↑ → maintenance line up → k*↓ · technology ↑ → f and sf up → k*↑.

What this paper teaches

  • Every block is one diagram plus 2–7 questions. Read the labelled points carefully. Every numeric answer here (P*, Q*, ε, monopoly Q/P, imports, DWL, Pigouvian tax) comes straight from the dashed gridlines.
  • Elasticity on a grid: point method, signed answer, and the inverse (−0.8) is always a distractor. Check: above the midpoint of D means elastic.
  • "Demand" vs "quantity demanded": price controls and taxes move you along a curve. Only non-price factors (like a substitute being banned) shift it.
  • Monopoly: MR = MC gives Q, then read up to demand for P. DWL is described as "units with positive social surplus not produced" = Qcomp − Qm.
  • AD-AS: wages shift SRAS (self-correction). Two simultaneous shocks: one variable is certain, the other is "uncertain". Fiscal vs monetary labels are tested directly. After a supply shock, "restore output" means expansionary policy.
  • Comparative advantage: OC from hours = own hours ÷ other good's hours. Trade needs the ToT strictly between the two OCs. Outside the range, both countries sell the same good.
  • Large-country tariff: imports are read at PWt+t. Revenue spans PWt → PWt+t. Remove the tariff if DWL (C+F) > ToT gain. The area letters change between papers, so rebuild them every time.
  • Externality: DWL height is measured at the market quantity (SMC − SMB there). Pigouvian tax = SMC − PMC at the optimum.
  • Game theory typed answers: state Yes/No, then give both conditional comparisons with the numbers. One dominant strategy → unique NE.
  • Solow: steady state is at sy ∩ (n+δ)k. Consumption = f(k*) − sf(k*). The Golden Rule is the tangent point, so k* below it means "save more". Changes in n move the maintenance line.

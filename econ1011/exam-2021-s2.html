Q2 1 mark · T1 D&S · T3 Market power

Five year old Rhys shows considerable aptitude for mountain bike riding. His father engages a 12 year old boy to improve Rhys's biking skills. Rhys's father pays $0 for the first lesson and $30 for each additional lesson. Rhys's father obtains $200 of perceived benefit from commissioning 6 lessons, and $250 of perceived benefit from commissioning 7 lessons. Which of the following statements is true?

  • (a) The marginal benefit of commissioning the seventh lesson is $40.
  • (b) Rhys's father should commission the seventh lesson because the marginal benefit is greater than the marginal cost.
  • (c) The marginal cost of the seventh lesson is $50.
  • (d) Rhys's father should not commission the seventh lesson because the marginal benefit is less than the marginal cost.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) Rhys's father should commission the seventh lesson because the marginal benefit is greater than the marginal cost.  🟢 confident

  • MB(7th) = 250 − 200 = $50 (so (a) is false).
  • MC(7th) = $30 (each additional lesson; (c) is false, since $50 is the MB).
  • MB 50 > MC 30, so do it: (b). This is the cost–benefit principle: take an action iff MB ≥ MC.
(c) swaps MB and MC. Label each number before choosing.

Q3 1 mark · T1 D&S

Which one of the following factors will cause movements along the demand curve of good X?

  • (a) A change in the price of good X.
  • (b) A change in the price of complementary good Y.
  • (c) A change in the price of substitute good Z.
  • (d) A change in consumer preferences for good X.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) A change in the price of good X.  🟢 confident

Only the good's own price moves you along its demand curve. Prices of related goods, preferences, income and the number of buyers shift it.

Q4 1 mark · T1 D&S

When buyers and sellers have placed bids on a stock trading on the ASX, but no trades are currently being executed between these bids, this is a case of:

  • (a) a deadweight loss.
  • (b) market equilibrium.
  • (c) market inefficiency.
  • (d) Government intervention.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) market equilibrium.  🟡 judgement call

No trades happen because every remaining buyer's bid is below every remaining seller's ask. All mutually beneficial trades have already been made. That is equilibrium: no excess demand or supply pushes the price, and no unexploited gains from trade remain (so no DWL and no inefficiency).

🟡 There is no answer key, and the wording is unusual. But it matches the textbook idea that equilibrium = no remaining gains from trade.

Q5 1 mark · T1 D&S

Penny often buys pomegranates for less than her reservation price. She later learns that pomegranates provide more health benefits than she realised. She now values pomegranates more than before. If the price of pomegranates does not change, then:

  • (a) Penny's consumer surplus will increase.
  • (b) Penny's consumer surplus will decrease.
  • (c) Penny's consumer surplus will be unaffected because the price of pomegranates has not changed.
  • (d) Any change in Penny's consumer surplus cannot be determined from this information alone.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) Penny's consumer surplus will increase.  🟢 confident

CS = willingness to pay (reservation price) − price paid. Her WTP rises and the price is unchanged, so CS rises. (Her demand / MB curve shifts up.)

Q6 1 mark · T3 Market power

Which of the following costs should NOT be considered by a firm in the short-run?

  • (a) Marginal costs.
  • (b) Sunk Costs.
  • (c) Opportunity costs.
  • (d) Average variable costs.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) Sunk Costs.  🟢 confident

A sunk cost has already been paid and cannot be recovered, whatever the firm does next. So it cannot change the best decision. L5 says this directly: “fixed costs are sunk costs … and so should be ignored” (that is why PS = TR − VC, and maximising PS = maximising profit). MC, opportunity cost and AVC all change with the decision, so they matter.

Opportunity cost is always relevant. Don't pick it just because it sounds abstract.

Q8 1 mark · T1 D&S

When there is an increase in production input prices and a decrease in consumer income, the impact on the market equilibrium will be a/an ____ in quantity and a/an ____ in price.

  • (a) uncertain change; decrease
  • (b) increase; uncertain change
  • (c) uncertain change; increase
  • (d) decrease; uncertain change
Answer & working

Answer: (d) decrease; uncertain change  🟢 confident

  1. Input prices↑: S shifts left, so P↑ and Q↓.
  2. Income↓ (assume a normal good): D shifts left, so P↓ and Q↓.
  3. Both cut Q, so Q decreases. P is pushed both ways, so P is uncertain.
If the good were inferior, income↓ would shift D right, and the answer would flip to P↑, Q uncertain (c). The default assumption is a normal good.

Q9 1 mark · T2 Intervention · T5 Trade

Which of the following does NOT create a deadweight loss in competitive markets?

  • (a) Taxes
  • (b) Shift in demand
  • (c) Import quotas
  • (d) Tariffs
Answer & working

Answer: (b) Shift in demand  🟢 confident

A DWL appears only when something forces Q away from where MB (demand) = MC (supply). Taxes, quotas and tariffs all drive a wedge, so Q ≠ Q*. A demand shift just moves the market to a new equilibrium, where MB = MC still holds, so there is no DWL.

Q10 1 mark · T1 D&S

If the price goes above the market equilibrium, there will be

  • (a) a surplus of production.
  • (b) a shortage of production.
  • (c) Pareto efficiency.
  • (d) excess demand.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) a surplus of production.  🟢 confident

At P > P*: QS rises and QD falls, so QS > QD. That is excess supply (a surplus). Excess demand (shortage) is what happens below P*.

Q11 1 mark · T1 D&S

What would NOT cause a shift in the supply curve?

  • (a) Improved technology.
  • (b) Increasing input costs.
  • (c) Increasing the number of suppliers.
  • (d) Increasing the price of a complement good.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) Increasing the price of a complement good.  🟢 confident

Technology, input costs and the number of sellers are supply shifters. The price of a complement (in consumption) is a demand shifter for this good. It changes the price and quantity of this good only through a movement along supply.

Don't confuse this with “complements in production” (e.g. beef and leather), which is a supply idea. The 2026 course doesn't use it.

Q13 1 mark · T6 Climate & games

Which of the following has positive consumption externalities?

  • (a) Dumping waste in the ocean.
  • (b) Drinking alcohol.
  • (c) Educating your children.
  • (d) Building and operating a new airport.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) Educating your children.  🟢 confident

Educated people benefit society too (productivity spill-overs, civic benefits). That is a benefit to third parties, so it is a positive externality. Ocean dumping and alcohol are negative externalities. An airport has a negative production externality (noise).

Q15 1 mark · T6 Climate & games

In a market where there is a positive consumption externality, the socially optimal quantity can be achieved:

  • (a) with a subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit.
  • (b) with a tax on consumers equal to the size of the externality.
  • (c) without any tax or subsidy, as the market quantity is always socially optimal.
  • (d) with a tax on producers equal to the marginal external benefit.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) with a subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit.  🟢 confident

With a positive externality the market under-produces (Qmkt < Qeff), because buyers ignore the benefit to others. A Pigouvian subsidy = MEB (at the efficient Q) lifts private MB up to social MB, so the market reaches Qeff (L10). A tax would make things worse.

Q16 1 mark · T2 Intervention

A price floor set above the equilibrium price will NOT result in a/an

  • (a) surplus due to excess quantity supplied.
  • (b) deadweight loss.
  • (c) efficient outcome.
  • (d) decrease in consumer surplus.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) efficient outcome.  🟢 confident

A floor above P* is binding. QS > QD (surplus), trade falls to QD (so a DWL appears), and buyers pay more for fewer units (CS falls). The one thing it cannot be is efficient.

“NOT” questions: three options are true, so tick off each one against the diagram.

Q17 1 mark · T2 Intervention

For a price floor to be effective, it must be set to a price ____ the equilibrium price.

  • (a) higher than
  • (b) lower than
  • (c) equal to
  • (d) All of the above.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) higher than  🟢 confident

A floor is a legal minimum. If it's below P*, the market price is already above it and nothing changes. It only binds (is “effective”) above P*.

  • (b) B + C.

Q20 1 mark · T6 Climate & games

Game theory can NOT be applied to

  • (a) a firm's behaviour in a perfectly competitive market.
  • (b) negotiating your salary when two potential employers have made you a job offer.
  • (c) deciding whether to share your study notes with friends.
  • (d) two neighbouring fuel stations deciding their fuel prices.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) a firm's behaviour in a perfectly competitive market.  🟢 confident

Game theory needs strategic interdependence: my best move depends on what you do. A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker among thousands of firms. Nothing it does affects anyone else, so there is no game. (b), (c) and (d) are all small-number strategic situations; (d) is the classic duopoly pricing game.

Q23 1 mark · T3 Market power

If the demand curve a monopoly faces becomes more elastic, to maximise profits the monopoly will have to:

  • (a) sell more goods and lower prices.
  • (b) sell less goods and increase prices.
  • (c) sell more goods and increase prices.
  • (d) sell less goods and decrease prices.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) sell more goods and lower prices.  🟡 judgement call

More elastic demand means buyers are more price-sensitive and have closer substitutes, so market power is weaker. The monopolist's mark-up over MC shrinks: the profit-max price falls and it sells more, moving toward the competitive outcome (L5: “market power is a matter of degree”; perfectly elastic D gives P = MC).

🟡 Strictly, the quantity effect depends on how the curve changes. But the intended logic is “more elastic → behaves more like a competitive firm → lower P, higher Q”.

Q24 1 mark · T3 Market power

If a monopolist faces a linear downward-facing demand curve, the firm's marginal revenue curve:

  • (a) lies above the demand curve.
  • (b) lies below the demand curve.
  • (c) intersects the supply curve at the point where it is unit elastic.
  • (d) lies below the average total cost curve at all output quantities.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) lies below the demand curve.  🟢 confident

To sell one more unit the monopolist must cut the price on all units, so MR < P at every Q > 0. For linear D: P = a − bQ gives MR = a − 2bQ. It has the same intercept and twice the slope, so it hits the Q-axis halfway to demand's intercept.

Q25 1 mark · T3 Market power

Refer to the Figure. Consider the monopoly above. Calculate the profit made by this monopoly selling at the profit maximising point.

  • (a) $12.50
  • (b) $25

Profit/PS sits above MC, not above MR. With a rising MC, it's a rectangle plus a triangle.

Q29 1 mark · T5 Trade

If the Domestic Price is less than the World Price, then:

  • (a) an open economy will not affect producer surplus.
  • (b) an open economy will increase producer surplus.
  • (c) an open economy will not affect consumer surplus.
  • (d) an open economy will increase consumer surplus.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) an open economy will increase producer surplus.  🟢 confident

Pdom < PW means the country exports once open. The domestic price rises to PW, so PS↑ and CS↓ (net gain). This is the reverse of S1 Q29.

Q30 1 mark · T5 Trade

When a country becomes an exporter of a good, domestic consumer surplus ____ and domestic producer surplus ____

  • (a) increases; increases
  • (b) decreases; decreases
  • (c) increases; decreases
  • (d) decreases; increases
Answer & working

Answer: (d) decreases; increases  🟢 confident

A country exports when PW > domestic P*. Opening up raises the domestic price to PW. Buyers pay more, so CS falls. Sellers get more and sell more, so PS rises by more than CS falls, and total surplus rises. (Lecture: McDonald Island, the exporter case.)

Q32 1 mark · T1 D&S · T3 Market power

Which of the following is a feature of a perfectly competitive market?

  • (a) Few buyers and sellers.
  • (b) Homogeneous products.
  • (c) Sellers are price makers.
  • (d) Limited access to knowledge and information.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) Homogeneous products.  🟢 confident

Q33 1 mark · T3 Market power

In the long-run, perfectly competitive markets will NOT have

  • (a) substitutes.
  • (b) economic profits.
  • (c) many suppliers.
  • (d) perfect information.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) economic profits.  🟢 confident

L5: perfect competition has free entry and exit. If firms earn economic profit, new firms enter, supply shifts right and price falls until P = minimum ATC, where economic profit = 0. Many suppliers and perfect information are defining features, and substitutes exist (identical products are perfect substitutes).

Zero economic profit still lets firms cover all costs, including the opportunity cost of the owner's capital. It is not the same as zero accounting profit.

Q34 1 mark · T5 Trade

What would shift the production possibility curve (PPC) to the right?

  • (a) An increase in the number of people producing the goods.
  • (b) An increase in infrastructure (factories and equipment).
  • (c) An increase in technology and knowledge.
  • (d) All of the above.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) All of the above.  🟢 confident

The PPC shows the maximum output from given resources and technology. More labour (a), more capital (b) and better technology (c) each expand what can be produced, so the curve shifts out. (The same three are the sources of growth in T7 Growth.)

Q36 1 mark · T5 Trade

When a tariff is introduced, producer surplus is ____ than that of a closed economy and ____ than that of a fully open economy.

  • (a) greater; less
  • (b) less; less
  • (c) greater; greater
  • (d) less; greater
Answer & working

Answer: (d) less; greater  🟢 confident

Rank the domestic price: closed P* > PW + tariff > PW (free trade). Domestic producers like a high price, so PS: closed > tariff > free trade. With a tariff, PS is less than closed and greater than fully open.

CS runs the other way (closed < tariff < free trade). Read whether the blank asks about PS or CS. The two 2021 papers swap them.

Q37 1 mark · T5 Trade

When a tariff is introduced, consumer surplus is ____ than that of a closed economy and ____ than that of a fully open economy.

  • (a) greater; less
  • (b) less; less
  • (c) greater; greater
  • (d) less; greater
Answer & working

Answer: (a) greater; less  🟢 confident

Price ranking: closed P* > PW + t > PW. Consumers like a low price, so CS: free trade > tariff > closed. With a tariff, CS is greater than closed and less than fully open.

This is the mirror image of the PS question. With CS + PS together (S1 Q36) the answer is also “greater; less”, because the tariff recovers part of the gains from trade but leaves a DWL.

Answer & working

Answer: (c) $22.50  🟢 confident

  1. D: P = 7 − Q/5; S: P = 1 + Q/5 ⇒ Q* = 15, P* = $4.
  2. PS = triangle above S and below P*: ½ × 15 × (4 − 1) = $22.50.
Supply starts at P = 1, not 0, so the height is 4 − 1 = 3, not 4. (Using 4 gives $30, which isn't offered; $45 forgets the ½.)

Q39 1 mark · T2 Intervention

Refer to the Figure. If a $2 per unit tax was charged to suppliers, how much tax revenue will the government receive?

  • (a) $20
  • (b) $15
  • (c) $10
  • (d) $30
Answer & working

Answer: (a) $20  🟢 confident

  1. Read the curves: D: P = 7 − Q/5; S: P = 1 + Q/5. No-tax equilibrium: Q* = 15, P* = $4.
  2. A $2 tax on suppliers shifts S up by $2: P = 3 + Q/5.
  3. New equilibrium: 7 − Q/5 = 3 + Q/5 ⇒ QT = 10. Buyers pay PB = 7 − 2 = $5, sellers keep PS = $3.
  4. Revenue = tax × QT = 2 × 10 = $20.

Bonus: DWL = ½ × 2 × (15 − 10) = $5. The curves have equal and opposite slopes, so the burden is split evenly: $1 to buyers and $1 to sellers. Method: T2 Intervention.

$30 = 2 × 15 uses the old quantity. Revenue always uses the quantity after the tax.

Q40 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Refer to the Figure. Suppose the economy is at point D. A temporary and unanticipated rise in labour productivity will shift ____ and move the economy along ____, meaning that the inflation rate is ____ and output is ____ in the short run.

  • (a) AD1 to AD2; SRAS1; decreasing; decreasing
  • (b) SRAS2 to SRAS1; AD1; increasing; decreasing
  • (c) LRAS; AD1; decreasing; decreasing
  • (d) SRAS1 to SRAS2; AD1; decreasing; increasing
Answer & working

Answer: (d) SRAS1 to SRAS2; AD1; decreasing; increasing  🟢 confident

  1. Higher labour productivity lowers unit costs, so SRAS shifts right/down: SRAS₁ → SRAS₂.
  2. “Temporary” means potential (LRAS) is unchanged, so LRAS doesn't move.
  3. The economy slides along AD₁ from D to B: inflation ↓, output ↑ (above Y*, an expansionary gap).
“Temporary” is the clue that it's an SRAS shift, not LRAS. A permanent productivity rise would also shift LRAS right.

Q41 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Refer to the Figure. SRAS curve will shift right/downwards if:

  • (a) expected inflation rate was to rise.
  • (b) actual inflation rate was to rise.
  • (c) expected inflation rate was to fall.
  • (d) actual inflation rate was to fall.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) expected inflation rate was to fall.  🟢 confident

Firms and workers build expected inflation into wages and prices (L6: “if 4% is expected, workers negotiate 4% pay rises”). Lower expected inflation means smaller wage and price rises at every output level, so SRAS shifts down/right. A change in actual inflation is the variable on the axis: a movement along SRAS.

In 2026 the vertical axis is the price level, so this becomes “lower expected inflation / lower input costs → SRAS right”. Same logic.

Q42 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

With the imposition of stricter immigration laws, the amount of foreign workers is expected to decrease. How would this affect the economy in the long run?

  • (a) The short-run aggregate supply curve will shift to the left.
  • (b) The long-run aggregate supply curve will shift to the left.
  • (c) The economy will move down along the short-run aggregate supply curve.
  • (d) The economy will move down along the long-run aggregate supply curve.
Answer & working

Answer: (b) The long-run aggregate supply curve will shift to the left.  🟢 confident

L6: LRAS (potential GDP) is “determined solely by the economy's access to factors of production, e.g. labour force, capital”. Fewer workers mean less potential output, so LRAS shifts left. The question says “in the long run”, so the answer is about LRAS, not SRAS.

In the 2026 lecture, immigration also appears as an AD shifter (2022: population↑ ⇒ C↑). If a question asks about the short-run spending effect, it's AD. If it asks about the labour force or the long run, it's LRAS.

Q43 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

If the Australia dollar decreases in value relative to other currencies, how does this affect the aggregate demand curve?

  • (a) This will move the economy down along a stationary aggregate demand curve.
  • (b) This will move the economy up along a stationary aggregate demand curve.
  • (c) This will shift the aggregate demand curve to the left.
  • (d) This will shift the aggregate demand curve to the right.
Answer & working

Answer: (d) This will shift the aggregate demand curve to the right.  🟢 confident

A weaker AUD makes Australian goods cheaper overseas (X↑) and imports dearer (M↓). NX↑, so AD = C + I + G + NX shifts right.

Exchange rates aren't a 2026 lecture topic as such, but the “NX is an AD component” logic is. Treat it as an AD shifter.

Q44 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

Suppose the Australian economy is currently in the expansionary stage of the business cycle. Which policy could the Government adopt to bring the economy back towards long run equilibrium?

  • (a) Decrease income tax rates for all workers.
  • (b) Increase the cash rate by 25 basis points.
  • (c) Delay planned construction projects.
  • (d) Decrease taxes on cigarettes.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) Delay planned construction projects.  🟡 judgement call

Expansionary stage (Y > Y*) calls for contractionary policy (AD left). (a) and (d) cut taxes, which is expansionary, so they're wrong. Both (b) and (c) are contractionary. But (b) is monetary policy, set by the RBA (L7: “The RBA controls the Cash Rate”). The Government controls fiscal policy: delaying construction cuts G. So (c).

🟡 There is no answer key. The word “Government” is the discriminator. If a 2026 question says “policymakers” or “the RBA”, the cash-rate rise is the answer.

Who does what: Government = fiscal (G, T); RBA = monetary (cash rate). 2026 questions use this split in their dropdowns.

Q45 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

GDP will ____ if consumption increases by $30 billion and exports decrease by $10 billion, keeping other components constant.

  • (a) decrease
  • (b) increase
  • (c) not change
  • (d) increase first and then decrease
Answer & working

Answer: (b) increase  🟢 confident

Y = C + I + G + (X − M). ΔY = +30 − 10 = +$20b, so GDP increases.

Q46 1 mark · T4 AD-AS

If nominal GDP rises we can conclude that

  • (a) prices have risen and production remains constant.
  • (b) production has risen and prices remain constant.
  • (c) production has risen or prices have risen, or both have risen.
  • (d) prices have fallen and production has risen.
Answer & working

Answer: (c) production has risen or prices have risen, or both have risen.  🟢 confident

Nominal GDP = Σ P × Q at current prices. A rise can come from P↑, Q↑ or both. You can't separate them without real GDP or a price index. (Strictly, one could even fall if the other rises by more, but (c) is the only safe conclusion offered.)

Q47 1 mark · T4 AD-AS · T7 Growth

One reason why economists do NOT consider GDP the perfect measure of well-being of a society is because

  • (a) GDP is not adjusted for pollution.
  • (b) GDP is adjusted for the increase in crime rates.
  • (c) GDP includes the value of leisure.
  • (d) GDP cannot account for inflation.
Answer & working

Answer: (a) GDP is not adjusted for pollution.  🟢 confident

GDP counts market production. It ignores environmental damage (pollution), leisure, home production and distribution. (b) and (c) are false statements (GDP makes neither adjustment). (d) is false because real GDP does adjust for inflation.

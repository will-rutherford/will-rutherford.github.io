The big idea

Why are some countries richer than others, and can poor countries catch up? The standard of living is measured by real GDP per capita, and the only thing that can raise it forever is labour productivity (output per worker, y = Y/L). Labour productivity rises when workers get more capital (k = K/L ↑) or better technology (A ↑). The lecture's Solow-type growth model shows that capital alone can't keep growth going: diminishing returns push the economy to a steady state where y stops growing. A higher saving rate, lower population growth or better technology raises the level of the steady state, but only continual technological progress keeps GDP per capita growing.

The lecture's real-world story: GDP per capita in 2022 was US$76k (US) and US$65k (Australia), but only US$2.4k in India. For most of history incomes stagnated (Malthus). Some "late-comers" caught up fast, some stalled (the middle-income trap) and some went backwards (poverty traps, weak institutions).

How this is examined (S1 2026 final Q27–29, S2 2025 Q21–22, practice exam Q6): one diagram with y = f(k), sf(k) and the (n+δ)k "Maintenance" line, sometimes with a dashed tangent line. You click the steady state, pick dropdowns (saving too much or too little? which line shifts? up or down? new k* higher or lower?), read steady-state consumption off labelled axes, and give a short-answer reason. Week 13: "make sure you can identify Steady States, and the Golden Rule Steady State."

1 · Measuring a country's income

GDP per capita = GDP ÷ Population. This is output per head, which can be read as average income (recall from L5 that GDP = expenditure = income). Real GDP per capita is the average quantity of goods and services available to each resident, so it's an (imperfect) measure of the standard of living.

Worked example. China's GDP is about 10.6× Australia's (17,963/1,693), but Australia's GDP per capita is 5.1× China's (65,009.8/12,720.2). Implied populations: Australia = 1,693bn / 65,009.8 ≈ 26.0m and China = 17,963bn / 12,720.2 ≈ 1,412m. A US resident has 76,329.6/2,410.9 ≈ 31.7× the average income of an Indian resident. Total GDP measures size; GDP per capita measures living standards.

  • Economic growth means the long-run growth of real GDP per capita. It's about producing more goods and services, not about money; money is only the unit of account.
  • Nominal GDP per capita is not useful for growth because it can rise from inflation alone.
  • Caveat: it's an average, so it ignores income distribution. With very unequal incomes, "average income" can mislead.
Real vs nominal GDP and GDP per capita as a living-standards measure (with its limitations). Same idea.

2 · Why labour productivity is everything

Break GDP per capita into pieces:

Y/Pop = (Y/Hours) × (Hours/L) × (L/Pop)
GDP per capita = Labour productivity × Hours per worker × Fraction of population working

  • Hours per worker have a ceiling (< 24 hours a day).
  • The fraction of the population working has a ceiling (≤ 100%).
  • ⇒ The only way to raise GDP per capita indefinitely is to raise labour productivity (Y/L, output per worker or per hour worked).

Worked example. Y = $2,000bn, hours worked = 40bn, workers L = 14m, population = 27m.
Y/Hours = 2,000bn/40bn = $50 per hour; Hours/L = 40bn/14m = 2,857.1 hrs; L/Pop = 14/27 = 0.5185.
Product: 50 × 2,857.1 × 0.5185 = $74,074, which matches 2,000bn/27m = $74,074 ✓.

What raises labour productivity? (1) Capital per worker k = K/L (capital deepening) and (2) technological change (A).

3 · Production function, technology and TFP

A production function links inputs (capital K, labour L) to output Y for a given technology. New technology gives a different production function. The lecture's carrot example: no fertiliser gives Y = FA(K,L) = K × L, and with fertiliser Y = FB(K,L) = 2 × K × L. You get more output from the same K and L, which is technological progress.

Cobb-Douglas: Y = A Kα L1−α, with 0 < α < 1.
• α = capital share of income (αY goes to capital owners). 1 − α = labour share ((1−α)Y is labour income).
• A = total factor productivity (TFP): the efficiency with which inputs become output. It's a catch-all for technology, human capital, rainfall, the legal system and so on. Technological progress means A grows.
• Per worker: y = Y/L = A kα, where k = K/L. Labour productivity depends on k and A.

Worked example. A = 2, α = 0.5, K = 100, L = 25. Y = 2 × 1000.5 × 250.5 = 2 × 10 × 5 = 100. Capital income = αY = 50 and labour income = (1−α)Y = 50. Per worker: k = 100/25 = 4 and y = A kα = 2 × 40.5 = 4 = Y/L = 100/25 ✓. If A rises to 2.5, Y = 125 with the same inputs, which is technological progress.

Falling labour share (RBA): more rental and imputed rent income, the mining boom (capital-intensive) and ATMs and apps replacing bank tellers.

Capital vs technology (common MCQ)

CapitalTechnology
Physical, tangible, durable manufactured goods used to produce other goods and services over a long period, e.g. computers, robots, machines, buildings, roads. (Not human or financial capital.)Intangible methods or processes that turn inputs into outputs. Technological progress means more output from the same inputs. Sources: better-trained workers (human capital is sometimes treated as technology), better organisation and management, better (not just more) machinery.
Technological improvements need not require new machines. Ford's moving assembly line (specialisation plus conveyors) cut assembly time from 12 hours to 90 minutes and the Model T price from $850 to $300, by reorganising production.

4 · Diminishing returns to capital

Hold A fixed and write y = f(k). The curve slopes up (more capital per worker means more output per worker) but gets flatter: each extra unit of k adds less y. Lecture example: 20 programmers sharing 1 computer, so a 2nd computer roughly doubles output. With 21 computers, a 22nd adds almost nothing because the workers are now the limiting factor.

Worked example. With y = A k0.5, doubling k multiplies y by 20.5 ≈ 1.41, not 2. So doubling capital per worker raises output per worker by only 41%.

5 · The growth model: how k changes

This is the Solow (neoclassical) growth model, which QCAA doesn't cover. Learn the three curves and the one equation; the lecturer says the derivation is "for my amusement… don't need to memorise."

Assumptions: households save a fraction s (0 < s < 1) of income and saving = investment (via banks), so investment = sY and consumption = (1−s)Y. The population and labour force grow at rate n. Capital depreciates at rate δ (0 < δ < 1).

Aggregate capital: ΔK = sY − δK (new investment minus depreciation).

Per worker (since k = K/L, %Δk = %ΔK − %ΔL = (sY/K − δ) − n):

Δk = s·y − (δ + n)·k

  • s·y = s·f(k) is investment per worker, which raises k.
  • δk is depreciation per worker, which lowers k.
  • nk is dilution: new workers must be equipped. With K fixed and L↑, k = K/L falls.
  • (δ + n)k is break-even investment, also called required investment or the maintenance line (the exam writes it as (n+δ)k "Maintenance"). It's the investment per worker needed just to keep k constant. It's a straight line through the origin with slope δ + n.

Worked example (aggregate). K = 200, Y = 80, s = 0.25, δ = 0.08. Investment sY = 20 and depreciation δK = 16, so ΔK = 20 − 16 = +4 and %ΔK = 4/200 = 2%. If n = 1%, then %Δk = 2% − 1% = 1%.

Worked example (per worker). y = √k, s = 0.3, δ = 0.1, n = 0.05 (so δ + n = 0.15). At k = 1: y = 1, s·y = 0.30 and (δ+n)k = 0.15, so Δk = +0.15 and next period k = 1.15 (y ≈ 1.072).

6 · Steady state

The steady state (the model's long-run equilibrium) is where per-worker variables stop changing: Δk = 0, so Δy = 0. This happens where investment per worker exactly covers break-even investment:

s·f(k*) = (δ + n)·k*   ⇒   y* = f(k*)

On the graph: k* is where the saving curve sf(k) crosses the break-even line (δ+n)k. Go straight up to f(k) to read y*.

Worked example. y = √k, s = 0.3, δ + n = 0.15. Solve 0.3√k = 0.15k ⇒ √k = 2 ⇒ k* = 4, y* = 2, investment = sy* = 0.6 = (δ+n)k* = 0.15 × 4 ✓, consumption c* = y* − sy* = 1.4.

The steady state is NOT where f(k) crosses (δ+n)k. That point is where consumption would be zero. Always use the saving curve.
Aggregate variables in the steady state: k = K/L is constant, so K and Y grow at the same rate as L, i.e. at rate n. Per-worker growth is 0. (This follows from the model; the lecture lists "long-run growth rates of aggregate variables" as something parameter changes affect but doesn't spell out the number.)

7 · Transition to the steady state

  • Below k*: investment exceeds what's needed to replace worn-out capital and equip new workers, so k rises and y rises toward y*.
  • Above k*: required (maintenance) investment exceeds actual investment, so k falls and y falls toward y*.
  • Growth slows as k approaches k*: because of diminishing returns, sf(k) rises at a diminishing rate while (δ+n)k rises at a constant rate, so the gap shrinks. This is why poorer countries (further below their steady state) can grow faster and catch up, if they share the same s, n and A.

Worked example. Same economy (k* = 4). At k₀ = 9: y = 3, sy = 0.9, (δ+n)k = 1.35, so Δk = −0.45 < 0 and k falls toward 4 while y falls toward 2.

8 · Changes in s, n and A (comparative statics)

Start in a steady state, change one parameter, see which curve moves, and find the new intersection of the saving curve with the break-even line.

Increase in saving rate s₀ → s₁

Numbers: s₁ = 0.45 ⇒ 0.45√k = 0.15k ⇒ √k = 3 ⇒ k₁* = 9, y₁* = 3. The level of GDP per capita rises permanently, but growth is only temporary (short-run growth during the transition, then back to 0).

Increase in population growth rate n₀ → n₁

Numbers: δ + n₁ = 0.20 ⇒ 0.3√k = 0.2k ⇒ √k = 1.5 ⇒ k₁* = 2.25, y₁* = 1.5. More new workers need equipping each period. Higher population growth means a lower standard of living, with negative per-capita growth during the transition. A fall in n (e.g. lower fertility) does the reverse: the maintenance line shifts down (flatter) and k* and y* are higher. A rise in δ works exactly like a rise in n.

Increase in technology A₀ → A₁

Numbers: 0.3 × 1.2√k = 0.15k ⇒ √k = 2.4 ⇒ k₁* = 5.76, y₁* = 1.2 × 2.4 = 2.88. y rises for two reasons: the direct effect of better technology, and more capital accumulated as a result.

9 · Golden Rule: the optimal saving rate

Why not save 100%? With no government, Y = C + I and I = sY, so C = (1 − s)Y. At s = 1, C = 0: workers starve ("a slave labour camp, not utopia"). People care about consumption, so a benevolent planner picks s to maximise steady-state consumption per worker.

Per worker (L = Pop): c = y − sy. In a steady state sy* = (δ+n)k*, so

c* = f(k*) − (δ + n)k*  (SS output minus SS break-even investment)

On the graph, c* is the vertical gap between f(k) and the (δ+n)k line at k*, which equals the gap between f(k*) and sf(k*).

Raising k* raises output (+MPK·Δk*) but also raises required investment (+(δ+n)·Δk*), so Δc* = (MPK − (δ+n))·Δk*, where MPK = slope of f(k).

At the current k*…MeaningTo raise c*
f(k) steeper than (δ+n)k (MPK > δ+n)k* < kG*: saving too littleraise k*, i.e. increase s
f(k) flatter than (δ+n)k (MPK < δ+n)k* > kG*: saving too muchlower k*, i.e. decrease s
Slopes equal (MPK = δ+n)k* = kG*: Golden Rulealready at maximum

Worked example. y = √k, δ + n = 0.15. The slope of √k is 1/(2√k). Set it equal to 0.15: √k = 3.33 ⇒ kG* ≈ 11.1, yG* = 3.33, iG* = 0.15 × 11.1 = 1.67, cG* = 3.33 − 1.67 = 1.67. The required sG solves sG × 3.33 = 1.67 ⇒ sG = 0.5. Our economy with s = 0.3 has k* = 4 < 11.1 and c* = 1.4 < 1.67, so it's saving too little. (Too much: s = 0.7 gives k* = (0.7/0.15)² ≈ 21.8 and c* = 4.67 − 3.27 = 1.40, also below 1.67.)

Bonus shortcut (not in the lecture; derived from Cobb-Douglas): with y = Akα, the Golden Rule saving rate is sG = α, the capital share. Here α = 0.5, so sG = 0.5 ✓. Use it only to check a numeric answer.

Lecture takeaway: too low a saving rate traps developing countries at low y ("if you earn $2/day and need $1.90 for food and shelter…"), but a high saving rate doesn't guarantee a high standard of living (China, "Is thrift a virtue?").

10 · Technology, feedback and the middle-income trap

  • Feedback loop: better technology raises k*, and more capital per worker gives firms an incentive to improve tools further (R&D). This positive loop can sustain long-run growth.
  • Middle-income trap: a country grows at first by accumulating capital without technological progress. Then diminishing returns kick in and growth stalls. Causes: protectionism (trade barriers, protected local monopolies) blocks imports of the latest foreign capital goods and removes the competitive pressure to innovate. Centralised infrastructure projects can also crowd out an environment for innovative business.
  • Fix: continual technological progress, through trade (importing the latest machinery and production and management techniques) or incentives for domestic R&D.

11 · Malthus, population and the poverty trap

Malthus (1766–1834): any improvement in economic conditions gets diluted by population growth, so poverty is inevitable. That's wrong for today's developed world, but the Malthusian trap may be real for under-developed countries. Vicious circle: low income per capita ⇄ high population growth.

n = birth rate − death rate, and both depend on income per capita y:

Income per capitaDeath rateBirth raten
LowHigh (poor healthcare)High (no birth control; children are economic resources)low
Rising / middleFalls quickly (healthcare affordable)Unchanged or rises (better maternity and infant care)high
HighLittle further changeFalls rapidly (high opportunity cost of children: lost income, education, career)low

Assume δ = 0, so break-even investment = n·k with slope n. Because n depends on income, the break-even curve is S-shaped: shallow at low k (low n), steep at middle k (high n), shallow again at high k (low n). So it can cross s·f(k) three times:

Policies to escape the poverty trap:

  1. Increase the saving rate (at least temporarily): this shifts the investment curve s·f(k) up and eliminates the poverty-trap steady state.
  2. Population control: policies encouraging low birth rates even at low incomes shift the break-even investment curve down.

Institutions matter: with poor property rights (expropriation by corrupt officials or criminals, caused by weak legal institutions, war and conflict, or complex and inefficient bureaucracy), people won't invest in capital or technology. Inefficient or defunct financial markets stop savings being channelled into investment. Both hamper growth.

Step-by-step methods

M1 · Find the steady state on a diagram ("click the intersection")
  1. Identify the three lines: concave and highest = y = f(k) (output); concave and lower = sf(k) (saving); straight from the origin = (n+δ)k (maintenance).
  2. Click where sf(k) crosses (n+δ)k, not the f(k) crossing and not the tangent point.
  3. k* is directly below that point. y* is where the vertical line at k* meets f(k).
M2 · Transition from a given k₀
  1. Is k₀ left or right of k*? Answer "lower than" or "higher than" the SS ratio.
  2. At k₀, compare heights. Left of k*: sf(k) is above (n+δ)k, so required investment is lower than actual investment and k rises. Right of k*: required investment is higher than actual investment and k falls.
  3. It adjusts until it reaches k* (never the Golden Rule, k₀ or the origin).
  4. Per-capita output increases/falls towards y* (not "towards sf(k*)": that's investment, not output).
M3 · Golden Rule comparison (saving too much/too little?)
  1. Find the dashed line drawn tangent to f(k). It's parallel to (n+δ)k, and its touching point is kG. (No tangent drawn? Find where f(k) has the same slope as the maintenance line.)
  2. Find k* (M1).
  3. k* left of kG ⇒ k* < kG ⇒ saving too little ⇒ raise s to increase c*.
    k* right of kG ⇒ k* > kG ⇒ saving too much ⇒ lower s to increase c*.
    Same point ⇒ saving optimally.
  4. Alternative test at k*: f(k) steeper than the maintenance line ⇒ too little; flatter ⇒ too much.
M4 · Read SS consumption (and investment) off labelled axes
  1. Find k* (M1). Read the height of f(k) at k* (= y*) and the height of the intersection sf(k*) = (n+δ)k* (= investment i*).
  2. c* = y* − sf(k*), i.e. the f(k) label at k* minus the label at the intersection height.
  3. Ignore heights at kG or at the point where f(k) crosses (n+δ)k, unless the question asks about the Golden Rule. Golden Rule consumption = f(kG) − (n+δ)kG.
M5 · Comparative statics dropdown ("This will shift the ___ ___; new k* is ___")
  1. Map the change to a parameter: fertility, births, baby bonus or immigration → n; wear and tear → δ; thrift, superannuation or saving incentives → s; innovation, R&D, better management or TFP → A.
  2. n or δ → Maintenance line: up if ↑, down if ↓. s → Saving line: up if ↑, down if ↓. A → Per capita output line up if ↑ (the saving line follows).
  3. Find the new intersection of saving and maintenance. k* higher if maintenance goes down, saving goes up or A goes up; lower otherwise.
  4. Same direction for y*. Short-run per-capita growth has the same sign as the change in k*; long-run per-capita growth is back to 0.
M6 · Numeric steady state (if numbers are given)
  1. Write Δk = s·f(k) − (δ+n)k. For a given k: compute y = f(k), then s·y, then (δ+n)k, and subtract. Sign: + means k rises, − means k falls.
  2. SS: set s·f(k) = (δ+n)k. With f(k) = A√k: √k* = sA/(δ+n), so k* = (sA/(δ+n))² and y* = A√k*.
  3. c* = y* − s·y* = (1−s)y*. Check: s·y* = (δ+n)k*.
  4. Golden Rule with f = A√k: slope A/(2√k) = δ+n ⇒ √kG = A/(2(δ+n)), and sG = 0.5 (= α).
M7 · GDP per capita and decomposition
  1. GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population. Watch the units (bn vs m: 1bn/1m = 1,000).
  2. Y/Pop = (Y/Hours) × (Hours/L) × (L/Pop). Fill in the known pieces and solve for the missing one.
  3. Growth rates of a ratio: %Δ(K/L) ≈ %ΔK − %ΔL. For the Cobb-Douglas example: y = A kα.
M8 · Poverty-trap diagram (which equilibria are stable?)
  1. Find every crossing of s·f(k) with the S-shaped n(k)·k curve.
  2. For each region between crossings: s·f(k) above n·k ⇒ Δk > 0 (arrow right); below ⇒ Δk < 0 (arrow left).
  3. Arrows pointing into a crossing ⇒ stable steady state (A, C). Arrows pointing away ⇒ unstable (B, the threshold).
  4. The low stable one = poverty trap. Escape: raise s (saving curve up) or lower n (break-even curve down).

How it's examined

Format seen in the practice exam (Q6, ~18 marks), S1 2026 final (Q27–29, 10 marks) and S2 2025 final (Q21–22, 10 marks). It's always the same set-up text: "per capita output depends only on the capital-labour ratio k; saving rate s; population growth rate n; depreciation rate δ", plus one diagram. The examples below are fresh, using the y = √k economy from Learn.

Type 1 · "Click on the intersection that determines the steady state." 2 marks

Diagram: f(k), sf(k) and (n+δ)k, with a dashed tangent to f(k) further right. Candidate points: f(k) ∩ (n+δ)k at k = 44.4 (off-screen in our scale), sf(k) ∩ (n+δ)k at k = 4, and the tangent point at k = 11.1.

Answer: the point at k = 4, where sf(k) meets (n+δ)k. The tangent point is the Golden Rule, not the steady state, which is a distractor.

Type 2 · Transition fill-in-the-blanks ~10 marks

"The economy starts at k₀ = 2 (k* = 4). Its initial capital-labour ratio is ___ the SS ratio. As required investment to maintain k is ___ the amount of investment, k will ___. It adjusts until it reaches ___. Per capita output will ___."

Answer: lower than. At k = 2: actual investment = 0.3√2 = 0.424 and required = 0.15 × 2 = 0.30, so required is lower than actual. k will rise until it reaches k*, and per capita output will increase towards y* (from √2 = 1.41 to 2).

Mirror case (k₀ right of k*): higher than / higher than / fall / k* / fall towards y*. That was the practice exam answer.

Type 3 · Golden Rule MCQ 2–4 marks

Right-hand diagram: s = 0.7, n + δ = 0.3. "The SS capital-labour ratio is ___ the Golden Rule ratio; relative to the Golden Rule saving rate the country is saving ___."

Answer: k* = (0.7/0.3)² ≈ 5.44 and kG = (0.5/0.3)² ≈ 2.78, so k* is higher than the Golden Rule ratio and the country is saving too much. To raise steady-state consumption it should decrease its saving rate. Check: c* = (1 − 0.7) × 2.33 = 0.70, while cG = 1.667 − 0.3 × 2.78 = 0.83.

Type 4 · Read SS consumption off a labelled diagram 2 marks

The vertical axis is labelled y₁ = 0.6, y₂ = 1.4, y₃ = 1.67, y₄ = 2, y₅ = 3.33. The saving curve meets the maintenance line at k₁ (height y₁). f(k₁) = y₄, and the tangent point k₂ has f(k₂) = y₅. "SS consumption is: (a) y₅ − y₁ (b) y₄ − y₁ (c) y₄ (d) y₅ − y₃."

Answer: (b) y₄ − y₁ = 2 − 0.6 = 1.4, i.e. output at k* minus investment at k*. (d) is Golden-Rule consumption, (a) mixes two different k's, and (c) is output, not consumption. Follow-up: "this country can raise SS consumption by…" → increasing its saving rate (k₁ < k₂).

Type 5 · Comparative statics dropdowns ~6 marks

(i) "More women in the workforce raises the opportunity cost of children, so fertility falls." → shifts the Maintenance line down, and the new SS k is higher.
(ii) "A national superannuation scheme raises s." → Saving line, up, k* higher (y* higher; growth temporarily positive).
(iii) "Machines wear out faster (δ↑)." → Maintenance line, up, k* lower.
(iv) "A new management technique raises TFP." → Per capita output line, up (saving line up too), k* higher.
(v) "A baby boom (n↑)." → Maintenance line, up, k* lower; numerically k* 4 → 2.25 and y* 2 → 1.5 when n + δ goes from 0.15 to 0.20.

Distractors in the real dropdowns: "Tangent to the output line" never shifts on its own; "first up then down" and "uncertain" are never right for a single one-off change.

Type 6 · Short answer: policy and the Golden Rule 4 marks

"The economy's k* is above the Golden Rule level. Will a baby bonus that raises n move k* closer to the Golden Rule? Why?"

Model answer: Yes. A higher population growth rate makes the maintenance line (n+δ)k steeper, so the steady-state capital-labour ratio falls. Because k* is currently above the Golden Rule level, a lower k* moves it towards the Golden Rule level (and the economy is no longer over-saving by as much). Practice-exam version (k* below the Golden Rule): No. k* falls, which moves it further away.

Structure for 4 marks: (1) the answer, (2) which line shifts and how, (3) the effect on k*, (4) a comparison with kG.

Type 7 · Concept MCQs (possible)

"Which can raise GDP per capita indefinitely?" → labour productivity growth (technology), not more hours or higher participation. "Which is capital?" → a physical, durable produced input (a truck), not a patent method or worker skills. "Which point in the poverty-trap diagram is unstable?" → the middle one (B). "Policy to escape a poverty trap?" → raise s / lower n (population control) / improve property rights.

Traps & misconceptions

The steady state is sf(k) ∩ (n+δ)k. Not f(k) ∩ (n+δ)k (that's where c = 0) and not the tangent point (that's the Golden Rule).
Per capita output converges to y* (on f(k)), not sf(k*). sf(k*) is investment per worker.
Higher s does not always raise consumption. If k* is already above kG, more saving lowers c*. It always raises y* and k*.
Saving "too little" ⇔ k* left of (less than) kG ⇔ f(k) steeper than the maintenance line at k*. Don't flip it.
Population growth n ↑ makes the maintenance line steeper, which the lecture and exam call shifting it "up". It does NOT shift the saving or output line. Lower fertility ⇒ maintenance line down ⇒ k* higher.
A rise in s (or a one-off rise in A) gives only a temporary rise in the growth rate and a permanent rise in the level of y. Only continual technological progress gives sustained growth.
Technology ≠ new machines. Better organisation (Ford's assembly line) is technological progress with no new capital. Capital in this course = physical capital only.
"Economic growth" = growth in real GDP per capita, not total GDP or nominal GDP. China has big GDP but lower GDP per capita than Australia.
In the poverty trap, B is an equilibrium (Δk = 0) but not a steady state, because it's unstable. A and C are the steady states.
MPK here means the slope of f(k). The Golden Rule condition is MPK = δ + n, i.e. f(k) and the break-even line have the same slope. It does not mean f(k) and the break-even line intersect.

Quick self-check

1. Y = $1,200bn, population 24m. GDP per capita?

1,200bn / 24m = $50,000.

2. Output per hour $60, 1,800 hours per worker, 50% of the population works. GDP per capita?

60 × 1,800 × 0.5 = $54,000.

3. y = √k, s = 0.2, δ = 0.06, n = 0.04. Find k*, y*, c*.

δ + n = 0.10. √k* = 0.2/0.10 = 2 ⇒ k* = 4, y* = 2, investment = 0.4 = 0.10 × 4 ✓, c* = 1.6.

4. Same economy, currently k = 9. Δk? Which way does y move?

y = 3, sy = 0.6, (δ+n)k = 0.9 ⇒ Δk = −0.3. k falls, so y falls towards y* = 2.

5. Same economy: Golden Rule k and saving rate. Is it saving too much or too little?

Slope 1/(2√k) = 0.10 ⇒ √k = 5 ⇒ kG = 25. sG × 5 = 0.10 × 25 ⇒ sG = 0.5. k* = 4 < 25 ⇒ saving too little (cG = 5 − 2.5 = 2.5 > 1.6).

6. Depreciation rate rises. Which line, which way, new k*?

Maintenance (break-even) line, up/steeper, k* and y* lower.

7. Why can't a one-off rise in the saving rate deliver permanent growth in GDP per capita?

Diminishing returns to capital: as k rises, sf(k) grows ever more slowly while required investment (δ+n)k grows linearly, so Δk → 0 at the new (higher) k*. Growth is temporary; only the level rises. Sustained growth needs continual technological progress.

8. In the poverty-trap diagram, an economy sits just to the right of the middle equilibrium B. Where does it end up?

Right of B, sf(k) > n·k ⇒ Δk > 0 ⇒ it grows to the high steady state C. Just left of B it falls back to the poverty trap A.

9. Name two lecture policies to escape a poverty trap and the curve each moves.

(1) Raise the saving rate, which shifts s·f(k) up and eliminates A. (2) Population control (lower birth rates), which shifts the break-even curve n·k down. Plus better institutions and property rights and working financial markets.

10. Why does protectionism risk a middle-income trap?

Growth by capital accumulation alone eventually stalls (diminishing returns). Protectionism blocks imports of the latest foreign capital goods and techniques and removes competitive pressure to innovate, so technology (A) doesn't grow.

Definitions
  • GDP per capita = GDP / Population = average income (ignores distribution).
  • Economic growth = long-run growth of real GDP per capita.
  • Labour productivity y = Y/L (output per worker or per hour).
  • Capital = physical, durable produced goods (machines, buildings, roads), not human or financial.
  • Technology = intangible methods turning inputs into outputs. Progress = more output from the same inputs (need not need new machines).
  • TFP (A) = catch-all efficiency: technology, human capital, rainfall, legal system.
  • α = capital share of income; 1−α = labour share.
  • Break-even / required investment / maintenance = (δ+n)k.
  • Steady state: Δk = 0 ⇒ Δy = 0.
  • Golden Rule kG*: the SS k that maximises SS consumption per worker. sG is the saving rate that delivers it.
Formulas
  • Y/Pop = (Y/Hours) × (Hours/L) × (L/Pop)
  • Cobb-Douglas: Y = A KαL1−α ⇒ y = A kα, k = K/L
  • Saving = investment = sY; C = (1−s)Y; s = 1 ⇒ C = 0
  • ΔK = sY − δK; %Δk = %ΔK − %ΔL; %ΔL = n
  • Δk = s·y − (δ+n)k (δk = depreciation, nk = dilution)
  • SS: s·f(k*) = (δ+n)k*; y* = f(k*)
  • c = y − sy; c* = f(k*) − (δ+n)k* = y* − sy*
  • Δc* = (MPK − (δ+n))·Δk*, MPK = slope of f
  • Golden Rule: MPK = δ + n (f(k) parallel to (δ+n)k)
  • If y = A√k: k* = (sA/(δ+n))²; kG = (A/(2(δ+n)))²; sG = 0.5 (in general sG = α, a derived check)
  • In SS, aggregate K and Y grow at rate n (per-worker growth = 0)
  • n = birth rate − death rate

Transition rules
  • k < k*: sf(k) > (δ+n)k ⇒ Δk > 0 ⇒ k↑, y↑ towards k*, y*.
  • k > k*: (δ+n)k > sf(k) ⇒ Δk < 0 ⇒ k↓, y↓ towards k*, y*.
  • Growth slows as k → k* (diminishing returns): further below SS means faster growth, which is how late-comers catch up.
Golden Rule rules
  • At k*: f steeper than maintenance (MPK > δ+n) ⇒ k* < kG ⇒ too little saving ⇒ raise s.
  • f flatter (MPK < δ+n) ⇒ k* > kG ⇒ too much ⇒ cut s.
  • Equal slopes ⇒ Golden Rule ⇒ optimal.
  • Too low s traps poor countries; too high s means too little consumption (China, "Is thrift a virtue?").
Poverty trap / Malthus
  • Malthus: income gains are diluted by population growth ⇒ poverty inevitable (may hold in under-developed countries).
  • Low y: high births and high deaths ⇒ low n. Middle y: deaths fall fast, births steady ⇒ high n. High y: births fall (opportunity cost of children) ⇒ low n.
  • Break-even n(k)·k is S-shaped ⇒ 3 equilibria. A = trap (high n, low y), C = ideal (low n, high y), B = unstable threshold.
  • Escape: (1) raise s, temporarily if needed (s·f(k) up, removes A); (2) population control (n·k down).
  • Institutions: poor property rights (corruption, war, bureaucracy) and broken financial markets mean low investment and low growth.
Technology & middle-income trap
  • A↑ ⇒ k*↑ ⇒ incentive for more R&D: a positive feedback loop sustains growth.
  • Middle-income trap: growth from capital accumulation alone stalls. Caused by protectionism (no foreign capital goods, no competition) and centralised projects instead of innovation.
  • Fix: trade (import the latest tech) + domestic R&D incentives.
  • Ford assembly line = technology without new capital (12 h → 90 min; $850 → $300).
Mini procedure: numeric SS
  1. Δk at a given k: y = f(k); s·y; (δ+n)k; subtract.
  2. SS: s·f(k) = (δ+n)k. With A√k: k* = (sA/(δ+n))².
  3. y* = f(k*); c* = (1−s)y*; check s·y* = (δ+n)k*.
  4. Golden: slope A/(2√k) = δ+n.

Ex: s=0.3, δ+n=0.15, A=1 ⇒ k*=4, y*=2, c*=1.4; kG=11.1, cG=1.67.

  2. Y/Pop = productivity × hours/worker × L/Pop: solve for the unknown.
  3. Ratios across countries: divide the per-capita figures. Big GDP ≠ high living standards.

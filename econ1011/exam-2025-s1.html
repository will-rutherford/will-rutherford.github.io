Section 1 · Housing market (Q1–Q4, 20 marks)

Stimulus: "This question concerns the residential housing market in Country A. The demand-and-supply diagram of the market is given below." Price in $'000, quantity in '000 units.

Q1 · Equilibrium and a fixed price 8 marks

According to the diagram, the equilibrium price in this market is [$350,000 / $600,000 / $800,000 / indeterminable] while the equilibrium quantity transacted is [210,000 / 420,000 / 580,000 / indeterminable].

If the price is fixed at $800,000, there will be a(n) [government injection / excess demand / excess supply / equilibrium] of [160,000 / 210,000 / 370,000 / 580,000] units in the market.

Answer & working

$600,000 · 420,000 · excess supply · 370,000 🟢

  1. Equilibrium = where D crosses S: the dashed lines from that point go to P = 600 and Q = 420.
  2. At P = $800k (above equilibrium): QD = 210 (read along 800 to D), QS = 580 (along 800 to S).
  3. QS > QD → excess supply = 580 − 210 = 370 ('000).
The 350 line is a decoy: it's the price where suppliers would offer only 210. The trap answers are 210 (just QD) and 580 (just QS). A price fixed above equilibrium works like a price floor (T2 methods), so you get a surplus. Only 210 would actually be transacted (the short side), but the question asks for the size of the gap.

Q2 · Mortgage Insurance scheme 6 marks

To address a housing crisis in the country, Country A's government announces a Mortgage Insurance scheme, which allows eligible first home buyers to buy a house with a lower deposit. In other words, the policy makes it easier for first home buyers who had already saved a proportion of their deposits to buy a house.

This policy will [increase the demand for / decrease the demand for / increase the supply of / decrease the supply of] residential housing properties, leading to a(n) [lower / higher / uncertain / more volatile] equilibrium price and a(n) [lower / higher / uncertain / more competitive] quantity transacted in the residential housing market.

Answer & working

increase the demand for · higher · higher 🟢

  1. The policy helps buyers (it lowers the deposit they need), so more people are willing and able to buy at every price → D shifts right. Nothing changes for sellers or builders.
  2. D right along an upward-sloping S → P↑ and Q↑ (T1 shift table).
"Helps first home buyers" sounds like it should make housing cheaper. It doesn't: demand-side help in a market with upward-sloping supply pushes the price up. This is the lecture's housing-crisis point.

Q3 · Would you support it? (written) 2 marks

Suppose you are an aspiring young person who would like to purchase a home in the future, but would not be able to utilise the announced Mortgage Insurance policy immediately (because you do not have sufficient savings even for a reduced deposit). Would you support the policy? Why or why not?

Answer & working

No (most defensible). 🟡 This is an opinion question, so the marks are for the reasoning, not the yes/no.

Model answer: "No. The policy increases demand for housing, which raises the equilibrium house price (Q2). I can't use the scheme now, so I get none of the benefit, and when I am ready to buy, houses will be more expensive and I'll need a bigger deposit. The policy makes housing less affordable for me."

A "yes" answer can get marks if it's argued properly, e.g. "Yes, because I expect to qualify once I've saved a smaller deposit, which comes sooner than a full deposit." It still has to say that prices rise in the meantime. Two marks means two points: the price effect and how it affects you.

Q4 · Urban vs rural (supply elasticity) 4 marks

It turns out that there are two narrower-defined housing markets in Country A: an urban residential housing market and a rural residential housing market. For simplicity, suppose the demand curves in the urban and rural markets are the same and have the same responses to the Mortgage Insurance policy. However, the supply in the urban market is more elastic than the supply in the rural market.

(a) Which one, the urban market or the rural market, will experience a bigger change in equilibrium price due to the Mortgage Insurance policy? [The urban market / The rural market / The price changes in the two markets are the same / There is insufficient information to determine]

(b) Which one will experience a bigger change in equilibrium quantity? [The urban market / The rural market / The quantity changes in the two markets are the same / There is insufficient information to determine]

Answer & working

(a) The rural market · (b) The urban market 🟢

  1. Demand shifts by the same amount in both markets. The market moves along supply, so supply elasticity decides how the change splits between P and Q (T1 M9).
  2. Rural S is less elastic (steeper), so P rises more and Q rises less.
  3. Urban S is more elastic (flatter), so P rises less and Q rises more.
Don't give the same market for both parts. With an identical demand shift, the market with the bigger price change always has the smaller quantity change.

Section 2 · Monopoly (Q5–Q7, 12 marks)

Stimulus: "The market for invisible cloaks (the cloaks, not their wearers, are invisible) is a monopoly. The market demand, marginal revenue and marginal cost curves are given as in the following diagram."

Q5 · Monopoly quantity, price, and vs competition 8 marks

According to the diagram, the monopolist will choose a quantity of [ ] and charge a price of $[ ].

Comparing to a perfectly competitive market, the monopolist [overproduces / underproduces / differentiatedly produces / advantageously produces] by [ ] units.

Answer & working

QM = 40 · PM = $60 · underproduces · by 32 units 🟢

  1. MR = MC where MR crosses MC: Q = 40 (at MC = 20). Check: MR = 100 − 2(40) = 20 and MC = 10 + 0.25(40) = 20 ✓.
  2. Go up to D at Q = 40: P = 100 − 40 = 60.
  3. Perfect competition: P = MC, so D crosses MC at Q = 72 (P = 28). Check: 100 − 72 = 28 = 10 + 0.25(72) ✓.
  4. 72 − 40 = 32 → underproduces by 32.
The answer is not P = 20 (that's MC at QM, where MR = MC). Price is read off demand. The 50 on the axis is just MR's Q-intercept, which you don't use here. Method: T3 methods.

Q6 · Elasticity at the monopoly price 3 marks

Calculate the price elasticity of demand at the monopoly price. You can leave your answer in fractions.

Answer & working

εD = −3/2 (|ε| = 1.5) 🟢

  1. Slope of D = (100 − 0)/(0 − 100) = −1, so ΔQ/ΔP = −1.
  2. At the monopoly point P/Q = 60/40 = 3/2.
  3. εD = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q) = −1 × 3/2 = −3/2.

Show the formula and both numbers. The course keeps the sign negative (T3 M4, T1 M6).

Use the monopoly point (40, 60) on D, not the MR = MC point (40, 20), which gives −1/2, and not the competitive point (72, 28), which gives −7/18. Don't use the midpoint formula.

Q7 · Elastic or inelastic? 1 mark

At the monopoly price, the demand is: [perfectly elastic / elastic / inelastic / perfectly inelastic]

Answer & working

elastic 🟢

|−3/2| > 1. This is always the answer: a monopolist sets MR = MC > 0, so it must be on the elastic part of D (QM = 40 is left of D's midpoint at 50).

Section 3 · AD-AS in Wonderland (Q8–Q11, 18 marks)

Stimulus (Q8): "The following diagram depicts Wonderland's Aggregate Demand (AD), Short-run Aggregate Supply (SRAS), and Long-run Aggregate Supply (LRAS). Suppose Wonderland's economy is in a short-run equilibrium but not a long-run equilibrium."

Q8 · Click the economy's point 2 marks

Click on the point Wonderland's economy is at.

Answer & working

Click the intersection of AD and SRAS (the crossing to the left of LRAS). 🟢

Short-run equilibrium = AD ∩ SRAS. It isn't a long-run equilibrium because that point is not on LRAS. Don't click AD ∩ LRAS or SRAS ∩ LRAS. (T4 methods)

Q9 · Output gap and fiscal fix 4 marks

At the equilibrium point you identified, Wonderland's real output is [above / exactly at / below / not comparable to] its potential GDP.

Which of the following is a fiscal policy that can return Wonderland's real output to its potential GDP level? [A reduction in income tax / A reduction in government purchases / A reduction in the interest rate / A reduction in money supply]

Answer & working

below · A reduction in income tax 🟢

  1. The equilibrium is left of LRAS, so Y < YP (recessionary gap).
  2. To close it we need AD → right, i.e. an expansionary policy.
  3. Fiscal = G or T. Cutting income tax → disposable income ↑ → C ↑ → AD ↑ ✓. Cutting G is contractionary (wrong way).
  4. Interest rate and money supply are monetary tools, so they're ruled out by the word "fiscal" even though a rate cut is expansionary.
Two traps in one question. "Reduction in G" sounds like policy but goes the wrong way. "Reduction in the interest rate" goes the right way but is monetary policy.

Q10 · Immigration: AD and SRAS 8 marks

Recently, Wonderland experienced an increase in immigration.

As immigrants consume goods and services in Wonderland, an increase in immigration will lead to a(n) [increase in AD / decrease in AD / increase in SRAS / decrease in SRAS]. Meanwhile, as immigrants work, they reduce the labour cost of production in Wonderland. This leads to a(n) [increase in AD / decrease in AD / increase in SRAS / decrease in SRAS].

Combining the two effects, immigration has a(n) [positive / negative / uncertain / volatile] effect on real output and a(n) [positive / negative / uncertain / volatile] effect on the price level of Wonderland.

Answer & working

increase in AD · increase in SRAS · positive (output) · uncertain (price level) 🟢

  1. More consumers → C ↑ → AD right (T4 lists population/immigration as an AD shifter).
  2. Labour costs ↓ → SRAS right.
  3. AD right: Y↑, P↑. SRAS right: Y↑, P↓. Output rises either way → positive. Price pushes go opposite ways → uncertain (depends on which shift is bigger). See T4 combined shifts.
Pick "uncertain" for the variable whose two effects conflict, not for both variables. "Volatile" is never the answer.

Q11 · Does immigration cause inflation? (written) 4 marks

Suppose Wonderland's government asks you to evaluate whether immigration causes inflation. If you know that most immigrants in Wonderland are young, skilled migrants, what would your answer likely be? Would your answer be different if most immigrants in Wonderland are dependents who are not in the labour force? Explain your answer briefly.

Answer & working

Young skilled migrants → probably not inflationary (SRAS effect is large). Dependants → yes, inflationary (AD effect only). 🟢 (written; the reasoning is what earns marks)

Model answer: "Immigration raises AD (they consume) and may raise SRAS (they work and lower labour costs). If most migrants are young and skilled, they join the labour force and add a lot to productive capacity, so the SRAS shift is large and can offset the AD shift. The price level may barely rise or even fall, so immigration is unlikely to be inflationary. If most migrants are dependants outside the labour force, they add to consumption (AD↑) but not to labour supply (SRAS unchanged). That is a pure AD increase, so the price level rises: immigration is inflationary."

Four marks means you need: (1) AD effect, (2) SRAS effect depends on joining the labour force, (3) the skilled case conclusion, (4) the dependants case conclusion.

Section 4 · Trade (Q12–Q16, 20 marks)

Stimulus (Q12–14): "Each of Country A and Country B produces two (2) goods: Fish and Chips. The number of labour hours required to produce one unit of each good in each country is given by the following table. Country A has 200 labour hours while Country B has 150 labour hours."

FishChips
Country A10 labour hours5 labour hours
Country B5 labour hours1 labour hour

Worked numbers: opportunity cost of 1 Fish = A: 10/5 = 2 Chips; B: 5/1 = 5 Chips. Opportunity cost of 1 Chip = A: 1/2 Fish; B: 1/5 Fish. Max output: A 20 F or 40 C; B 30 F or 150 C.

Q12 · Absolute and comparative advantage 4 marks

(a) Which two (2) of the following statements about absolute advantage are correct? [A has AA in Fish / A has AA in Chips / B has AA in Fish / B has AA in Chips]

(b) Which two (2) of the following statements about comparative advantage are correct? [A has CA in Fish / A has CA in Chips / B has CA in Fish / B has CA in Chips]

Answer & working

(a) B has absolute advantage in Fish; B has absolute advantage in Chips. (b) A has comparative advantage in Fish; B has comparative advantage in Chips. 🟢

  1. Absolute advantage = fewer hours per unit. B needs 5 < 10 hrs for Fish and 1 < 5 hrs for Chips, so B wins both.
  2. Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost. Fish: A gives up 2 C, B gives up 5 C, so A. Chips: A gives up ½ F, B gives up ⅕ F, so B.
The country with absolute advantage in everything still can't have comparative advantage in both goods. Don't compare total labour (200 vs 150); absolute advantage is per unit. Method: T5 methods.

Q13 · Who sells what; feasible terms of trade 4 marks

(a) If Country A and Country B can trade with each other, which two of the following statements are correct? [A will sell Fish / A will sell Chips / B will sell Fish / B will sell Chips]

(b) Which two (2) of the following terms of trade are mutually beneficial to the two countries? [2 Fish for 3 Chips / 1 Fish for 3 Chips / 2 Fish for 11 Chips / 2 Fish for 5 Chips]

Answer & working

(a) A will sell Fish; B will sell Chips. (b) 1 Fish for 3 Chips; 2 Fish for 5 Chips. 🟢

  1. Each country exports its comparative-advantage good.
  2. The ToT must lie strictly between the two opportunity costs: 2 < Chips per Fish < 5.
  3. Convert each option to Chips per 1 Fish: 3/2 = 1.5 ✗ (below A's 2, so A loses); 3 ✓; 11/2 = 5.5 ✗ (above B's 5, so B loses); 5/2 = 2.5 ✓.
"2 for 11" and "2 for 3" are set to catch you if you don't divide. Always convert to a per-1-Fish rate before comparing to 2 and 5.

Q14 · Bundle vs PPC and CPC 2 marks

Suppose Country A and Country B agree on trading 1 Fish for 4 Chips. Under this terms of trade, the consumption bundle (15 Fish, 20 Chips) is: [below A's PPC and its CPC / on A's PPC and above its CPC / above A's PPC and on its CPC / above A's PPC and its CPC]

Answer & working

above Country A's Production Possibility Curve and on its Consumption Possibility Curve. 🟢

  1. PPC test: hours needed = 15×10 + 20×5 = 250 > 200 → above (outside) the PPC. Equivalently, the PPC is F + C/2 = 20, and 15 + 10 = 25 > 20.
  2. CPC: A specialises in Fish (20 F) and trades at 1 F : 4 C. CPC: F + C/4 = 20. Check: 15 + 20/4 = 15 + 5 = 20 → exactly on the CPC (sell 5 Fish, receive 20 Chips).
The CPC is anchored at A's specialisation point (20 Fish, A's max), with slope = the terms of trade (4), not A's own opportunity cost (2).

Stimulus (Q15–16): "Country X produces and consumes coconuts. It trades coconuts in the international market. The international market price is PW. Country X's demand and supply are given in the following diagram."

Q15 · Export or import, and how many 4 marks

According to the diagram, Country X will [export / import] [ ] units of coconuts.

Answer & working

import 300 units 🟢

PW is below the autarky price (S ∩ D at Q = 600). At PW: QS = 450 and QD = 750, so imports = QD − QS = 750 − 450 = 300.

600 is the autarky quantity, a decoy. Imports are the horizontal gap between S and D at the world price.

Q16 · Surplus areas under free trade 6 marks

(a) Country X's consumers' surplus under free trade is: [A+B+C / A+B+C+D+E / A+B+C+D+E+F+G / D+E+H]

(b) Country X's producers' surplus under free trade is: [A+B+C / D+E+H / H / H+J+K]

(c) Country X's increase in total surplus due to free trade is: [D+E+F+G / F+G / H+I+J+K / J+K]

Answer & working

(a) A+B+C+D+E+F+G · (b) H · (c) F+G 🟢

Autarky (PA, Q = 600)Free trade (PW)Change
CS (under D, above price)A+B+CA+B+C+D+E+F+G+D+E+F+G
PS (above S, below price)D+E+HH−(D+E)
TotalA+B+C+D+E+HA+…+H+F+G

D+E is transferred from producers to consumers. F+G is the net gain from importing.

D+E+F+G is the consumer gain, not the total gain. I, J and K lie below PW and under the supply curve or below the price, so they're never surplus. They're decoys. Don't memorise letters; read the areas off the diagram each time (T5 methods).

Section 5 · Dragon externality & tariff game (Q17–Q22, 20 marks)

Stimulus: "The rearing of mythical dragons turns out to be environmentally beneficial: dragons eat and break down plastics, while firing power plants with little ambient pollution. They produce benefits that are not completely captured by their owners, leading to externalities. The following diagram depicts the private marginal benefit, social marginal benefit, and marginal cost of dragon rearing."

Q17 · Market vs socially optimal quantity 4 marks

According to the diagram, the equilibrium quantity in the competitive market is [ ]. Meanwhile, the socially optimal quantity is [ ].

Answer & working

Market Q = 50 · socially optimal Q = 80 🟢

The market uses private benefit: PMB = MC at Q = 50 (P = 500). Society counts the external benefit: SMB = MC at Q = 80 (value 800). Positive externality → under-production (T6 methods).

20 is a decoy: it's where MC = 200, and 200 is PMB's height at Q = 80.

Q18 · Deadweight loss (show working) 3 marks

Calculate the deadweight loss associated with the externality of dragon rearing. Show your working.

Answer & working

DWL = $6,000 🟢

The DWL is the triangle between SMB (above) and MC (below) over the units not produced, Q = 50 to 80:

  • Base (vertical side at Q = 50): SMB − MC = 900 − 500 = 400
  • Height (horizontal): 80 − 50 = 30
  • DWL = ½ × 400 × 30 = 6,000
The triangle's corners are (50, 500) on MC, (50, 900) on SMB and (80, 800) where they meet. The PMB curve is not a side. Using 800 − 500 or 900 − 200 gives the wrong base.

Q19 · Corrective tax or subsidy 3 marks

If the government wants to correct the externality, it can impose a per-unit [tax / subsidy] of the amount [$900 − $800 = $100 / $900 − $500 = $400 / $800 − $500 = $300 / $800 − $200 = $600].

Answer & working

subsidy · $800 − $200 = $600 🟢 (very confident on "subsidy". The amount is 🟢 under the lecture rule "subsidy = MEB at Q*", see below.)

  1. Positive externality → under-production → subsidise.
  2. Size = marginal external benefit at the socially optimal quantity Q* = 80: SMB(80) − PMB(80) = 800 − 200 = 600 (T6 table: "per-unit subsidy = MEB at Q*").
  3. Check: with a $600 subsidy buyers effectively get PMB + 600, which passes through (80, 800) = MC(80), so the market moves to Q = 80 ✓.
$900 − $500 = $400 is the gap at the market quantity (the DWL base). That subsidy only moves PMB up to (80, 600) < MC = 800, so you'd stop short at about Q = 70. SMB and PMB aren't parallel here, so where you measure the gap matters.

Stimulus (Q20–22): "Country A and Country B are competing to establish their dragon-rearing industry. Each of them can choose, independently, to raise tariffs on dragon imports or to maintain the status quo. The payoff matrix is given as follows:" (payoffs: A, B)

Country B
Raise tariffsStatus quo
Country ARaise tariffs2, 20, 4
Status quo3, 21, 1

Q20 · Does A have a dominant strategy? (written) 4 marks

Does Country A have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

Yes: Status quo is A's dominant strategy. 🟢

Model answer: "Yes. If B raises tariffs, A gets 3 from status quo vs 2 from raising tariffs, so status quo is better. If B keeps the status quo, A gets 1 from status quo vs 0 from raising tariffs, so status quo is better again. Status quo gives A a higher payoff whatever B does, so it is A's dominant strategy."

Compare A's first numbers down each column (T6 M6). Full marks need both comparisons with numbers.

Q21 · Does B have a dominant strategy? (written) 4 marks

Refer to the same payoff matrix as above. Does Country B have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

No, B has no dominant strategy. 🟢

Model answer: "No. If A raises tariffs, B gets 2 from raising tariffs vs 4 from status quo, so B prefers status quo. If A keeps the status quo, B gets 2 from raising tariffs vs 1 from status quo, so B prefers to raise tariffs. B's best choice depends on what A does, so B has no dominant strategy."

Read B's second numbers across each row. A common slip is reading the 2, 2 column and concluding B is indifferent.

Q22 · Nash equilibrium 2 marks

The Nash Equilibrium/Equilibria of this game is/are: [(Raise tariffs, Raise tariffs) / (Status quo, Raise tariffs) / (Status quo, Status quo) / (Raise tariffs, Raise tariffs) and (Status quo, Status quo)]

Answer & working

(Status quo, Raise tariffs), i.e. A plays status quo and B raises tariffs, payoffs (3, 2). 🟢

  1. A always plays its dominant strategy: Status quo.
  2. B's best response to A's status quo: raise tariffs (2 > 1).
  3. Check A: given B raises tariffs, A's status quo 3 > 2 ✓. Neither wants to deviate.
  4. Check others: (R, R): A would switch to SQ (3 > 2), so not Nash. (SQ, SQ): B would switch to R (2 > 1), so not Nash. (R, SQ): A would switch (1 > 0), so not Nash.

Order in the options is (A's strategy, B's strategy).

Section 6 · Solow growth model (Q23–Q25, 10 marks)

Stimulus: "Consider an economy in which: the per capita output level y depends only on the capital-labour ratio k; the saving rate is s; the population growth rate is n; and the depreciation rate is δ. The diagram below depicts the per-capita output line y = f(k), the saving line sf(k), and the maintenance line (n + δ)k."

Q23 · Click the steady state 2 marks

Click on the intersection that determines the steady-state capital-labour ratio.

Answer & working

The intersection of sf(k) (saving line) and (n+δ)k (maintenance line). 🟢

Steady state: sf(k*) = (n+δ)k*, so Δk = 0. Don't click where f(k) crosses (n+δ)k (top right), and don't click the tangent point on f(k) (that's the Golden Rule). See T7 methods.

Q24 · Golden Rule comparison 2 marks

According to the diagram above, this country is: [saving too much relative to its Golden Rule saving rate / saving too little / saving exactly at its Golden Rule saving rate / oscillating between saving above and below]

Answer & working

saving too much relative to its Golden Rule saving rate. 🟢

  1. The dashed line tangent to f(k) is parallel to (n+δ)k (the little arrow marks on both lines show this), so the dashed vertical marks the Golden Rule kG, where f′(k) = n + δ.
  2. The steady state (sf ∩ maintenance) is clearly to the right of kG, so k* > kG.
  3. Too much capital means the saving rate is too high: saving too much (T7 M3).
Don't decide by how close the lines look. Compare the two k values on the axis. If k* were left of kG, the answer would be "too little".

Q25 · Depreciation rises 6 marks

Suppose, due to extreme climate events, the country is experiencing a higher depreciation rate of its capital, that is, δ increases. This will shift the [Tangent to the output line / Maintenance line / Per capita output line / Saving line] [up / down / first up then down / first down then up]. Comparing to the original steady-state, the new steady-state capital-labour ratio will be [higher / lower / the same as / uncertain].

Answer & working

Maintenance line · up · lower 🟢

δ only appears in (n+δ)k, so that line pivots up (steeper) around the origin. It now meets sf(k) further left, so k* is lower, and y* is lower too. f(k) and sf(k) don't move. (T7 sheet: "Depreciation rate rises → maintenance line up → k* lower".)

What this paper teaches

  • The blocks are predictable. Housing D&S (20) → monopoly (12) → AD-AS (18) → comparative advantage + world-price diagram (20) → externality + payoff matrix (20) → Solow (10). Every block has a grid diagram with numbers or letters, and every number you need is printed on an axis.
  • Decoy values on the axes. Each grid shows extra values (350 in Q1, 50 in Q5, 20 and 200 in Q17, 600 in Q15). The traps are built from them. Before answering, say which curves cross at the point you're reading.
  • "Fixed price" = floor or ceiling. Above equilibrium → excess supply = QS − QD at that price.
  • Policy helping buyers → D↑ → P↑. Expect a short written follow-up: "who wins, who loses".
  • Elasticity comparison: the curve you move along decides the split. Less elastic → bigger ΔP and smaller ΔQ, so the two answers are always opposite markets.
  • Monopoly kit: MR = MC → up to D for price → compare to D = MC → point elasticity (1/slope × P/Q), signed fraction → always "elastic".
  • Macro dropdowns: "fiscal" rules out interest rates and money supply. When AD and SRAS shift together, one variable is certain and the other is "uncertain". The written question asks which curve the shock actually moves (labour-force migrants shift SRAS, dependants only shift AD).
  • Trade table: per-unit hours → absolute advantage. Opportunity-cost ratio → comparative advantage. ToT strictly between the ratios, so convert each option to a per-1 rate. CPC is anchored at the specialisation point with slope = ToT.
  • World-price diagram: PW below autarky → import QD − QS. Gain from trade = the triangle(s) between the curves outside the old Q. Producer loss becomes consumer gain.
  • Positive externality: market where PMB = MC, optimum where SMB = MC. The DWL triangle's base is SMB − MC at the market Q. Subsidy = SMB − PMB at the optimal Q. When the curves aren't parallel, the option that uses the market-Q gap is the trap.
  • Payoff matrix written answers: state both conditional comparisons with numbers, then conclude. A player can have a dominant strategy while the other doesn't, and then the Nash equilibrium is the dominant strategy plus the other's best response.
  • Solow: steady state = sf ∩ (n+δ)k. Golden Rule = tangent to f parallel to (n+δ)k. k* right of kG means saving too much. δ↑ or n↑ → maintenance line up → k* lower.

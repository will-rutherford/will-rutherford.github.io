1 · The big idea

Countries (and people) trade because they have different opportunity costs. Even if one country is better at making everything (absolute advantage in both goods), each country is relatively cheaper at something (comparative advantage). If each country specialises in its comparative-advantage good and trades at a price between the two countries' opportunity costs, both end up consuming bundles they could never make by themselves.

Part 2 zooms into one market. When a country opens to trade at a world price below its own price, consumers gain more than producers lose, so the country gains overall. A tariff claws some of that back for producers and the government, but creates two deadweight-loss triangles. If the tariff also pushes the world price down, there's a terms-of-trade gain that might offset them.

Lecture story: Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs (2 April 2025, minimum 10%, higher for "worst offenders"); the 2026 Supreme Court IEEPA ruling; the fish trade between Heard Island and McDonald Island. Do tariffs reduce trade deficits, revive manufacturing, or raise revenue? Mostly no.

How it's examined: one section, ~20 marks. (a) labour-hours table → absolute/comparative advantage, opportunity costs, which terms of trade work, which bundles are achievable with or without trade. (b) a tariff diagram with lettered areas → import quantities, CS/PS/revenue areas, "can the country be better off?". Sometimes there are concept MCQs on trade deficits, or tariffs in AD-AS.

2 · Concepts, one bite at a time

2.1 Exports, imports, trade openness

  • Exports: domestically produced goods/services sold to residents of other countries (Australian coal to China, and also services to Norwegian tourists in Australia).
  • Imports: foreign-produced goods/services bought by domestic residents (Thai rice at Woolies, and also an Aussie tourist's spending in Bali).
  • Trade volume = Imports + Exports. Trade openness = (Imports + Exports) / GDP. Higher openness means trade matters more to the economy.

Example: GDP $2,000b, X = $500b, M = $450b → openness = 950/2000 = 47.5%.

2.2 Absolute advantage

A country has an absolute advantage in a good if it is more productive at it: it makes the same amount with fewer inputs, or equivalently more output from the same inputs. One country can have absolute advantage in both goods. ("Life is unfair": Jonny Kim is a SEAL, a Harvard MD and an astronaut.)

Lecture example (labour hours to make 1 unit):

ChocolateDumplings
Australia10 h10 h
Belgium4 h8 h

Belgium needs fewer hours for both goods, so it has absolute advantage in both.

2.3 Opportunity cost → comparative advantage

Opportunity cost (OC) of 1 unit of good X = how many units of the other good Y you give up to make it. In an input (labour-hours) table, the hours that made 1 X could have made (hoursX / hoursY) units of Y:

OC of X = hours per X ÷ hours per Y  (units of Y per X)

Opportunity costof 1 Chocolateof 1 Dumpling
Australia10/10 = 1 dumpling10/10 = 1 chocolate
Belgium4/8 = ½ dumpling ✔ lower8/4 = 2 chocolates

Comparative advantage = the lower opportunity cost. Belgium has the CA in chocolate (½ < 1) and Australia has the CA in dumplings (1 < 2).

The OC of Y is the inverse of the OC of X (Belgium: ½ and 2). So a country with CA in one good must have a comparative disadvantage in the other, and no country can have CA in both. ("Life is fair, in a sense.")

Output tables flip the formula. Sometimes the table gives units produced per hour/worker instead of hours per unit. Then OC of X = output of Y ÷ output of X, and absolute advantage goes to the bigger number. Ask yourself: "is this hours-per-unit (smaller = better) or units-per-hour (bigger = better)?" The lecture only uses hours-per-unit (input) tables, but older exams used output tables. See Method M1.
You've seen PPFs and opportunity cost. The new part is the precise formula from a labour-hours table, and that the lecture writes terms of trade as "x units of the y-axis good per 1 unit of the x-axis good."

2.4 Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

With limited labour hours (L), all the hours used on X plus all the hours used on Y can't exceed L:

QX × hX + QY × hY = L  ⇒  QY = L/hY − (hX/hY)·QX

  • Intercepts: all hours on X gives L/hX; all hours on Y gives L/hY. (Australia, 240 h: 24 choc or 24 dumplings. Belgium, 240 h: 60 choc or 30 dumplings.)
  • Slope = −OC of the x-axis good. The PPC is a straight line because the OC is constant.
  • On the PPC: attainable and efficient (all hours used). Below it: attainable. Above it: unattainable without trade.
  • If one PPC lies entirely outside the other, that country has absolute advantage in both. The steeper PPC (with good X on the x-axis) has the higher OC of X.
  • A general PPC is bowed out (increasing OC). Then countries focus on a good but don't completely specialise. With straight lines they specialise completely.

Bundle check without trade: a bundle is achievable if hX·QX + hY·QY ≤ L.

2.5 Specialisation, terms of trade & the consumption possibility curve (CPC)

Theory of comparative advantage: specialise in the good you have the CA in, and trade for everything else.

Terms of trade (ToT) = the "price" at which the countries swap goods. The lecture writes it as x units of Y for 1 unit of X (X = x-axis good), so it matches the PPC slope.

Range of mutually beneficial ToT: the price of X has to sit between the two countries' OCs of X.

  • If the price of X is above your OC of X, you are a willing seller of X. If it's below, you're a willing buyer.
  • Trade needs one seller and one buyer, so: lower OC ≤ price ≤ higher OC. Lecture: ½ ≤ x ≤ 1 dumplings per chocolate.
  • Outside the range, both countries want to sell the same good (or both want to buy it), so there's no trade.
  • At exactly an endpoint, one country is indifferent and gains nothing. The lecture's range uses ≤ (inclusive).

CPC: once a country specialises and trades at ToT x (Y per X), it can consume along a line with slope −x through its production point. Australia makes 24 dumplings and trades at ⅔ dumpling per chocolate, so its CPC is QD = 24 − ⅔ QC. If it trades away all 24 dumplings it gets 24 × 3/2 = 36 chocolates.

  • No trade: CPC = PPC. With trade: CPC lies on or outside the PPC, because the country can always choose not to trade.

2.6 Worked example: Ozland & Nordia (full chain)

Labour hours per unit. Ozland has 120 h and Nordia has 80 h.

WineCheese
Ozland6 h3 h
Nordia4 h1 h
  1. Absolute advantage: Nordia in both (4 < 6 and 1 < 3).
  2. OC of wine: Ozland 6/3 = 2 cheese, Nordia 4/1 = 4 cheese, so Ozland has the CA in wine. OC of cheese: Ozland 3/6 = ½ wine, Nordia 1/4 = ¼ wine, so Nordia has the CA in cheese.
  3. PPCs: Ozland: 120/6 = 20 wine or 120/3 = 40 cheese, so C = 40 − 2W. Nordia: 80/4 = 20 wine or 80/1 = 80 cheese, so C = 80 − 4W.
  4. ToT range: 2 ≤ price of wine ≤ 4 cheese. Pick 3 cheese per wine.
  5. Specialise: Ozland makes 20 wine. Nordia makes 80 cheese.
  6. CPCs: Ozland C = 3(20 − W) = 60 − 3W. Nordia C = 80 − 3W.
  7. Trade: Ozland sells 8 wine for 8 × 3 = 24 cheese. Ozland consumes (12 W, 24 C) and Nordia consumes (8 W, 80 − 24 = 56 C).
  8. Gains (labour-hours check): Ozland's bundle would cost 12×6 + 24×3 = 144 h > 120 h, so it's unattainable alone. Nordia's would cost 8×4 + 56×1 = 88 h > 80 h, also unattainable. Both gain.

2.7 Why the gains, and who loses

  • The gain from trade comes from differences in OC. The bigger the gap, the wider the ToT range. Identical OCs mean no gain.
  • Exporting country: AD rises (via NX), and productivity rises (economies of scale, learning-by-exporting; exporters are more productive).
  • Importing country: lower prices, more variety, more competition (less monopoly power), and access to foreign technology.
  • Distribution: even if the country gains overall, some groups lose (import-competing producers). The winners could compensate the losers through transfers, but that may not happen.
  • Barriers to trade: transport/transaction costs, risk diversification (Covid supply chains), income distribution (protecting industries), national security (tech spillovers), politics (sanctions).
  • The 2-good, 2-country model has balanced trade (no store of value). In reality bilateral trade doesn't have to balance (see 2.8).

2.8 Trade balance, trade deficits & net foreign investment

  • Bilateral balance of A against B: XAB − MAB (positive = surplus, negative = deficit). Overall trade balance = net exports NX = X − M.
  • Bilateral deficits just reflect relative demand. "I run a trade deficit with Coles." They're not evidence of unfair trade, which is why poor export-oriented countries (Vietnam, Cambodia) top Trump's list.
  • An overall deficit means spending more than you earn, so the country is borrowing from the rest of the world or selling assets.
Identities (lecture's derivation)
Spvt = Y − C − T;   Spub = T − G;   S = Spvt + Spub = Y − C − G
Savings are used at home or abroad: S = I + NFI (NFI = net outbound foreign investment)
Y = C + I + G + NX ⇒ S = I + NX ⇒ NX = NFI
NX < 0 ⇔ NFI < 0: a trade deficit equals net inbound foreign investment (foreigners acquire our assets or lend to us).

The causation runs both ways. An attractive investment destination (the US: rule of law, innovation hub, big market, low domestic saving so S < I) gets NFI = S − I < 0, which means NX < 0. Tariffs don't target the root cause, which is low saving. The fix is to raise domestic saving (social security reform, tighter credit, smaller budget deficits).

Worked: S = $300b, I = $350b ⇒ NFI = S − I = −$50b ⇒ NX = −$50b (a $50b trade deficit, financed by foreigners buying $50b of our assets net).

2.9 Surplus refresher (from Lecture 4)

Total benefit = area under demand up to Q. Variable cost = area under supply up to Q. CS = area under demand and above the price paid. PS = area above supply and below the price received. Total (social) surplus = CS + PS (+ government revenue when there's a tax or tariff).

2.10 Opening to trade: importing country (Heard Island)

Without trade the domestic price is PH. The world price PW is set in the international market where import demand MD (the importer's excess demand, Qd − Qs, at each price) meets export supply XS (the exporter's excess supply). If PW < PH, the country imports Qd − Qs at PW.

2.12 Tariffs: what happens in the international market

A unit tariff $t is an import tax collected from the importer on each unit. At any world price P, domestic buyers now face P + t, so they demand less and local firms supply more. Excess demand shrinks at every world price, so import demand MD shifts down by t.

Who pays the tariff? The same logic as tax incidence in Lecture 4.

  • Exporters' share = PW − PWt (the drop in the world price they receive) = 6 − 5 = 1.
  • Importing country's share = PWt + t − PW (the rise in the domestic price) = 9 − 6 = 3.
  • Tariff passthrough = (PWt + t − PW) / t = 3/4 = 75% = 1 − (PW − PWt)/t.
  • The incidence depends on the relative elasticities of import demand and export supply. If the exporter has lots of other customers (the tariff-imposer is a small part of world demand), the world price barely moves and the importing country pays almost all of it. Evidence: passthrough was large for the 2018 and 2025 Trump tariffs.
The world price falls with the tariff (a "large-country" model). QCAA and most intro textbooks assume a small country, where the world price is fixed and the domestic price rises by the full t. This course draws the world price falling from PW to PWt (notation: PW, PWt, PWt+t; past exams use P0, P1, P1+t). That creates an extra area, the terms-of-trade gain G.

2.13 Tariffs: welfare in the importing country

Quantities: before, Qs = 80, Qd = 280, imports 200. After, read at the domestic price PWt + t = 9: Qst = 140, Qdt = 220, imports Mt = 80. Producers make more and consumers buy less. (60 and 300 are where the curves cut the new world price. That's a distractor, because nobody in the country faces PWt.)

Before tariff (PW)After tariff (PWt+t)Change
ConsumersA+B+C+D+E = 1960A = 1210−(B+C+D+E) = −750
ProducersF = 160B+F = 490+B = +330
Government0D+G = t × Mt = 4×80 = 320+(D+G) = +320
Total21202020G − (C+E) = 80 − 180 = −100
  • C = DWL from over-production: the units Qst − Qs are now made locally even though they'd have been cheaper to import at the original world price. ½ × 60 × 3 = 90.
  • E = DWL from under-consumption: the units Qd − Qdt would have been consumed without the tariff. ½ × 60 × 3 = 90.
  • D = the part of tariff revenue paid by domestic buyers, a transfer from consumers to the government: (9−6) × 80 = 240.
  • G = terms-of-trade gain = the fall in the world price × the new import volume = (6−5) × 80 = 80. It's paid by foreign exporters.
Can the country gain from a tariff? Only if G > C + E (the terms-of-trade gain beats the deadweight losses). That needs strong price influence in the world market: relatively price-insensitive excess demand and/or a large share of world import demand, i.e. low passthrough. High passthrough means big C and E with a tiny G, so the country loses. A small country (PW doesn't move) has G = 0 and always loses C + E.

Ranking: with a tariff, PS is greater than under free trade but less than with no trade. CS + PS is less than under free trade but greater than with no trade. (Here CS + PS = 1700, which sits between 1620 and 2120.)

2.14 Why tariffs rarely achieve their goals

  • Manufacturing vs revenue are incompatible goals. To protect producers, a tariff has to cut imports. But revenue = t × imports, so the tax base shrinks.
  • Producers ≠ workers: producer gains don't have to become jobs or wages. Reshored lines are highly automated.
  • Producers are also buyers: intermediate goods (bottles, aluminium, parts) are a large share of trade. Tariffs hurt domestic buyers unambiguously, so they raise local production costs.
  • Behavioural responses: re-routing through low-tariff third countries (hence tariffs on the real Heard & McDonald Islands, which are penguin-only), and reclassification (Subaru BRAT jump seats; the 1964 "Chicken Tax" of 25% on light trucks vs 2.5% on cars).
  • Retaliation from trading partners.
  • Non-tariff barriers: import quotas, local content requirements, export credit subsidies, red tape (e.g. Australian Design Rules, Ducati 916). They also have adverse welfare effects but are "more precise policy tools".
Quotas: the lecture only lists import quotas as a non-tariff barrier. There's no quota diagram. Textbook idea (not in the lectures): a quota that limits imports to the same amount as a tariff gives the same domestic price and the same C and E deadweight losses. The difference is that the rectangle the government would have collected goes to whoever holds the import licences ("quota rents").

Macro (Tutorial 5): a tariff lowers imports, so NX rises and AD rises. Pricier imported inputs and final goods mean SRAS falls. Net effect: the change in GDP is uncertain and the price level definitely rises. The 2025 tariffs had a limited macro effect because the actual rate (~14%) was much lower than the statutory rate (~28%): shipment lags, exemptions (e.g. TSMC at 9%), USMCA exemptions, and stockpiling before the tariffs took effect.

3 · Step-by-step methods

M1 · Absolute & comparative advantage from a table
  1. Identify the table type. "Hours/labour required to produce 1 unit" = input table (smaller is better). "Units produced per hour/worker/day" = output table (bigger is better).
  2. Absolute advantage: input table → the fewer hours for that good. Output table → the more output. Compare down each column. It can be one country for both goods, or neither if they're equal.
  3. OC of X: input table → hX/hY. Output table → outY/outX. Write the units: "units of Y per X".
  4. OC of Y = 1/(OC of X) for each country.
  5. CA in X = the country with the lower OC of X. The other country automatically has the CA in Y. (If the OCs are equal: neither, and no gains from trade.)
M2 · Is a given terms of trade mutually beneficial?
  1. Compute both countries' OC of X (in Y per X). The range is [lower OC, higher OC].
  2. Convert each option to "Y per 1 X": "a units of X for b units of Y" ⇒ price of X = b/a Y per X. (L13: "7 bread for 2 sausages" = 3.5 bread per sausage.)
  3. Make sure the option uses the same good as the denominator as your OCs. If not, invert.
  4. Inside the range = mutually beneficial. Outside = no trade (both want to sell, or both want to buy, the same good).
M3 · Who sells / buys what at a given ToT?
  1. Price of X (Y per X) vs your OC of X: price > OC → sell X (buy Y). Price < OC → buy X (sell Y).
  2. Check each country separately. If both "sell X", the ToT is outside the range and no trade happens.
M4 · PPC, CPC and bundle checks
  1. PPC intercepts: L/hX and L/hY. Equation: QY = L/hY − OCX·QX.
  2. Achievable without trade? Check hXQX + hYQY ≤ L (the hours test). "=" means on the PPC, "<" means inside.
  3. With trade: the country specialises fully in its CA good. If it produces Q̄ of Y and the ToT is p Y per X: CPC QY = Q̄Y − p·QX. If it produces Q̄ of X: CPC QY = p·(Q̄X − QX).
  4. Achievable with trade? Plug QX into the CPC. If the bundle's QY ≤ the CPC value, it's achievable ("=" means on the CPC, "<" means below it).
  5. "Achievable with trade but NOT without" means it fails the hours test and passes the CPC test.
M5 · Production / exports / imports / consumption under a ToT
  1. Production: CA good only = L ÷ hours per unit.
  2. To consume c units of the imported good, export c × (price in units of the exported good).
  3. Consumption of your own good = production − exports. Check that the partner's numbers mirror yours (my exports = their imports).
  4. Gain from trade (the lecture's cost-reduction view): hours the consumed bundle would have needed alone (hXQX + hYQY) vs the hours actually available.
M6 · Read a trade / tariff diagram (quantities)
  1. Find the price the domestic buyers and sellers face: PW under free trade, PWt + t with a tariff (never PWt).
  2. At that price read Qs (on supply) and Qd (on demand).
  3. Qd > Qs → importing Qd − Qs. Qs > Qd → exporting Qs − Qd.
  4. Tariff revenue = t × imports after the tariff = (PWt+t − PWt) × (Qdt − Qst).
M7 · Surplus areas with letters (any labelling)
  1. CS = everything under demand, above the domestic price line, from 0 to Qd.
  2. PS = everything above supply, below the domestic price line, from 0 to Qs.
  3. Tariff revenue = the rectangle of height t (from PWt up to PWt+t) and width = imports after the tariff. It's split by the old PW line into "paid by domestic buyers" (above) and the "terms-of-trade gain" (below).
  4. DWL = the two triangles between the old and new domestic price: left one (between supply, Qs and Qst) = over-production; right one (between demand, Qdt and Qd) = under-consumption.
  5. Net change for the country = ToT gain − (both DWL triangles). It's better off iff ToT gain > DWL.
  6. Watch the note "a letter across a dashed line covers both sides". A letter can be an L-shaped or two-part region.
M8 · Numeric areas from a to-scale grid
  1. Triangle = ½ × base (ΔQ) × height (ΔP). Rectangle = ΔQ × ΔP. Trapezoid = ½ (Q1 + Q2) × ΔP.
  2. CS (linear demand) = ½ × Qd × (choke price − P). PS (linear supply) = ½ × Qs × (P − supply intercept), if supply starts on the price axis.
  3. Always cross-check: ΔCS + ΔPS + ΔRevenue = G − (C + E).
M9 · Tariff incidence / passthrough
  1. Importing country's share per unit = (PWt + t) − PW. Exporters' share = PW − PWt. The two add up to t.
  2. Passthrough = importer's share / t. Exporter fraction = 1 − passthrough.
  3. Small country: PWt = PW, so passthrough = 100% and G = 0.
M10 · Trade balance identities
  1. NX = X − M. S = Y − C − G = Spvt + Spub, with Spvt = Y − C − T and Spub = T − G.
  2. S = I + NX and S = I + NFI ⇒ NX = NFI = S − I. Plug in the numbers. A negative value means a deficit = net inbound investment / borrowing.

4 · How it's examined (fresh worked examples)

Shapes calibrated from the 2026 practice exam (Q4), the S1 2026 final walked through in L13 (Q4, Q5) and the 2025 S2 final. The numbers here are new.

Setup used for QT1–QT6

Avalon and Brindle make surfboards (S) and wetsuits (W). Labour hours per unit:

SurfboardsWetsuits
Avalon12 h4 h
Brindle9 h6 h

Avalon has 240 h. Brindle has 180 h.

QT1 · "Which country has an absolute / comparative advantage in …?" (Neither / A / B / Both)

Absolute: surfboards → Brindle (9 < 12). Wetsuits → Avalon (4 < 6).

OC: surfboard: Avalon 12/4 = 3 W, Brindle 9/6 = 1.5 W. Wetsuit: Avalon 4/12 = ⅓ S, Brindle 6/9 = ⅔ S.

Comparative: surfboards → Brindle (1.5 < 3). Wetsuits → Avalon (⅓ < ⅔).

"Both" is never right for comparative advantage. "Neither" is only right if the OCs are equal. For absolute advantage, "Both" is never right either (only one country can use fewer hours for the same good). "Neither" fits only if the hours are equal.

QT2 · OC dropdowns: "Avalon's opportunity cost of producing 1 surfboard is [__] wetsuits"

3 (Avalon S), 1.5 (Brindle S), 1/3 (Avalon W), 2/3 (Brindle W). The distractors are usually the inverses (e.g. 1/3 offered for the surfboard). Check the units: "wetsuits per surfboard" means the surfboard hours go on top.

QT3 · "Which two terms of trade are mutually beneficial?"

Range: 1.5 ≤ price of a surfboard ≤ 3 wetsuits.

QT4 · "If the ToT is 4 wetsuits for 1 surfboard, which two statements are true?"

The price of S is 4. Avalon's OC is 3 < 4, so Avalon sells surfboards. Brindle's OC is 1.5 < 4, so Brindle sells surfboards. True: "Avalon will sell surfboards" and "Brindle will sell surfboards". There are no buyers, so no trade happens. That's exactly why 4 is outside the range.

QT5 · "Without trade, which bundle is achievable by Avalon?" then "With trade at 2 wetsuits per surfboard, which is achievable with trade but not without?"

Hours test 12S + 4W ≤ 240. Avalon specialises in wetsuits (60), so its CPC is W = 60 − 2S.

QT6 · "At 2 wetsuits per surfboard, Avalon will produce [__] while Brindle produces [__]. Avalon can consume 8 surfboards and [__] wetsuits by exporting [__] in exchange for [__]."

Avalon produces 240/4 = 60 wetsuits. Brindle produces 180/9 = 20 surfboards. To get 8 S, Avalon exports 8 × 2 = 16 wetsuits in exchange for 8 surfboards, and consumes 60 − 16 = 44 wetsuits. Brindle consumes 20 − 8 = 12 S and 16 W (on its CPC W = 40 − 2S ✔).

Gain check: Avalon's (8 S, 44 W) would need 96 + 176 = 272 h > 240. Brindle's (12 S, 16 W) would need 108 + 96 = 204 h > 180. Both gain.

QT8 · Tariff diagram (lettered, maybe to scale): quantities, then areas

Use the diagram in 2.13 (letters A–G, numbers on the axes).

  1. "Before the tariff the country was [importing] [200] units. After the tariff it will [import] [80] units." Read at 6, then at 9. Don't use 60/300 (those are at PWt = 5).
  2. "CS after the tariff is": A. "Compared with no tariff, consumers": lose B+C+D+E.
  3. "PS after the tariff": B+F. "Government revenue": D+G (= 4 × 80 = 320).
  4. "Total surplus with the tariff": A + B + F + D + G. "Without the tariff": A + B + C + D + E + F.
  5. "The country as a whole": gains from the tariff iff G > C + E. Here G = 80 < C + E = 180, so it loses 100. Equivalently: "If (C+E) is bigger than the terms-of-trade gain, the country is better off by removing the tariff."

The exam letters will differ (e.g. L13 used CS before A+B+C+D+E+F, revenue E+I, net change I − (D+F)). Don't memorise letters. Apply M7 to whatever picture you're given.

QT9 · Tariff incidence numbers

PW = $40, unit tariff t = $10, new world price PWt = $38. Domestic price = 38 + 10 = $48. Importer's share = 48 − 40 = $8. Exporter's share = 40 − 38 = $2. Passthrough = 8/10 = 80%.

QT10 · Concept MCQs / dropdowns

  • "When a country becomes an exporter, domestic CS ___ and PS ___" → decreases; increases.
  • "If the domestic price is greater than the world price, an open economy will…" → increase consumer surplus (the country imports).
  • "With a tariff, PS is ___ than in a closed economy and ___ than under free trade" → less; greater.
  • "With a tariff, CS + PS is ___ than in a closed economy and ___ than in a fully open economy" → greater; less.
  • "Tariff → AD [increases], SRAS [decreases], so output is [uncertain] and the price level is [higher]."
  • "A country with S < I must have…" → NFI < 0 and NX < 0 (a trade deficit).

5 · Traps & misconceptions

Input vs output table. Hours per unit: OCX = hX/hY, and smaller = absolute advantage. Units per hour: OCX = outY/outX, and bigger = absolute advantage. Read the table heading every time.
Absolute ≠ comparative. A country can have absolute advantage in both goods but never comparative advantage in both. CA uses the OC, not the raw hours.
ToT units. "2 ice cream for 5 ice pops" is 2.5 pops per ice cream, not 2/5. Write every option as "units of Y per 1 X" in the same direction as your OCs before comparing.
Out-of-range ToT ≠ "one country gains, the other loses". Outside the range nobody trades: both countries want to sell (or buy) the same good.
Bundle "achievable with trade but not without" must fail the hours test and pass the CPC test. A bundle on the PPC is achievable without trade, so it's not the answer to the second question.
Specialise in the CA good, not the absolute-advantage good. Brindle has the absolute advantage in surfboards and the CA there, but in the Ozland example Nordia is absolutely better at wine yet specialises in cheese.
Reading tariff quantities at the wrong price. Domestic agents face PWt + t. The new world price PWt is only what exporters receive. The past exam deliberately labelled Q at P1 (90, 320) as distractors.
Tariff revenue is t × new imports, not t × old imports. Its rectangle runs from PWt to PWt+t (not from PW).
The ToT gain G isn't a DWL, and it isn't paid by domestic consumers. It's the part of the revenue paid by foreign exporters (the fall in the world price × new imports).
"Tariffs always make the country worse off" is false in this course's model. The correct answer is "better off only if the ToT gain > DWL (C+E)". "No regardless" is the trap option. Exception: if the question says the world price doesn't change (small country), it always loses.
Producer-surplus area after a tariff includes the original PS. PSafter = old PS triangle + the band B. It isn't just B.
A bilateral deficit isn't "being ripped off", and tariffs don't fix overall deficits. The cause is S < I (low saving).

6 · Quick self-check

1. Hours per unit. X: apples 2, bananas 5. Y: apples 3, bananas 6. Who has the absolute and comparative advantage in each good?

Absolute: X in both (2 < 3, 5 < 6). OC apple: X = 2/5 = 0.4 bananas, Y = 3/6 = 0.5, so X has the CA in apples. OC banana: X = 2.5 apples, Y = 2, so Y has the CA in bananas.

2. Same countries. Is "9 apples for 4 bananas" a mutually beneficial ToT? What about "5 apples for 3 bananas"?

Range for bananas: 2 ≤ apples per banana ≤ 2.5. 9/4 = 2.25 → yes. 5/3 ≈ 1.67 → no (below 2, so neither country sells bananas).

3. Output table: in 1 hour Ann bakes 6 pies or 3 cakes, and Ben bakes 4 pies or 1 cake. Who has the CA in cakes?

OC of a cake = pies output/cakes output: Ann 6/3 = 2 pies, Ben 4/1 = 4 pies. Ann has the CA in cakes, Ben in pies (OC of a pie: Ann ½ cake, Ben ¼ cake). Ann also has the absolute advantage in both.

4. A country has 300 labour hours. A widget takes 5 h and a gadget takes 10 h. Write the PPC (widgets on the x-axis). Is (40 widgets, 12 gadgets) achievable alone?

Gadgets = 30 − 0.5 × widgets. Hours: 40×5 + 12×10 = 320 > 300 → not achievable without trade.

5. In 2.13, what would ΔTS be if the world price had NOT fallen (small country, domestic price 10 with t = 4)?

At P = 10: Qd = 200 and Qs = 160. C = ½×(160−80)×4 = 160, E = ½×(280−200)×4 = 160, G = 0. ΔTS = −320. (Check: CS 1000, PS 640, revenue 4×40 = 160, total 1800 = 2120 − 320 ✔.)

6. PW = $20, t = $5, and the world price falls to $19. Domestic price? Passthrough?

Domestic price = 19 + 5 = $24. Importer's share 4, exporter's share 1. Passthrough 80%.

7. Opening to trade at a world price above the autarky price: what happens to CS, PS and TS?

The country exports. CS falls, PS rises by more, TS rises (the gain is the triangle between the curves above PH).

8. Spvt = 250, Spub = −50, I = 260. Find NX and NFI.

S = 200. NFI = S − I = −60, and NX = NFI = −60 (a trade deficit of 60, financed by net inbound foreign investment of 60).

9. Why does a country with low tariff passthrough have a better chance of gaining from a tariff?

Low passthrough means the world price falls a lot (big G) and the domestic price rises little (small C and E). It needs market power in the world market: price-insensitive excess demand and/or a big share of world import demand.

10. Why are "reviving manufacturing" and "raising tariff revenue" conflicting goals?

Protecting producers needs a big fall in imports, but revenue = t × imports. The more the tariff protects, the smaller the tax base.

OC of X = (units of Y per hour)/(units of X per hour).
Quick fix: convert to hours per unit = 1/output, then use the input formula.
PPC (labour L)
hXQX + hYQY = L
QY = L/hY − (hX/hY)QX
Intercepts L/hX (x-axis), L/hY (y-axis). Slope = −OC of x-axis good.
Above PPC = unattainable alone. On = efficient. Below = attainable.
Steeper PPC (X on x-axis) = higher OC of X. PPC entirely outside = absolute adv. in both.
Bowed-out PPC → increasing OC → no complete specialisation.
Terms of trade & CPC
ToT written as x units of Y (y-axis) per 1 X (x-axis).
Range: OCX(low) ≤ x ≤ OCX(high) (lecture: ½ ≤ x ≤ 1).
Convert "a X for b Y" → x = b/a.
CPC: line through the specialised production point with slope −x.
Made Q̄Y of Y: QY = Q̄Y − x·QX. Made Q̄X of X: QY = x(Q̄X − QX).
No trade: CPC = PPC. With trade: CPC on/outside PPC.
Lecture: Aus 24 dump, x = ⅔ → QD = 24 − ⅔QC, max 36 choc. Aus (18C, 12D) costs 300 h > 240. Belg (42C, 12D) costs 264 h > 240.
Gains from trade come from differences in OC. Same OC → no gain. Specialise in the CA good and trade for the rest. Trade is voluntary, so no country accepts a ToT that makes it worse off.
Trade openness = (X + M)/GDP. Trade volume = X + M.
Balance: bilateral XAB − MAB. Overall NX = X − M (+ surplus, − deficit).
Spvt = Y−C−T, Spub = T−G, S = Y−C−G
S = I + NFI and S = I + NX ⇒ NX = NFI = S − I

Tariff incidence
Importer share = PWt + t − PW. Exporter share = PW − PWt. Sum = t.
Passthrough = (PWt+t−PW)/t = 1 − (PW−PWt)/t.
MD shifts down by t. The world price falls and trade volume falls.
Who pays more depends on relative elasticities (import demand vs export supply). Exporter has many other customers / importer a small part of world demand → importer pays most (high passthrough; the 2018 and 2025 Trump tariffs).
Gain is possible only with world-market power: price-insensitive excess demand and/or a large share of world import demand (low passthrough).
Why tariffs disappoint (L9)
  • Manufacturing vs revenue conflict: revenue = t × imports, so it shrinks as protection works
  • Producers ≠ workers (automation)
  • Producers buy imported intermediate goods → costs ↑
  • Behavioural: re-routing (Heard & McDonald Is.), reclassification (Chicken Tax, Subaru BRAT)
  • Retaliation
  • Don't fix the trade deficit (the cause is S < I)
Non-tariff barriers: import quotas, local content requirements, export credit subsidies, red tape. "More precise" than tariffs, but still a welfare loss. (No quota diagram in the lectures. Textbook: same P and DWL as the equivalent tariff, but the rectangle goes to licence holders.)
Macro effect of tariffs (Tut 5)
M ↓ → NX ↑ → AD ↑. Input costs ↑ → SRAS ↓. ⇒ Price level ↑ (definite), real GDP ? (uncertain).
2025: actual rate ~14% vs statutory ~28% (lags, exemptions, USMCA, stockpiling) → smaller effect.
Benefits of trade
Exporter: AD ↑, productivity ↑ (economies of scale, learning-by-exporting).
Importer: lower prices, more variety, more competition, access to technology.
Losers exist (import-competing producers). Winners could compensate them.
Barriers: transport costs, risk diversification, income distribution, national security, politics.

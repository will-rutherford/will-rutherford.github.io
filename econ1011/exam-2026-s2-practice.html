Q1 · Rent control on Robinson Island 20 marks · T2 + T1

The rental market condition on Robinson Island is given by the diagram below. The diagram is drawn to scale, as indicated by the grid.

Read-offs (grid = 100 per square): S runs from (100, $100) to (900, $900), so QS = P. D₀ runs from (100, $900) to (580, $100). D₁ runs from (420, $900) to (900, $100) and is parallel to D₀, 320 units to the right. D₀ ∩ S = (400, $400). D₁ ∩ S = (600, $600).

1(a)12 marks First suppose the demand for rental units is at D₀. If the government imposes a rent ceiling at $500, the rent control will be ineffective / effective as it is equal to / below / above the competitive equilibrium rental rate.
Now suppose the demand shifts to D₁. In the absence of rent control, the competitive equilibrium rental rate would be $400 / $500 / $600 / indeterminable and the quantity transacted would be 400 / 500 / 600 / 700. However, with the rent control at $500, the quantity transacted is (about) 350 / 400 / 500 / (about) 650 while the black market rent will be $400 / $500 / $600 / (about) $750.

Answer & working — 1(a)

ineffective · above · $600 · 600 · 500 · (about) $750 ✅ official

  1. Effective? The D₀ equilibrium is (400, $400). The ceiling of $500 is a maximum set above P* = $400. The market price is already under the cap, so the cap does nothing: it is ineffective (non-binding). (T2 · ceiling binding test: a ceiling bites only if it is set BELOW P*.)
  2. D₁, no control: D₁ ∩ S at $600, Q = 600. Check: on D₁, Q = 420 + 0.6(900 − P), so at P = 600, Q = 420 + 180 = 600 = QS. ✓
  3. Ceiling $500 under D₁: now $500 < P* = $600, so it binds. QS(500) = 500 and QD(500) = 420 + 0.6×400 = 660. Quantity traded = the short side = QS = 500.
  4. Black-market rent: only 500 units exist, so go up from Q = 500 to D₁ and read across. 500 = 420 + 0.6(900 − P) gives 900 − P = 133.3, so P ≈ $767, "about $750". On the grid it sits just above the $700 line, about ⅔ of the way to $800.
"(about) 650" is QD at the ceiling (actually 660), not the quantity traded. Nobody can be made to supply more than QS. "$600" for the black-market rent is the old equilibrium. The black-market price is read off the demand curve at QS, which is above P*.
For the first blank the wording is a ceiling above P*, so it is ineffective. Students who think "the ceiling is $500 and it's a ceiling, so it must bind" lose 4 marks here.

1(b)8 marks (4 × 2) Now suppose the government of Robinson Island removes the rent control, so that the rental market equilibrium is determined by demand and supply.

(i) One day, a shipwreck near Robinson Island brought a large number of sailors needing rental housing. How will this impact the rental market on Robinson Island?

  • Demand for rental housing will increase.
  • Demand for rental housing will decrease.
  • Supply for rental housing will increase.
  • Supply for rental housing will decrease.

(ii) The shipwreck also brought ashore a lot of materials for building houses. Ignoring the increase in sailors above for the moment, how will the increase in building materials impact the rental market on Robinson Island? (You can assume that some of the rental units are newly built.)

  • Demand for rental housing will increase.
  • Demand for rental housing will decrease.
  • Supply for rental housing will increase.
  • Supply for rental housing will decrease.

(iii) How will the two effects (that is, an increase in the number of people needing rental housing and an increase in building materials) combined affect the equilibrium rent on Robinson Island?

  • The equilibrium rent will increase.
  • The equilibrium rent will decrease.
  • The equilibrium rent will remain unchanged.
  • The effect on the equilibrium rent is uncertain.

(iv) How will the two effects combined affect the quantity of rental units transacted on Robinson Island?

  • The quantity transacted will increase.
  • The quantity transacted will decrease.
  • The quantity transacted will remain unchanged.
  • The effect on the quantity transacted is uncertain.
Answer & working — 1(b)

(i) Demand increases · (ii) Supply increases · (iii) Rent: uncertain · (iv) Quantity: increases ✅ official

  1. More sailors means more buyers (renters), a change in the number of buyers, so D shifts right.
  2. Cheaper or more plentiful building materials lower an input cost for producers (landlords and builders), so S shifts right. The hint "some units are newly built" tells you this works through the supply side.
  3. D↑ pushes P up and S↑ pushes P down. The sizes are not given, so the effect on P is ambiguous.
  4. D↑ raises Q and S↑ raises Q, so Q rises for certain.

Method: T1 · combined-shift table. When both curves shift the same way (both right), Q is certain and P is ambiguous. When they shift opposite ways, P is certain and Q is ambiguous.

"The equilibrium rent will remain unchanged" is the tempting wrong answer for (iii). It is only true if the shifts happen to be exactly equal, which you are not told. With no magnitudes given, "uncertain" is the answer for the variable the two shifts pull in opposite directions.

Q2 · Foursome Inc. (monopoly) 8 marks · T3

Foursome Inc. is the monopolist of four-toe socks. Its product does not sell very well, but some people find it a perfect gift for their in-laws, giving Foursome Inc. a stable customer base. The demand, marginal revenue and marginal cost curves of Foursome Inc. are given below.

Values on the original diagram: D runs from (0, 20) to (20, 0). MR runs from (0, 20) to (10, 0). MC is upward-sloping through (8, 4) and (12, 8). The dashed guides are at Q = 8, 12, 16 and P = 4, 8, 12. Equations (for checking only): P = 20 − Q, MR = 20 − 2Q, MC = Q − 4.

2(a)4 marks As a monopolist, Foursome Inc. produces the quantity of   and charges the price of  .

Answer & working — 2(a)

Q = 8, P = 12 ✅ official

  1. Find where MR = MC: the MR and MC lines cross at (8, 4). Check: 20 − 2(8) = 4 = 8 − 4. ✓
  2. Go up to the demand curve at Q = 8: P = 20 − 8 = 12.

Method: T3 · monopoly Q & P.

Three distractor points are drawn on purpose. (12, 8) is where D = MC, the competitive or efficient outcome. P = 4 is the height of MR = MC, which is the marginal cost, not the price. (16, 4) is on D at the MC height. The monopoly price is always read off D, above the MR = MC point.

2(b)4 marks Calculate the price elasticity of demand at the monopoly price and quantity. (written answer box)

Answer & working — 2(b)

εD = −3/2 (= −1.5) ✅ official

Official model answer, which you can copy as a layout:

Price elasticity of demand at (P = 12, Q = 8)
= slope of ray from the origin ÷ slope of the demand curve
= (12/8) ÷ (−20/20)
= −3/2
  • Ray from the origin to the point: P/Q = 12/8 = 1.5.
  • Slope of demand = rise/run = −20/20 = −1 (use the intercepts).
  • ε = 1.5 ÷ (−1) = −1.5. |ε| > 1, so demand is elastic, as it must be at a monopoly point: a monopolist always produces where MR > 0, which is on the elastic part of D.

Method: T3 · point elasticity at the monopoly point (same formula as T1).

Use the point formula, not the QCAA midpoint formula, and keep the minus sign (the official answer is −3/2). Don't flip it into Q/P × slope: the slope here is ΔP/ΔQ, so you divide by it. If you get a value between −1 and 0 at a monopoly point, you have inverted something.

Q3 · Macroland AD-AS 12 marks · T4

3(a)2 marks Macroland is an economy whose Aggregate Demand, Short-Run Aggregate Supply and Long-Run Aggregate Supply are given in the diagram below. Suppose Macroland is currently at a short-run equilibrium but not in a long-run equilibrium. Click on the point that represents Macroland's output and price level in the diagram.

The original diagram has no numbers. AD and SRAS cross to the left of the vertical LRAS. Each of AD and SRAS also cuts LRAS at a different height, so there are three candidate intersections. Point X is marked here for reference.

Answer & working — 3(a)

Click AD ∩ SRAS (point X, left of LRAS) ✅ official

The short-run equilibrium is where AD = SRAS. Here it is not on LRAS, so it is a short-run equilibrium only: output is below potential, a recessionary gap. The other two crossings (AD ∩ LRAS and SRAS ∩ LRAS) are not equilibria at all, because they are not where AD = SRAS.

Don't click AD ∩ LRAS. That is where the economy would end up in the long run after SRAS adjusts, but the question says "currently at a short-run equilibrium".

Method: T4 · reading AD-AS diagrams.

3(b)4 marks Suppose Macroland's economy is at the point you indicated in part (a). Which two of the following policies can the government of Macroland use if it wants to move the economy's output to its potential GDP level?

  • Reduce government expenditure in Macroland.
  • Reduce the tax rate in Macroland.
  • Reduce money supply in Macroland.
  • Reduce the interest rate in Macroland.
Answer & working — 3(b)

Reduce the tax rate · Reduce the interest rate ✅ official

Y < YP (recessionary gap), so we need AD to shift right, which means expansionary policy.

  • Tax rate ↓ → disposable income ↑ → C ↑ → AD ↑. ✔ (expansionary fiscal)
  • Interest rate ↓ → borrowing cheaper → C ↑, I ↑ → AD ↑. ✔ (expansionary monetary)
  • G ↓ and money supply ↓ are contractionary, so they would widen the gap. ✘

Method: T4 · gap → policy table.

The wording is "reduce" in all four options, so you can't just look for the word "increase". Decide whether each reduction expands or contracts AD. Lowering taxes or interest rates is expansionary. Lowering G or the money supply is contractionary.

3(c)6 marks Wishing to host an AFL team in Macroland, Macroland's government decides to build a new stadium in its capital city. The increase in government purchases due to the new stadium construction will reduce the AD / reduce the SRAS / increase the AD / increase the SRAS, leading to the same / a lower / an uncertain / a higher output level and a lower / the same / an uncertain / a higher price level.

Answer & working — 3(c)

increase the AD · a higher output · a higher price level ✅ official

G is a component of AD = C + I + G + NX. G↑ shifts AD right along an upward-sloping SRAS, so Y↑ and P↑ in the short run.

A stadium is "capital", which tempts "increase the SRAS". The question asks about the government purchases (spending), which is demand-side. Any long-run capacity effect is not what's asked. In this course, a change in G is always an AD shift.

Method: T4 · shock → curve → P, Y.

Q4 · Arendelle & Northuldra (trade) 20 marks · T5

Arendelle and Northuldra produce two goods: ice pops and ice cream. The number of labour hours required to produce one unit of each good in each country is given by the following table.

Ice popsIce cream
Arendelle2 labour hours8 labour hours
Northuldra3 labour hours6 labour hours

Arendelle has 240 labour hours while Northuldra has 180 labour hours.

4(a)8 marks (4 × 2) For each question, choose one of: Neither / Arendelle / Northuldra / Both.
(i) Which country has an absolute advantage in the production of ice pops?
(ii) Which country has an absolute advantage in the production of ice cream?
(iii) Which country has a comparative advantage in the production of ice pops?
(iv) Which country has a comparative advantage in the production of ice cream?

Answer & working — 4(a)

(i) Arendelle · (ii) Northuldra · (iii) Arendelle · (iv) Northuldra ✅ official

Absolute advantage: fewer hours per unit. For ice pops, A needs 2 h and N needs 3 h, so Arendelle. For ice cream, A needs 8 h and N needs 6 h, so Northuldra. Total hours (240 vs 180) are irrelevant for absolute advantage, which is about productivity per unit.

Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost. With an input table (hours per unit), the OC of 1 unit of X = hoursX ÷ hoursY.

OC of 1 ice popOC of 1 ice cream
Arendelle2/8 = ¼ ice cream ✔ lower8/2 = 4 ice pops
Northuldra3/6 = ½ ice cream6/3 = 2 ice pops ✔ lower

Method: T5 · input table → OC → comparative advantage.

With an input table (hours per unit), smaller is better, and OC of X = own hours for X ÷ own hours for Y. With an output table (units per hour or per day) it flips. Check which kind of table you've been given before dividing. "Both" is never right for comparative advantage in a 2-good, 2-country setting unless the OCs are equal, in which case the answer is "Neither".

4(b)4 marks Only two of the following terms of trade between Arendelle and Northuldra are mutually beneficial. Which two are they? (Select two.)

  • 2 units of ice cream for 3 units of ice pops
  • 2 units of ice cream for 5 units of ice pops
  • 2 units of ice cream for 7 units of ice pops
  • 2 units of ice cream for 9 units of ice pops
Answer & working — 4(b)

2 ice cream for 5 ice pops · 2 ice cream for 7 ice pops ✅ official

The price of 1 ice cream must lie between the two countries' OCs: N's OC of 2 ice pops and A's OC of 4 ice pops, so 2 < price < 4 pops per ice cream. Convert each option to "per 1 ice cream":

OptionPops per 1 ice creamIn (2, 4)?
2 : 31.5✘ N would rather make its own pops

2 : 73.5✔
2 : 94.5✘ A would rather make its own ice cream

Method: T5 · terms-of-trade window.

The options are written as "2 units for …", so divide by 2 before comparing with the per-unit OCs. Endpoints (exactly 2 or 4) give one side zero gain, so they count as "not mutually beneficial".

4(c)4 marks (2 × 2) (i) If there is no trade, which of the following bundles is achievable by Arendelle? (ii) If the two countries trade at 1 unit of ice cream for 3 units of ice pops, which of the following consumption bundles of Arendelle is achievable with trade but was not achievable without trade? (Same options for both.)

  • 36 units of ice pops and 30 units of ice cream
  • 48 units of ice pops and 25 units of ice cream
  • 60 units of ice pops and 18 units of ice cream
  • 72 units of ice pops and 10 units of ice cream
Answer & working — 4(c)

(i) 72 ice pops + 10 ice cream · (ii) 60 ice pops + 18 ice cream ✅ official

Without trade, A's PPF is 2·(pops) + 8·(ice cream) ≤ 240 hours (intercepts: 120 pops or 30 ice cream).

With trade at 1 IC = 3 pops, A specialises in pops (its comparative advantage): 120 pops. It can then buy ice cream at 3 pops each, so its consumption possibilities are pops + 3·IC ≤ 120.

Method: T5 · PPF / consumption-possibility check.

For (ii), (72, 10) is also achievable with trade, but the question asks for a bundle that was not achievable without trade, which rules it out. Read every condition in the stem.

4(d)4 marks (2 × 2) As Arendelle developed, it started trading with the rest of the world, importing ice cream at the world price of P₀. One day, however, King Runeard decided to impose a unit tariff of t on ice-cream imports. This sends the world price of ice cream to P₁. The demand and supply of ice cream in Arendelle is given by the following diagram. (Note: if a letter sits across a dashed line, it refers to the area on both sides of the dashed line.)

Schematic redraw with the same layout and letters as the original (no numbers on the original). From top to bottom: autarky price (dashed), P₁ + t (solid), P₀ (dashed), P₁ (solid). The two vertical dashed lines are QS and QD at P₁ + t. D, E and H straddle the P₀ line, so each spans the band from P₁ to P₁ + t. F is the import rectangle between P₀ and P₁ + t, and G is the import rectangle between P₁ and P₀. I lies below P₁ and above S.

(i) What is the producers' surplus in Arendelle after the tariff?

  • Area (A + B + C)
  • Area (D + E + F + G)
  • Area (D + I)
  • Area I

(ii) Can Arendelle as a whole be better off with the tariff?

  • Yes if Area G is bigger than the sum of Area (E + H).
  • Yes if Area (E + H) is bigger than Area (F + G).
  • No if Area (A + B + C) is bigger than Area (D + I).
  • No regardless of any area comparison.
Answer & working — 4(d)

(i) Area (D + I) · (ii) Yes if Area G is bigger than the sum of Area (E + H) ✅ official

  1. PS after the tariff: domestic sellers receive the domestic price P₁ + t. PS = area above S, below P₁ + t = I (below P₁) + D (the band from P₁ to P₁ + t, left of S) = D + I.
  2. Can the country gain? Tariff revenue = t × imports = the rectangle from P₁ up to P₁ + t between the two dashed verticals = F + G. Part of it (F, above P₀) comes from domestic consumers paying more. Part (G, below P₀) is paid by foreign exporters, because the world price fell from P₀ to P₁. That is the terms-of-trade gain. The losses are the two deadweight triangles E and H (over-production and under-consumption). So the country gains on net if G > E + H.

Method: T5 · tariff welfare (large-country, world price falls). The pack's T5 uses PW, PWt, PWt+t for the same three lines.

"No regardless" is the small-country (QCAA) answer. In this course the tariff pushes the world price down (P₀ → P₁), so a gain is possible. "(E + H) > (F + G)" compares the DWL with the whole revenue, but F is just a transfer from home consumers, so it can't count as a national gain.
Strictly, the welfare change measured against the old world price P₀ is G minus the parts of E and H that lie above P₀. The official answer frames it as G versus the full triangles E + H, and among the four options it is clearly the intended one. In the exam, pick the option of the form "terms-of-trade rectangle vs the two DWL triangles".

Q5 · Trawl fishing (externality + game) 20 marks · T6

The diagram below depicts the marginal benefits (MB), private marginal cost (PMC) and social marginal cost (SMC) of trawl fishing.

Labelled points on the original: MB ∩ SMC at (1200, $15). MB ∩ PMC at (2000, $12). PMC at Q = 1200 is $10. SMC at Q = 2000 is $18. SMC lies above PMC, and the gap widens from $5 to $6.

5(a)10 marks The given diagram illustrates the positive / negative / potential / uncertain externality from trawl fishing. The competitive market output level is 1200 / 1600 / 2000 / indeterminable while the socially optimal output level is 1200 / 1600 / 2000 / indeterminable. If the government wants to correct the externality, it can enforce a price / impose a tax / provide a subsidy of the amount $15 − $10 = $5 / $15 − $12 = $3 / $18 − $10 = $8 / $18 − $12 = $6 per unit.

Answer & working — 5(a)

negative · 2000 · 1200 · impose a tax · $15 − $10 = $5 ✅ official

  1. SMC > PMC: fishing imposes an external cost on others (depleted stocks, sea-floor damage), so it is a negative externality.
  2. Market output: firms ignore the external cost, so MB = PMC gives 2000.
  3. Social optimum: MB = SMC gives 1200. The market over-produces.
  4. Pigouvian tax = the marginal external cost at the social optimum = SMC − PMC at Q = 1200 = $15 − $10 = $5. With a $5 tax, PMC + 5 passes through (1200, $15), so the market now chooses 1200.

Method: T6 · externality diagram → Pigouvian tax.

"$18 − $12 = $6" is the external cost at the market quantity (2000). Because SMC and PMC aren't parallel here, a $6 tax would overshoot (PMC + 6 meets MB below 1200). "$15 − $12 = $3" is the price change, not the wedge. "$18 − $10" mixes points from two different quantities. The correct tax is the vertical gap between SMC and PMC at the optimal Q.

5(b)4 marks Country A and Country B are trying to reach an international agreement to restrict trawl fishing. Each of them can choose, independently, to Restrict the practice or to maintain the Status quo. The payoff matrix is as follows (Country A's payoff first):

Country B
RestrictStatus quo
Country ARestrict2, 24, 0
Status quo1, 03, 2

Does Country A have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer. (written)

Answer & working — 5(b)

Yes: Restrict is A's dominant strategy. ✅ official

Official model answer (copy this structure for full marks):

Yes. Country A has a dominant strategy. To see why:

If Country B Restricts: Restrict gives Country A a payoff of 2; Status quo gives Country A a payoff of 1. 2 is better than 1, so "Restrict" is Country A's best response against Country B's "Restrict".

If Country B maintains the Status quo: Restrict gives Country A a payoff of 4; Status quo gives Country A a payoff of 3. 4 is better than 3, so "Restrict" is Country A's best response against Country B's "Status quo".

As "Restrict" is the best response against each and every one of Country B's actions, it is Country A's dominant strategy.

Method: T6 · best-response stars. A is the row player, so compare the first numbers down each column.

Marks come from showing both cases ("if B does X… if B does Y…") with the actual payoffs, then the conclusion "best response to every action, therefore dominant". A bare "Yes, Restrict" will not get 4/4.

5(c)4 marks Refer to the same payoff matrix as in part (b). Does Country B have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer. (written)

Answer & working — 5(c)

No: Country B has no dominant strategy. ✅ official

No. Country B does not have a dominant strategy. To see why:

If Country A Restricts: Restrict gives Country B a payoff of 2; Status quo gives Country B a payoff of 0. 2 is better than 0, so "Restrict" is Country B's best response against Country A's "Restrict".

If Country A maintains the Status quo: Restrict gives Country B a payoff of 0; Status quo gives Country B a payoff of 2. 2 is better than 0, so "Status quo" is Country B's best response against Country A's "Status quo".

As there is no action that can be the best response against each and every one of Country A's actions, Country B does not have a dominant strategy.
B is the column player, so use the second number and compare across each row. A common slip is to read B's payoff from the first number. B's best response changes with A's choice (it wants to match A), so B has no dominant strategy.

5(d)2 marks The Nash equilibrium/equilibria of the game in part (b) is/are:

  • (Restrict, Restrict)
  • (Status quo, Status quo)
  • (Restrict, Restrict) and (Status quo, Status quo)
  • All four cells are equilibria.
Answer & working — 5(d)

(Restrict, Restrict) only ✅ official

A always plays Restrict (dominant). B's best response to Restrict is Restrict (2 > 0). So (Restrict, Restrict) is the only cell with both payoffs starred.

B: RestrictB: Status quo
A: Restrict2*, 2* ← NE4*, 0
A: Status quo1, 03, 2*
(Status quo, Status quo) looks like an equilibrium because B is happy there (2*). But A would deviate to Restrict (4 > 3). Both players must be best-responding for a cell to be a NE. Use the dominant strategy from (b) to shortcut: eliminate A's Status quo row, then let B best-respond.

Q6 · Solow growth model 20 marks · T7

Consider an economy in which: the per capita output level depends only on the capital-labour ratio; the saving rate is s; the population growth rate is n; and the depreciation rate is δ. This question concerns the long-run growth of this economy.

6(a)2 marks Click on the intersection that determines the steady state of this economy.

Schematic redraw of the original layout. y = f(k) (output), s·f(k) (saving) and the straight line (n+δ)k (maintenance). The dashed tangent to f(k) is parallel to the maintenance line, and dashed verticals drop from the two key points. Letters X, Y, Z are added here for reference (the original is click-on-image).

Answer & working — 6(a)

Click where s·f(k) crosses (n+δ)k (point X) ✅ official

Steady state: investment (saving) = maintenance investment, s·f(k) = (n+δ)k, so Δk = 0. That is the crossing of the saving curve with the straight line.

Two tempting wrong clicks are (1) where the maintenance line meets f(k), and (2) the tangency point Z on f(k), which marks the Golden Rule k, not the steady state. Only the s·f(k) curve crossing the line counts.

Method: T7 · Solow diagram reading.

6(b)10 marks Refer to the diagram below.

Original diagram: s·f(k) crosses the maintenance line at k*, with y* = f(k*) and sf(k*) marked on the y-axis. A larger k₀ is marked to the right of k*, where the maintenance line is above s·f(k).

If the economy starts at the capital-labour ratio k₀, its initial capital-labour ratio is lower than / the same as / higher than the steady-state capital-labour ratio. As the amount of investment required to maintain the current capital-labour ratio is indeterminable with respect to / higher than / lower than / the same as the amount of investment, the capital-labour ratio will be uncertain / rise / fall / remain unchanged. The economy will continue to adjust until it reaches the capital-labour ratio k* / at the Golden rule / k₀ / at the origin. Along this adjustment, the per capita output level will increase towards y* / increase towards sf(k*) / fall towards sf(k*) / fall towards y*.

Answer & working — 6(b)

higher than · higher than · fall · k* · fall towards y* ✅ official

  1. k₀ is to the right of k*, so it is higher than the steady state.
  2. At k₀ the maintenance line (n+δ)k lies above s·f(k). The investment needed to maintain k is higher than actual investment.
  3. Δk = s·f(k) − (n+δ)k < 0, so k falls.
  4. It keeps falling until Δk = 0, which is at k*.
  5. As k falls, y = f(k) slides down the output curve to y* = f(k*), so it falls towards y*.
"fall towards sf(k*)" is the trap. sf(k*) is saving/investment per worker at the steady state, not output. Output per worker is read off the f(k) curve, so the target is y*. Also note the order of comparison in blank 2: "the amount required to maintain is ___ the amount of investment". Required is higher, so the answer is "higher than", not "lower than".

Method: T7 · transition dynamics.

6(c)4 marks (2 × 2) Suppose the economy is in the situation depicted in the diagram from 6(a) (the steady state is at k₁, and the dashed tangent to f(k), parallel to (n+δ)k, touches f at k₂).

(i) The steady-state capital-labour ratio is:

  • higher than the Golden Rule capital-labour ratio.
  • lower than the Golden Rule capital-labour ratio.
  • the same as the Golden Rule capital-labour ratio.
  • indeterminable relative to the Golden Rule capital-labour ratio.

(ii) Relative to the Golden Rule saving rate, this economy is saving:

  • too much.
  • too little.
  • optimally.
  • The saving rate and the Golden Rule saving rate are incomparable.
Answer & working — 6(c)

(i) lower than the Golden Rule k · (ii) too little ✅ official

  • Golden Rule k: the k that maximises steady-state consumption c = f(k) − (n+δ)k, which is where the slope of f(k) equals the slope of the maintenance line (n+δ). On the diagram that is where the dashed tangent touches f(k): k₂ = kGR.
  • The steady state k₁ = k* is to the left of k₂, so k* < kGR.
  • To reach a higher k* you would need s·f(k) to cross further right, which means a higher s. So the economy is saving too little.

Method: T7 · Golden Rule.

k* < kGR means saving too little, and k* > kGR means saving too much (dynamic inefficiency). Don't say "too much" because the economy "has lots of saving".

6(d)4 marks Suppose you are the government in part (c). Will encouraging your people to have more babies move the steady-state capital-labour ratio closer to the Golden Rule capital-labour ratio? Why or why not? (written)

Answer & working — 6(d)

No. ✅ official

Official model answer: No. Increasing the population growth rate will reduce the steady-state capital-labour ratio. As the current steady-state capital-labour ratio is already below the Golden rule level, the policy will move the steady-state capital-labour ratio further away from the Golden rule level.

Mechanism (add it for safety): more babies means n↑, so the maintenance line (n+δ)k becomes steeper. It now cuts s·f(k) further left, so k* ↓. (The Golden Rule k also falls a little, because the tangency slope n+δ is now steeper. The lecturer's answer ignores this, and the conclusion is that the gap does not close. Keep to the model answer: k* falls, and it is already below kGR.)

Method: T7 · s / n / δ shifts table.

The answer structure is Yes/No → what happens to k* → compare with kGR. If the question had said the economy was above the Golden Rule, the same policy would move it closer. Always link back to the diagram in the stem.

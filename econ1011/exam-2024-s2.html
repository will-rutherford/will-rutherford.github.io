Part 1 · Capital-city rental market (20 marks)

This question concerns the rental market of a major capital city in Australia. The city's rental market conditions are given by the diagram below.

Values marked on the paper: the downward line passes through (1000, $900), (2000, $600) and (3000, $300). The upward line passes through (1000, $300), (2000, $600) and (3000, $900).

Q1 · Equilibrium2 marks

According to the diagram, what are the equilibrium price and quantity transacted of rental units in the city?

  • ○ Equilibrium price is $900, quantity transacted is 1000.
  • ○ Equilibrium price is $600, quantity transacted is 2000.
  • ○ Equilibrium price is $300, quantity transacted is 1000.
  • ○ Equilibrium price is $300, quantity transacted is 3000.
Answer & working

Answer: $600, 2000. 🟢 confident

The two lines cross at (2000, $600). The swapped labels don't matter, because there is only one crossing.

Q2 · Fewer student visas8 marks

The government of Australia proposes to lower the number of student visas issued each year. This is expected to significantly reduce the number of international students studying and residing in the city. You can assume that the vast majority of international students stay in rental housing. (2 marks each)

"The government proposal to reduce the number of student visas will [increase the demand for / reduce the demand for / increase the supply of / reduce the supply of] rental housing, shifting the [demand curve to the left / demand curve to the right / supply curve to the left / supply curve to the right]. The equilibrium price of rental housing will [increase / decrease / remain unchanged / increase or decrease depending on the magnitude of the shock] while the equilibrium quantity of rental housing will [increase / decrease / remain unchanged / increase or decrease depending on the magnitude of the shock]."

Answer & working

Answers: reduce the demand for · demand curve to the left · decrease · decrease. 🟢 confident

Fewer students means fewer renters, so the number of buyers falls and demand shifts left. With supply unchanged, P↓ and Q↓. T1 (determinants of demand: number of buyers).

Q3 · Price floor vs price ceiling at $3008 marks

Now suppose the proposal is abandoned and the market is still given by the diagram. Each correct answer is worth 2 marks.

"If the government imposes a price floor of $300, the market price will [increase to $900 / fall to $300 / remain at $600], and the market quantity will [decrease to 1000 / remain at 2000 / increase to 3000]."

"If the government imposes a price ceiling of $300, the market price will [increase to $900 / remain at $600 / decrease to $300], and the market quantity will [decrease to 1000 / increase to 3000 / remain at 2000]."

Answer & working

Answers: floor → remain at $600, remain at 2000. Ceiling → decrease to $300, decrease to 1000. 🟢 confident

  1. Floor at $300 < $600: a floor only binds if it's set above equilibrium, so this one is non-binding and nothing changes (T2 M2).
  2. Ceiling at $300 < $600: binding, so the price falls to $300. At $300, QS = 1000 (upward line) and QD = 3000 (downward line). Quantity transacted = the short side = 1000 (T2 M1).
The swapped labels don't matter. Even if you took the labels literally, the quantity traded is min(1000, 3000) = 1000.
Both policies are set at the same $300, but they have opposite effects. Ask which side of equilibrium each one is on before anything else. 3000 is quantity demanded, not traded.

Q4 · Most efficient fix for a rental crisis2 marks

Suppose the city is dealing with a rental crisis: residents complain that rents are too high and there are not enough rental housing units available. Which of the following is the most efficient solution (i.e. the effective policy that involves the least unintended consequences)?

  • ○ Imposing a binding price floor.
  • ○ Imposing a binding price ceiling.
  • ○ Increasing demand for rental units.
  • ○ Imposing a non-binding price ceiling.
  • ○ Increasing supply of rental units.
Answer & working

Answer: Increasing supply of rental units. 🟢 confident

Supply shifts right, so rent falls and more units are available, with no shortage or black market. That matches the L4 message: "the most obvious solutions" are about housing supply (zoning, planning). A binding ceiling (rent control) causes shortages, quality cuts and non-price competition. A floor raises rents. More demand raises rents. A non-binding ceiling does nothing.

Q10 · Where is E, and what happens with no policy?6 marks

Suppose the state of the economy is illustrated by the AD–AS diagram below. (The SRAS, LRAS and AD curves are deliberately left unlabelled.) 2 marks per correct answer.

(i) If the economy is currently at Point E:

  • ○ The economy is neither in long-run nor short-run equilibrium.
  • ○ The economy is in short-run equilibrium and the level of real GDP is above potential GDP.
  • ○ The economy is both in long-run equilibrium and short-run equilibrium.
  • ○ The economy is in short-run equilibrium and the level of real output is below potential GDP.

(ii) "If the economy is currently at Point E and the government takes no action, then the economy will gradually move back to long-run equilibrium as [low inflation rate / high inflation rate / high rate of unemployment / low rate of unemployment] causes the [SRAS curve to shift right / AD curve to shift left / AD curve to shift right / SRAS curve to shift left]."

Answer & working

Answers: (i) short-run equilibrium, real GDP above potential. (ii) low rate of unemployment → SRAS curve to shift left. 🟢 confident

  1. E = AD ∩ SRAS, so it's a short-run equilibrium. It's right of LRAS, so Y > Ȳ (the economy is overheating).
  2. L7 "Movement back to LR equilibrium": when Y > Ȳ, unemployment is very low. Firms compete for workers, so real wages rise and real profits fall. Firms supply less at each price level, so SRAS shifts left until Y = Ȳ, with P higher. T4 M5.
"High inflation rate" sounds right for an overheating economy, but the lecture's trigger is the labour market: low unemployment pushes wages up. Self-correction always moves SRAS, never AD.

Q11 · Fighting high inflation4 marks

Ignore the scenario above. Suppose the main economic issue facing the economy is a higher-than-optimal rate of inflation. (1 mark each)

"The central bank can lower price levels by [increasing interest rates / reducing government purchases / increasing government purchases / reducing interest rates], which will [reduce short-run aggregate supply / increase aggregate demand / increase short-run aggregate supply / reduce aggregate demand]."

"The government can lower price levels by [increasing government purchases / reducing government purchases / reducing interest rates / increasing interest rates], which will [increase short-run aggregate supply / increase aggregate demand / reduce short-run aggregate supply / reduce aggregate demand]."

Answer & working

Answers: central bank → increasing interest rates → reduce aggregate demand. Government → reducing government purchases → reduce aggregate demand. 🟢 confident

Contractionary policy shifts AD left, so P falls. Monetary: i↑, which cuts C and I. Fiscal: G↓. Match each tool to the body that controls it. T4 M4.

The production of Ding-Dongs results in emotional damage to people living near the factory. The diagram shows the Marginal Benefit (MB), Private Marginal Cost (PMC) and Social Marginal Cost (SMC) of Ding-Dong production. The diagram is drawn to scale.

Grid read (checked twice): MB runs from (0, $60) through (200, $40) and (400, $20). PMC runs from (0, $0) to (600, $30). SMC runs from (0, $30) to (600, $60).

Answer & working

Answer: MB ∩ PMC, at Q = 400, P = $20. 🟢 confident

Q13 · Click: social optimum1 mark

Answer & working

Answer: MB ∩ SMC, at Q = 200, P = $40. 🟢 confident

Q14 · Deadweight loss4 marks

Calculate the deadweight loss associated with the externality of Ding-Dong production. Show your working. (Hint: area of a triangle = width × height / 2.)

Answer & working

Answer: DWL = $3,000. 🟢 confident

  1. Base = Qmkt − Qeff = 400 − 200 = 200.
  2. Height = SMC(400) − MB(400) = $50 − $20 = $30.
  3. DWL = 200 × 30 / 2 = $3,000.

T6 M3.

Q15 · Corrective policy4 marks

If the government wants to correct the externality, it can impose a per-unit [tax / subsidy] of the amount [$10 / $20 / $30 / $40].

Answer & working

Answers: tax · $30. 🟢 confident

Negative externality, so use a tax. MEC = SMC − PMC = (30 + 0.05Q) − 0.05Q = $30 at every Q, because the curves are parallel. The tax of $30 shifts PMC up onto SMC, so the market produces 200. T6 M2.

$20 is the change in the market price ($40 − $20). $40 is the optimal price. Neither is the tax. The tax is the vertical gap between SMC and PMC.

Q16 · Does A have a dominant strategy?4 marks

Does Country A have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

Answer: No. 🟢 confident

If B Regulates, A gets 10 from Regulate vs 7 from Status quo, so A picks Regulate. If B keeps the Status quo, A gets 20 from Regulate vs 35 from Status quo, so A picks the Status quo. A's best choice depends on B, so A has no dominant strategy. T6 M6.

Q17 · Does B have a dominant strategy?2 marks

Refer to the same payoff matrix. Does Country B have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

Answer: Yes. Regulate is B's dominant strategy. 🟢 confident

If A Regulates, B gets 10 from Regulate vs 5 from Status quo. If A keeps the Status quo, B gets 40 from Regulate vs 30 from Status quo. Regulate is better for B whatever A does.

Q18 · Nash equilibria4 marks

The 2 × 2 game has [0 / 1 / 2 / 4] Nash equilibrium/equilibria (in pure strategy). It is/They are [none of the combinations of choices / (Regulate, Regulate) / (Status quo, Status quo) / (Regulate, Regulate) and (Status quo, Status quo) / all four combinations of choices].

Answer & working

Answers: 1 · (Regulate, Regulate). 🟢 confident

B always Regulates. A's best reply to Regulate is Regulate (10 > 7), so (Regulate, Regulate) is the NE. (Status quo, Status quo) = (35, 30) isn't an NE because B would switch to Regulate (40 > 30).

This is the S1 2024 game with the roles of A and B swapped: here B has the dominant strategy, not A. Always recompute; never pattern-match from a previous paper.

Part 4 · Solow growth model and poverty trap (20 marks)

Consider an economy in which per-capita output (y) depends only on the capital–labour ratio (k), the saving rate is s, the population growth rate is n and the depreciation rate is δ. This question concerns the long-run growth of this economy. The diagram shows the per-capita output, saving and break-even investment curves.

Q19 · Steady-state k2 marks

At the steady state of the economy, the capital–labour ratio will be:

  • ○ k₁
  • ○ k₂
  • ○ k₃
  • ○ Cannot be determined given available information.
Answer & working

Answer: k₂. 🟢 confident

The steady state is where s·f(k) = (δ+n)k, i.e. where the saving curve crosses the break-even line. That's k₂. T7 M1.

k₃ is where f(k) (output, not saving) meets the break-even line. There consumption = 0.

Q20 · Golden Rule k and saving policy4 marks

(i) The level of steady-state capital–labour ratio that maximises consumption is:

  • ○ k₁
  • ○ k₂
  • ○ k₃
  • ○ Cannot be determined with available information.

(ii) In order to maximise consumption in the long run, the government should:

  • ○ leave the saving rate unchanged.
  • ○ increase the population growth rate.
  • ○ increase the saving rate.
  • ○ decrease the saving rate.
Answer & working

Answers: (i) k₁; (ii) decrease the saving rate. 🟢 confident

The Golden Rule k is where the slope of f(k) = the slope of (δ+n)k. That's the grey tangent at k₁. The current k* = k₂ > k₁, so the economy saves too much. Lowering s shifts s·f(k) down and moves k* left toward k₁, which raises c*. T7 M3.

Q21 · Transition from k₃6 marks

Suppose the economy starts with capital–labour ratio k₃. At k₃, saving per capita is [lower than / higher than / the same as / incomparable to] the per-capita investment required to maintain the capital–labour ratio. Hence the capital–labour ratio will [increase / decrease / remain unchanged / be indeterminable]. This adjustment will stop when the capital–labour ratio reaches [zero / the steady-state capital–labour ratio / the Golden Rule capital–labour ratio / infinity].

Answer & working

Answers: lower than · decrease · the steady-state capital–labour ratio. 🟢 confident

At k₃ (right of k₂), s·f(k) lies below (δ+n)k, so Δk < 0 and k falls back to k₂. T7 M2.

At k₃ it's f(k), not s·f(k), that touches the break-even line. Saving is well below it, so don't answer "the same as".

Q22 · Fall in population growth4 marks

A fall in the population growth rate will cause [the break-even investment curve to pivot up / the saving curve to shift down / the saving curve to shift up / the break-even investment curve to pivot down], which will cause [steady-state capital–labour ratio to fall and the steady-state output per capita to increase / both steady-state capital–labour ratio and output per capita to increase / steady-state capital–labour ratio to increase and the steady-state output per capita to fall / both steady-state capital–labour ratio and output per capita to fall].

Answer & working

Answers: the break-even investment curve to pivot down · both steady-state capital–labour ratio and output per capita to increase. 🟢 confident

The slope of (δ+n)k is δ+n, so n↓ makes it flatter (it pivots down around the origin). It now meets s·f(k) further right, so k*↑ and therefore y* = f(k*)↑. This is the L12 slide on a rise in n, run in reverse. T7 M5.

Q23 · Poverty trap characteristics2 marks

Consider an economy stuck in a poverty trap. This economy is likely to have [high population growth rates and low output per capita / low population growth rates and low output per capita / high population growth rates and high output per capita / low population growth rates and high output per capita].

Answer & working

Answer: high population growth rates and low output per capita. 🟢 confident

L12's poverty-trap slide: low income per capita and high population growth reinforce each other (point A in the lecture diagram). T7 M8.

Q24 · Escape the poverty trap (written)2 marks

Propose one policy that can help the economy out of the poverty trap. Explain your answer with the Solow growth model, i.e. how the policy shifts the output-per-capita curve f(k), the break-even investment curve (n+δ)k and/or the per-capita saving curve s·f(k).

Answer & working

Answer (either of L12's two recommendations): population-control policies, which pivot (n+δ)k down; or raising the saving rate, at least temporarily, which shifts s·f(k) up. 🟢 confident

Model answer: "Introduce population-control policies (access to birth control, education for women) to lower n. This pivots the break-even investment curve (n+δ)k down, so at the trap's low k, s·f(k) now exceeds break-even investment and k rises. If the shift is large enough, the low (poverty-trap) steady state disappears and the economy grows to the high steady state with higher k and y."

Also fine: raise s temporarily, e.g. through savings incentives or foreign investment, so s·f(k) shifts up and removes the low crossing. You can also mention technology transfer (f(k) and s·f(k) shift up). Whichever you choose, name the curve and its direction, since the question asks for it.

Q25 · Absolute and comparative advantage8 marks

Four questions, 2 marks each. Options for each: Neither / Country A / Country B / Both.

  1. Which country has an absolute advantage in the production of Computers?
  2. …an absolute advantage in Dinosaurs?
  3. …a comparative advantage in Computers?
  4. …a comparative advantage in Dinosaurs?
Answer & working

Answers: (1) B · (2) A · (3) B · (4) A. 🟢 confident

Absolute advantage: for computers, B's 20 h < A's 30 h; for dinosaurs, A's 20 h < B's 40 h. Comparative advantage: for computers, B's 0.5D < A's 1.5D; for dinosaurs, A's 0.67C < B's 2C. Here each country's absolute and comparative advantage are in the same good. T5 M1.

Q26 · Mutually beneficial terms of trade4 marks

Only two (2) of the following terms of trade between A and B are mutually beneficial. Which two?

  • ☐ 2 units of Dinosaurs for 1 unit of Computer
  • ☐ 3 units of Computers for 1 unit of Dinosaur
  • ☐ 1.25 units of Dinosaurs for 1 unit of Computer.
  • ☐ 1 unit of Computer for 1 unit of Dinosaurs.
Answer & working

Answers: "1.25 Dinosaurs for 1 Computer" and "1 Computer for 1 Dinosaur". 🟢 confident

The price of 1 computer must lie strictly between 0.5D and 1.5D. Check each option: 2D per C is too high (A would make its own computers). "3 C for 1 D" is 1/3 D per C, too low (B wouldn't sell). Watch the unit flip on that one. 1.25D per C ✓. 1D per C ✓. T5 M2.

Option 2 puts dinosaurs as the unit. Convert it to "D per 1 C" before comparing: 3C = 1D means 1C = 1/3 D.

Q27 · Who sells what at 1 : 14 marks

Suppose the terms of trade is 1 unit of Computers for 1 unit of Dinosaurs. Which two (2) statements are correct?

  • ☐ Country A will sell Dinosaurs.
  • ☐ Country A will sell Computers.
  • ☐ Country B will sell Dinosaurs.
  • ☐ Country B will sell Computers.
Answer & working

Answers: "Country A will sell Dinosaurs" and "Country B will sell Computers". 🟢 confident

At 1C = 1D, A gets a computer for 1D instead of 1.5D at home, so A buys computers and sells dinosaurs. B gets 1D per computer that costs it 0.5D, so B sells computers.

Q28 · Specialisation and consumption with trade4 marks

Two questions, 2 marks each. Suppose the two countries can trade at 1 Computer for 1 Dinosaur.

(i) Which of the following statements is true if trade is possible?

  • ○ Country A should produce 30 Dinosaurs, and import Computers.
  • ○ Country A should produce 20 Computers and import Dinosaurs.
  • ○ Country B should produce 30 Computers and import Dinosaurs.
  • ○ Country B should produce 20 Dinosaurs, and import Computers.

(ii) Which of the following combinations will it be possible for Country A to consume with trade?

  • ○ 30 Dinosaurs and 10 Computers.
  • ○ 15 Dinosaurs and 15 Computers.
  • ○ 20 Computers and 20 Dinosaurs.
  • ○ Not enough information to answer the question.
Answer & working

Answers: (i) Country A should produce 30 Dinosaurs and import Computers. (ii) 15 Dinosaurs and 15 Computers. 🟢 confident

  1. A has the comparative advantage in dinosaurs, so it fully specialises: 600/20 = 30 D. B would specialise in computers and make 40, not 30, so option 3 has the wrong number. Options 2 and 4 have the wrong goods.
  2. A's consumption-possibility frontier at 1:1 is D + C = 30. Check: 30 + 10 = 40 ✗; 15 + 15 = 30 ✓; 20 + 20 = 40 ✗. B can supply up to 40 computers, so 15 is available. 15D + 15C would need 15×20 + 15×30 = 750 h > 600 without trade, so this is a genuine gain from trade. T5 M4–M5.

What this paper teaches

  • Same 5 × 20 structure as S1 2024 and the 2026 papers. Several items are near-copies of S1 2024 with new numbers: the rental shift, the GDP table, the externality grid, the "Regulate / Status quo" game, Solow transition dropdowns, the trade advantage ×4 / ToT / who-sells / bundle questions. Treat those templates as guaranteed marks.
  • Floor vs ceiling at the same price (Q3). First ask whether it binds. A floor binds only above P*, a ceiling only below P*. A non-binding policy changes nothing.
  • "Exclude inflation" means real GDP growth. Nominal growth is always one of the options.
  • Self-correction wording: Y > Ȳ → low unemployment → SRAS left. Y < Ȳ → high unemployment → SRAS right. The dropdown offers "high inflation rate" as a decoy.
  • Parallel SMC/PMC (this paper) vs non-parallel (S1 2024). Always read MEC at Qeff; with parallel curves it's the same everywhere.
  • Payoff matrices get recycled with roles swapped. Write out both players' conditional best responses every time.
  • Solow k₁/k₂/k₃ diagram: kss = s·f(k) ∩ (δ+n)k. kG = tangent point. f(k) ∩ (δ+n)k = zero-consumption decoy. If kss > kG, the answer is "decrease the saving rate".
  • n↓ → break-even line pivots down → k*↑, y*↑. Poverty trap = high n + low y. The escapes are lower n (pivot down) or higher s (shift up). Learn the 2-sentence written answer.
  • ToT options can switch which good is the unit ("3 Computers for 1 Dinosaur"). Convert everything to "per 1 unit of the same good" before comparing with the OCs.

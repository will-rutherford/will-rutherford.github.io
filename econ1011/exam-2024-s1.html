Part 1 · City B rental market (20 marks)

This question concerns the rental market of City B, a major capital city in Australia. City B's rental market conditions are given by the diagram below.

Q1 · Equilibrium2 marks

According to the diagram, what are the equilibrium price and quantity transacted of rental units in City B?

  • ○ Equilibrium price is $500, quantity transacted is 700.
  • ○ Equilibrium price is $500, quantity transacted is 1200.
  • ○ Equilibrium price is $650, quantity transacted is 1000.
  • ○ Equilibrium price is $850, quantity transacted is 700.
Answer & working

Answer: price $650, quantity 1000. 🟢 confident

Equilibrium is where D and S cross: the dashed lines from that point meet the axes at $650 and 1000. The other options are the rent-control points used in Q3: (500, 700) is on S, (500, 1200) is on D and (850, 700) is on D. T1 M1.

Q2 · Olympics infrastructure → rental market10 marks

Suppose the local government of City B requires construction workers to build infrastructure projects (because the city is going to host an Olympics). Complete the following paragraph to describe how this would affect the rental market in City B. You can assume that part of the rental units on the market are newly built.

"As rental housing buildings and infrastructure buildings are [substitutes in consumption / complements in consumption / substitutes in production / complements in production], the government's infrastructure projects will [increase the demand for / reduce the demand for / increase the supply of / reduce the supply of] rental housing, shifting the [demand curve to the left / demand curve to the right / supply curve to the left / supply curve to the right]. The equilibrium price of rental housing will [increase / decrease / remain unchanged / increase or decrease depending on the magnitude of the shock] while the equilibrium quantity of rental housing will [increase / decrease / remain unchanged / increase or decrease depending on the magnitude of the shock]."

Answer & working

Answers: substitutes in production → reduce the supply of → supply curve to the left → price increase → quantity decrease. 🟢 confident

  1. Housing and infrastructure both use the same inputs (construction workers, materials), so they are substitutes in production. L3 defines these as goods that need the same or similar inputs.
  2. The Olympics projects pull workers and materials away from housing and bid up their price. That raises the cost of building rental housing, so supply falls. The "part of the rental units are newly built" line is the hint that supply depends on new construction.
  3. Supply shifts left with demand unchanged, so P↑ and Q↓. Only one curve moves, so neither direction is ambiguous.
Don't let "workers moving to the city" push you to "increase the demand for". The first dropdown has already framed this as a production relationship. Also, "complements in production" means goods produced together (chicken breasts and wings). That isn't the case here.

Q3 · Rent control at $5004 marks

Now suppose the local government abandons the infrastructure projects described in Q2, and City B's rental market conditions are still given by the diagram. (2 marks each)

(i) If the government imposes a rent control at $500, what will be the quantity transacted in City B's rental market?

  • ○ 700
  • ○ 1000
  • ○ 1200
  • ○ indeterminable — it depends on how the supply curve shifts.

(ii) If there is a black market for rental housing, what will be the black market rent?

  • ○ $500
  • ○ $650
  • ○ $850
  • ○ Indeterminable — it depends on how the supply curve shifts.
Answer & working

Answers: (i) 700; (ii) $850. 🟢 confident

  1. $500 is below the equilibrium $650, so the ceiling binds.
  2. At $500: QS = 700 and QD = 1200, a shortage of 500. Quantity transacted = the short side = min(700, 1200) = 700.
  3. Black-market rent is what renters are willing to pay for the 700 units available. Go up from Q = 700 to the demand curve: $850.

T2 M1.

1200 is quantity demanded, not transacted. $650 is the old equilibrium. The black-market price comes from the demand curve at QS, not from the supply curve.

Q4 · Drawbacks of rent controls4 marks

In addition to the impact you identified in Q3, which two (2) of the following are drawbacks of rent controls? Select two.

  • ☐ Reduction of information in the rental market.
  • ☐ Increase in landlords' cost of maintenance.
  • ☐ Reduction in the quality of rental housing.
  • ☐ Non-price competition among renters disadvantaging vulnerable renters.
Answer & working

Answers: "Reduction in the quality of rental housing" and "Non-price competition among renters disadvantaging vulnerable renters". 🟢 confident

These are the L4 "unintended consequences" slides. Landlords pick "desirable" tenants (non-price competition), and this hurts vulnerable renters most. Rent controls also cut landlords' returns, so they cut back on maintenance, which lowers quality.

"Increase in landlords' cost of maintenance" is the decoy. Rent control makes landlords spend less on maintenance; it doesn't raise the cost. "Reduction of information" isn't a lecture drawback.

Q5 · Nominal GDP 20233 marks

Calculate Far-Far-Away's nominal GDP in 2023. Show your working.

Answer & working

Answer: $10,000. 🟢 confident

Nominal GDP2023 = ΣP2023×Q2023 = 20×300 + 40×100 = 6,000 + 4,000 = $10,000. T4 M7.

Q6 · Real GDP 2023 (base 2022)4 marks

Calculate Far-Far-Away's real GDP in 2023 using 2022 as the base year. Show your working.

Answer & working

Answer: $9,500. 🟢 confident

Real GDP2023 = ΣP2022×Q2023 = 15×300 + 50×100 = 4,500 + 5,000 = $9,500.

Use base-year prices with current-year quantities. Writing 15×200 + 50×120 = 9,000 gives 2022 GDP, not 2023 real GDP.

Q7 · GDP deflator 20233 marks

Using 2022 as the base year, calculate the GDP deflator in 2023 for Far-Far-Away. Show your working.

Answer & working

Answer: ≈ 105.3 (prices about 5.3% higher than in 2022). 🟢 confident

GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100 = 10,000 ÷ 9,500 × 100 = 105.26.

Not in the 2026 lectures. The 2026 Week 6 slides cover nominal vs real GDP but never mention the deflator. This is probably one of the 2024→2025 topic changes. Add the formula to your binder anyway; it's one line. In the base year the deflator = 100.

Q9 · Where is E?1 mark

If Far-Far-Away's economy is at point E in the diagram in Q8, then:

  • ○ It is in neither a short-run nor long-run equilibrium.
  • ○ It is in a short-run but not long-run equilibrium.
  • ○ It is in a long-run but not short-run equilibrium.
  • ○ It is in both a short-run and a long-run equilibrium.
Answer & working

Answer: short-run but not long-run equilibrium. 🟢 confident

E is on AD ∩ SRAS, so it's a short-run equilibrium. It lies left of LRAS, so Y < potential (a recessionary gap) and it isn't a long-run equilibrium. T4 M3.

Q10 · Policy to close the gap4 marks

Suppose the economy is at point E. If the government would like to use fiscal policy to move the economy to its potential real GDP, it can [increase government expenditure / reduce government expenditure / increase the interest rate / reduce the interest rate].

Likewise, if the Central Bank would like to use monetary policy to move the economy to its potential real GDP, it can [same four options].

These measures will shift [the AD to the right / the SRAS to the left / the AD to the right and SRAS to the left / both AR and SRAS to the right], leading to [a decrease / an increase / an indeterminable / an unchanged] in the price level while moving the economy to its potential real GDP.

Answer & working

Answers: increase government expenditure · reduce the interest rate · the AD to the right · an increase in the price level. 🟢 confident

Y < potential calls for expansionary policy. Fiscal: G↑. Monetary: i↓, which raises C and I. Both raise spending, so AD shifts right along the upward SRAS. Y rises to potential and P rises. T4 M3–M4.

The fiscal dropdown includes interest-rate options and the monetary dropdown includes G options. Match the tool to who controls it. Policy moves AD, not SRAS. The SRAS shifting right is the no-policy self-correction path, and that would lower P.

Part 3 · Invisible-cloak externality and the regulation game (20 marks)

The production of invisible cloaks (the cloaks, not their wearers, are invisible) leaks a significant amount of "forever chemicals" into the environment, causing externalities. The diagram shows the Marginal Benefit (MB), Private Marginal Cost (PMC) and Social Marginal Cost (SMC) of invisible-cloak production. The diagram is drawn to scale, and the grid indicates length and width.

Grid read (checked twice against the scan): SMC runs from (200, $20) to (1000, $100). PMC runs from (200, $15) to (1000, $55). MB runs from (50, $72.5) to (1000, $25). Crossings: MB∩PMC at (700, $40) and MB∩SMC at (500, $50).

Q11 · Click: competitive market equilibrium1 mark

Indicate on the diagram the intersection that determines the competitive market equilibrium quantity.

Answer & working

Answer: click MB ∩ PMC, at Q = 700, P = $40. 🟢 confident

Producers ignore the external cost, so the market uses private costs. T6 M1.

Q12 · Click: social optimum1 mark

Indicate on the diagram the intersection that determines the quantity that maximises social surplus.

Answer & working

Answer: click MB ∩ SMC, at Q = 500, P = $50. 🟢 confident

Q13 · Deadweight loss4 marks

Calculate the deadweight loss associated with the externality of invisible-cloak production. Show your working.

Answer & working

Answer: DWL = $3,000. 🟢 confident

  1. Qmkt = 700 and Qeff = 500, so the base = 200.
  2. Height = gap at Qmkt between SMC and MB = SMC(700) − MB(700) = $70 − $40 = $30. (SMC at 700 is 7 squares up, i.e. $70.)
  3. DWL = ½ × 200 × 30 = $3,000.

T6 M3.

The height is SMC − MB at the market quantity ($30), not SMC − PMC at the optimum ($20), and not the price gap $50 − $40.

Q14 · Corrective policy3 marks

If the government wants to correct the externality, it can impose a per-unit [subsidy / tax] of the amount [$50 − $30 = $20 / $50 − $40 = $10 / $70 − $30 = $40 / $70 − $40 = $30].

Answer & working

Answers: tax · $50 − $30 = $20. 🟢 confident

Negative externality, so use a tax. Pigouvian tax = MEC at the efficient quantity = SMC(500) − PMC(500) = $50 − $30 = $20. PMC at 500 is 5 + 0.05×500 = $30. With t = $20, PMC + t passes through (500, $50), so the market picks Q = 500. T6 M2.

Here SMC and PMC aren't parallel: MEC grows with Q. The $70 − $40 = $30 option is the gap at the market quantity. A $30 tax would overshoot to Q < 500. $50 − $40 is just the price change.

Country A and Country B are trying to reach an international agreement to regulate their invisible-cloak industry. Each can choose, independently, to Regulate the industry or keep the Status quo. Payoffs are (A, B).

B: RegulateB: Status quo
A: Regulate1, 14, 0

Q15 · Does A have a dominant strategy?4 marks

Does Country A have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

Answer: Yes. Regulate is A's dominant strategy. 🟢 confident

If B Regulates, A gets 1 from Regulate vs 0 from Status quo, so A picks Regulate. If B keeps the Status quo, A gets 4 from Regulate vs 3 from Status quo, so A picks Regulate. Regulate is A's best response whatever B does, so it is dominant. T6 M6.

Model written answer: "Yes. If B regulates, A gets 1 by regulating vs 0 by status quo; if B keeps the status quo, A gets 4 vs 3. Regulating is A's best response regardless of B's choice, so Regulate is A's dominant strategy."

Q16 · Does B have a dominant strategy?4 marks

Refer to the same payoff matrix. Does Country B have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working

Answer: No. 🟢 confident

B's payoff is the 2nd number. If A Regulates, B gets 1 from Regulate vs 0 from Status quo, so B picks Regulate. If A keeps the Status quo, B gets 2 from Regulate vs 3 from Status quo, so B picks the Status quo. B's best response depends on A's choice, so B has no dominant strategy.

Read B's payoffs down the columns using the second number in each cell. Compare B's two options while holding A's row fixed.

Q17 · Nash equilibria3 marks

The 2 × 2 game has [0 / 1 / 2 / 4] Nash equilibrium/equilibria (in pure strategy, the only kind discussed in class). It is/They are [none of the combinations of choices / (Regulate, Regulate) / (Status quo, Status quo) / (Regulate, Regulate) and (Status quo, Status quo) / all four combinations of choices].

Answer & working

Answers: 1 · (Regulate, Regulate). 🟢 confident

A always plays Regulate (dominant). B's best reply to Regulate is Regulate (1 > 0). So the only NE is (Regulate, Regulate), with payoffs (1, 1). Check (Status quo, Status quo): A would switch to Regulate (4 > 3), so it isn't an NE, even though (3, 3) is better for both.

(Status quo, Status quo) gives the highest joint payoff, but "best for everyone" isn't the test for an NE. The test is "no player wants to deviate alone".

Part 4 · Solow growth model (20 marks)

Consider an economy in which per-capita output depends only on the capital–labour ratio, the saving rate is s, the population growth rate is n and the depreciation rate is δ. This question concerns the long-run growth of this economy.

Q18 · Label the Solow diagram8 marks

The diagram depicts the per-capita output, saving and capital maintenance of the economy. Complete it by dragging the labels to their positions, and label the two axes as well. y_ss, k_ss and c_ss are the steady-state per-capita output, capital–labour ratio and consumption. y_G, k_G and c_G are the Golden Rule values.

Labels: k_G · Capital-labour ratio · y_G · k_ss · Per capita output · c_ss · c_G · y_ss

Answer & working

Answers: ① Per capita output (y-axis) · ② y_ss · ③ y_G · ④ c_G · ⑤ c_ss · ⑥ k_G · ⑦ k_ss · ⑧ Capital-labour ratio (x-axis). 🟢 confident

  1. k_ss is where s·f(k) meets (n+δ)k. That's the right-hand dashed line (T7 M1).
  2. k_G is where f(k) has the same slope as (n+δ)k. That's where the dashed tangent touches f(k), the left-hand dashed line (T7 M3).
  3. The y-labels sit at f(k) above each k. The right one is higher, so it's y_ss; the lower one is y_G.
  4. Consumption c = f(k) − (n+δ)k in steady state, which is the arrow from the maintenance line up to f(k). The left arrow is c_G and the right arrow is c_ss (T7 M4).
At k_ss the arrow starts on the (n+δ)k line, which is also where s·f(k) is. At k_G it starts on (n+δ)k, not on s·f(k): Golden Rule consumption is f(k) − (n+δ)k. Don't put k_ss at the f(k) ∩ (n+δ)k crossing on the far right; that's where c = 0.

Q19 · Transition from k₀ < k_ss6 marks

Suppose the economy starts with a capital–labour ratio k₀ below the steady-state ratio. At k₀, saving per capita is [lower than / higher than / the same as / incomparable to] the per-capita investment required to maintain the capital–labour ratio. Hence the capital–labour ratio will [increase / decrease / remain unchanged / be indeterminable]. This adjustment will stop when the capital–labour ratio reaches [zero / the steady-state capital–labour ratio / the Golden Rule capital–labour ratio / infinity].

Answer & working

Answers: higher than · increase · the steady-state capital–labour ratio. 🟢 confident

Left of k_ss, the s·f(k) curve lies above (n+δ)k, so Δk = s·f(k) − (n+δ)k > 0. k rises until saving just covers break-even investment, which happens at k_ss. T7 M2.

The economy goes to the steady state, not the Golden Rule level. Nothing in the model steers it toward k_G unless s is changed.

Q20 · T/F: more saving → closer to Golden Rule2 marks

True or false: if the government of this economy wishes to stimulate economic growth by encouraging saving (i.e., increasing the saving rate), the new steady-state consumption level will be closer to the Golden Rule consumption level.

  • ○ True
  • ○ False
Answer & working

Answer: False. 🟢 confident

In the Q18 diagram, k_ss is to the right of k_G, so this economy already saves too much. Raising s pushes k_ss further right, away from k_G, so c_ss falls further below c_G. To get closer to the Golden Rule it should lower s. T7 M3.

"Saving is good" is the QCAA instinct. In Solow, whether more saving helps consumption depends on which side of k_G you're on, so always check the diagram.

Q21 · Policy for growth AND higher c_ss (written, ≤100 words)4 marks

Suggest one (1) policy to the government of this economy if it wants to stimulate long-run growth AND increase the steady-state consumption level. Briefly explain your suggestion. (Answers are expected to be at most 100 words; marks can be deducted for extraneous answers.)

Answer & working

Answer: a policy that raises technology/productivity (A), e.g. R&D subsidies or tax incentives, investment in education/human capital, or better institutions and property rights. 🟡 judgement call

Model answer (~70 words): "Subsidise R&D and education to raise total factor productivity A. Higher A shifts f(k) and s·f(k) up, so the steady-state k and y both rise. Steady-state consumption c = f(k) − (n+δ)k rises because output rises at every k. Sustained technological progress is the only source of long-run growth in per-capita output, because capital accumulation alone runs into diminishing returns."

Why 🟡: it's an open written question with no key. Technology fits both conditions, and L12 says "economic growth relies on technology". Don't propose raising s: this economy over-saves (Q20), so that would lower c_ss. Lowering s would raise c_ss but lower y, so it isn't "growth". Lowering n is also defensible (it raises k_ss and y_ss), but its effect on c_ss compared with the new Golden Rule is less clear-cut than technology's.

Q22 · Absolute and comparative advantage8 marks

Four questions, 2 marks each. Options for each: Neither / Country A / Country B / Both.

  1. Which country has an absolute advantage in the production of wheat?
  2. …an absolute advantage in cotton?
  3. …a comparative advantage in wheat?
  4. …a comparative advantage in cotton?
Answer & working

Answers: (1) B · (2) B · (3) A · (4) B. 🟢 confident

  • Absolute advantage means fewer hours per unit. For wheat, B's 2 < A's 3; for cotton, B's 4 < A's 15. B has the absolute advantage in both.
  • Comparative advantage means the lower opportunity cost. For wheat, A's 0.2C < B's 0.5C, so A has it. For cotton, B's 2W < A's 5W, so B has it.

T5 M1.

Having an absolute advantage in both goods doesn't mean having a comparative advantage in both. Each country always has a comparative advantage in one good (unless the OCs are equal).

Q23 · Mutually beneficial terms of trade4 marks

Only two (2) of the following terms of trade between A and B are mutually beneficial. Which two?

  • ☐ 1 unit of cotton for 1 unit of wheat
  • ☐ 1 unit of cotton for 3 units of wheat
  • ☐ 2 units of cotton for 7 units of wheat
  • ☐ 2 units of cotton for 11 units of wheat
Answer & working

Answers: "1 cotton for 3 wheat" and "2 cotton for 7 wheat". 🟢 confident

The price of 1 cotton must lie strictly between the two OCs: 2W < PC < 5W. Check each option: 1C:1W gives 1 W per C (too low, so A wouldn't sell wheat). 1C:3W gives 3 ✓. 2C:7W gives 3.5 ✓. 2C:11W gives 5.5 (too high, so A would rather make its own cotton). T5 M2.

Q24 · Who sells what at 1 cotton : 4 wheat4 marks

Suppose the terms of trade is 1 unit of cotton for 4 units of wheat. Which two (2) statements are correct?

  • ☐ Country A will sell wheat.
  • ☐ Country A will sell cotton.
  • ☐ Country B will sell wheat.
  • ☐ Country B will sell cotton.
Answer & working

Answers: "Country A will sell wheat" and "Country B will sell cotton". 🟢 confident

At 4W per cotton, A pays 4W for cotton on the market instead of 5W at home, so A buys cotton and sells wheat. B gets 4W for each cotton that costs it 2W at home, so B sells cotton. Each country exports its comparative-advantage good. T5 M3.

What this paper teaches

  • The structure matches 2026 exactly. Rental market → macro → externality + 2×2 game → Solow → trade, 20 marks each. The externality grid (click 2 points → DWL → tax amount) and the 2×2 regulation game (dominant for A? for B? number of NE?) come up in every paper. Pre-write the template answers.
  • Non-parallel SMC/PMC (Q14). The tax = MEC at Qeff. The gap at Qmkt is the decoy, and they give it as an option written out ("$70 − $40 = $30").
  • DWL height is taken at Qmkt (SMC − MB). The base is |Qmkt − Qeff|.
  • Rent control: quantity transacted = QS at the ceiling. Black-market rent = the demand curve at that QS. The drawbacks are quality↓ (maintenance cut) and non-price competition. The "maintenance cost ↑" option is a word-swap trap.
  • Solow diagrams: k_ss = s·f(k) ∩ (n+δ)k. k_G = tangent parallel to (n+δ)k. c = f(k) − (n+δ)k. Check whether k_ss is left or right of k_G before any "should s rise?" question. In this paper the economy over-saves, so "raise s" is wrong.
  • Trade bundle questions: test the no-trade bundles with the hours constraint (hWW + hCC ≤ hours). Test the with-trade bundles with "max own good + ToT" (W + 4C ≤ 200).
  • Drag-the-label questions (AD–AS, Solow) appear in 2024. They're the same knowledge as "click the intersection", so be able to name every curve, axis and arrow.
  • Add to your binder: GDP deflator = nominal/real × 100 (2024 only, but cheap insurance). Also add a 3-line model answer for "Does X have a dominant strategy?": state both conditional best responses, then the conclusion.

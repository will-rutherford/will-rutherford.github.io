1 · The big idea

Governments intervene in competitive markets with price controls (ceilings, floors), per-unit taxes and per-unit subsidies (and occasionally quotas). Every one of these drives a wedge between what the market would do on its own and what actually happens. A binding ceiling or floor cuts the quantity traded and creates a shortage or a surplus. A tax drives a gap between what buyers pay (PB) and what sellers keep (PS), and also cuts quantity. A subsidy raises quantity above the efficient level. Anything that moves quantity away from the competitive Q* creates a deadweight loss. Elasticity decides who carries the burden of a tax (or gets the gain from a subsidy): the less elastic side carries more of it.

Lecture story: Australia's housing/rental crisis. Rent control (a price ceiling) backfires because it causes shortages, black markets and run-down housing. A rental subsidy (NRAS, paid to landlords) gives lower rents and more units, but it costs the budget and mostly helps landlords because rental supply is inelastic. Increasing supply (zoning, Housing Australia Future Fund) fixes the root cause, but slowly.

2 · Concepts, one bite at a time

2.1 Price controls: ceiling vs floor, binding vs non-binding

  • Price ceiling = legal maximum price (e.g. rent control: rent caps or rent freezes).
  • Price floor = legal minimum price (e.g. minimum wage, AUD 26.44/hr on the slide. In the labour market the employers are the buyers).
  • A control only does anything if it is binding. The exam also says "effective" for binding and "ineffective" for non-binding.
Binding / effective when…ResultNon-binding / ineffective when…

Floor (min)set ABOVE P*P = floor; Q traded = QD (short side); excess supply = QS − QDset at or below P*: P*, Q* unchanged
Memory hook: a ceiling only bites if it is low enough to hit the price; a floor only bites if it is high enough. The quantity traded is always the smaller of QD and QS, because nobody can be forced to buy or sell more than they want.
Ceiling → shortage, floor → surplus, black markets. Same as QCAA.
Black-market price under a binding ceiling. Only QS units exist, so desperate renters will bid up to the demand curve's height at QS. Read it as: go up from QS to the demand curve, then across to the price axis (lecture: "the black-market rent may be as high as PBM, what renters are willing to pay given the quantity available").
Worked example (main market). D: P = 10 − Q/100, S: P = 1 + Q/200.
Equilibrium: 10 − Q/100 = 1 + Q/200 → 9 = 3Q/200 → Q* = 600, P* = $4.
• Ceiling $5: above $4, so non-binding. Nothing changes.
• Ceiling $3: below $4, so binding. QS: 3 = 1 + Q/200 → 400. QD: 3 = 10 − Q/100 → 700. Shortage = 300. Q traded = 400. Black-market rent = D at Q = 400 = 10 − 4 = $6.
• Floor $6: above $4, so binding. QD: 6 = 10 − Q/100 → 400. QS: 6 = 1 + Q/200 → 1000. Excess supply = 600. Q traded = 400.

Who wins and who loses (Lecture 4 slides 28 & 34):

Binding…BuyersSellers
Ceiling (rent control)Lucky renters pay less; many are left homeless or pushed into the black marketUnambiguously worse: lower price AND lower quantity
Floor (min wage)Overall bad: higher price and lower quantityAmbiguous: higher price (good) but lower quantity sold (bad)

Unintended consequences of rent control (slides 35–36): price competition (black-market renting, illegal rent-bidding), non-price competition (landlords pick "desirable" tenants: no students, pets, kids or immigrants, which hurts vulnerable renters most), lower quality (less maintenance) and lower long-run supply (landlords sell up, investors are deterred, so the shortage gets worse). Lecture verdict: "Rent control backfires."

2.2 Surplus, social surplus and deadweight loss (the area-accounting toolkit)

  • Demand curve = marginal benefit (MB) curve. The area under D up to Q = total benefit.
  • Supply curve = marginal cost (MC) curve. The area under S up to Q = total (variable) cost.
  • Consumer surplus (CS) = area below demand, above the price buyers pay, up to the quantity traded.
  • Producer surplus (PS) = area above supply, below the price sellers receive, up to the quantity traded.
  • Social surplus (SS) = CS + PS (+ government revenue − government spending) = total benefit − production cost.
  • Deadweight loss (DWL) = the SS lost compared with the competitive equilibrium. It is the triangle between D and S over the units not traded (under-production), or over the units that should not have been traded (over-production).
CS and PS triangles are familiar. Triangle area = ½ × base × height. Rectangle = width × height.
The lecturer defines SS as total benefit − production cost and says both under-producing and over-producing create a DWL (Lecture 5). So a subsidy also creates a DWL, even though Q rises. Area letters are always taken from the given figure. Read them carefully, because the letters in exam figures will not match the ones on this page.

DWL shortcut: DWL = ½ × (vertical gap between D and S at the new Q) × |Q* − Qnew|. For a tax or subsidy the vertical gap is just the tax or subsidy per unit.

2.3 Per-unit (sales) tax: PB, PS, PM

The lecturer's notation (L4 slide 44, L13 slide 23). Every exam answer is phrased with these three prices:

SymbolNameMeaningTax collected from sellers
PMMarket price"Sticker price": what the buyer hands the sellerthe price where D meets S with tax
PBBuyers' pricewhat buyers actually pay out of pocket (the price buyers care about)PB = PM
PSSellers' pricewhat actually ends up in the seller's pocket (the price sellers care about)PS = PM − Tax

Why supply shifts UP by the tax: the supply curve plots market price against QS, but sellers only care about PS. If sellers want PS = $5 to supply 12 apples, then with a $2 tax the market price must be PM = PS + 2 = $7 to get the same 12 apples. Every point on S moves up by exactly the tax (L13 slides 24–27). A unit tax works like a "negative subsidy". It reduces supply, raises PB, lowers PS and lowers Q.

Worked example: $3 tax on sellers (main market).
1. S with tax: P = 1 + Q/200 + 3 = 4 + Q/200.
2. Solve with D: 10 − Q/100 = 4 + Q/200 → 6 = 3Q/200 → Qtax = 400.
3. PB = PM = 10 − 4 = $6. PS = 6 − 3 = $3 (check: original S at Q = 400 is 1 + 2 = 3 ✓).
4. Consumers' tax burden = increase in buyers' price = PB − P* = 6 − 4 = $2 per unit. Sellers' tax burden = fall in sellers' price = P* − PS = 4 − 3 = $1 per unit. They sum to 2 + 1 = 3 = tax ✓.
5. Tax revenue = tax × Qtax = 3 × 400 = $1200 (= B + D = 2·400 + 1·400).
6. DWL = ½ × 3 × (600 − 400) = $300 (= C + E = 200 + 100).
Areas: before, CS = A+B+C = 1800 and PS = D+E+F = 900. After, CS = A = 800, PS = F = 400, Govt = B+D = 1200, lost = C+E = 300.
Burden is measured as the change in each side's own price (L13: "Consumers' tax burden: increase in buyers' price due to the tax"), not as who legally pays the cheque. It doesn't matter whether the tax is levied on buyers or sellers: the market outcome is the same (lecture says the same for subsidies). The lecture always works with the tax collected from sellers, so that is the case to know.

2.4 Per-unit subsidy (rental subsidy / NRAS)

  • Subsidy = a government payment to suppliers or consumers to encourage production or consumption. There are two kinds. An ad-valorem subsidy is a % of price. A unit subsidy is a fixed $ per unit. The course uses unit subsidies only.
  • The course pays it to sellers (NRAS paid landlords; the outcome is the same if paid to buyers).
  • Prices: PB = PM, PS = PM + subsidy.
  • Supply shifts DOWN (right) by the subsidy, so supply increases. The new PM is where D meets S−sub.
  • Buyers' gain = P* − PB. Sellers' gain = PS − P*. Government cost = subsidy × Qsub. DWL comes from over-production (Qsub > Q*).
Worked example: $3 subsidy to sellers. S−sub: P = 1 + Q/200 − 3 = −2 + Q/200. Solve with D: 10 − Q/100 = −2 + Q/200 → 12 = 3Q/200 → Q = 800. PB = PM = 10 − 8 = $2. PS = 2 + 3 = $5.
Renters gain 4 − 2 = $2/unit. Landlords gain 5 − 4 = $1/unit. Govt cost = 3 × 800 = $2400. CS rises by 3200 − 1800 = 1400. PS rises by 1600 − 900 = 700. Net = 1400 + 700 − 2400 = −300 = DWL = ½ × 3 × (800 − 600).

Lecture verdict: a rental subsidy lowers rents and increases the number of rental properties, so it is "probably a better policy than rent controls". But there's no free lunch. The opportunity cost is the social benefit lost from other government programs (education, health, social security) that the money could have funded.

2.5 Elasticity: what it is and exactly how this lecturer calculates it

Price elasticity of demand εD = %ΔQD / %ΔP, where %ΔQD = ΔQD/QD × 100% and %ΔP = ΔP/P × 100%. It is unit-free, unlike slope.

Sign convention: εD is a negative number (law of demand). The lecturer writes εD = −2 and uses |εD| = 2 only when he says "absolute value". Exam options include both signs, e.g. "−1.25 / 0.8 / −0.8 / 1.25 (do not use the absolute value)", so give the negative number unless told otherwise.
Method = POINT elasticity on a linear curve, not the QCAA/midpoint formula.
εD = (P/QD) ÷ (ΔP/ΔQD) = slope of ray from origin ÷ slope of demand curve = (1/slope) × (P/Q)
Lecture examples: Week 2 at A = (Q 6, P 8) on a demand from (0, 20) to (10, 0) gives ray 8/6 = 4/3, slope −2, so ε = −2/3. Practice exam at (Q 8, P 12) gives (12/8)/(−20/20) = −3/2. L13 Mario at (Q 36, P 64) gives (64/36)/(−1) = −16/9 ≈ −1.78.

Using ε: %ΔQD = εD × %ΔP. E.g. εD = −3 and price ↑1% gives QD ↓3%. Revenue: %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQD.

Determinants of demand elasticity (L3): (1) more close substitutes means more elastic (a single brand is more elastic than "food"); (2) longer time frame means more elastic; (3) luxury is more elastic than necessity; (4) a small share of the budget means more inelastic (salt).

Price elasticity of supply εS = %ΔQS/%ΔP and is positive. A vertical S is perfectly inelastic and a horizontal S is perfectly elastic. The lecturer says "don't worry about calculating it". You need it conceptually: housing supply is inelastic in the short run because it takes time to build, which is why demand increases cause big rent rises.

A steeper curve is (relatively) more inelastic when you compare curves through the same point. Never compare elasticity of two points on the same straight line by slope. Use the ray/slope formula.

2.6 Elasticity decides who bears a tax (and who gets a subsidy)

Rule: the LESS elastic side bears MORE of the tax burden (and gets MORE of a subsidy's gain).

  • Demand more elastic (buyers can walk away), so consumers bear a smaller share and sellers a larger share.
  • Demand less elastic, so consumers bear a larger share.
  • Subsidy (L4 slide 53): demand more elastic than supply means sellers get the larger share of the gain. Demand less elastic than supply means buyers get the larger share.
Housing application (L4 slide 54): rental demand is probably more elastic (housing is a big budget share, so households adjust), while rental supply is inelastic in the short run (building takes time). So landlords capture most of the gain from a rental subsidy. That is the catch in NRAS.
Optional cross-check (textbook, not on the slides): buyers' share of a tax ≈ εS / (εS + |εD|). Main market at (600, 4): εS = (4/600)·200 = 1.33 and |εD| = 0.67, so buyers' share = 1.33/2 = 2/3, which is $2 of $3 ✓. In the exam, just read PB and PS off the grid.

2.7 The other policies in Lecture 4

  • Increasing housing supply (supply shifts right): rents and prices fall and quantity rises. In the rental market, "rich renters" become owners, so rental demand also falls. The tools are zoning and density reform, the Housing Australia Future Fund ($10bn, 30,000 homes), inclusionary zoning and the Housing Accord (1.2m homes in 5 years). The obstacles are NIMBYism, builder bankruptcies, high interest rates, materials costs and labour shortages. It addresses the root cause, but slowly.
  • Negative gearing reform (side note): removing negative gearing cuts investor demand in the housing (purchase) market, so house prices fall. In the rental market, supply falls (investors sell) and demand falls (renters become owners, since owning and renting are substitutes), so the effect on rent is ambiguous. That is the classic both-curves-shift case.
  • Quota (theory left out): a cap on quantity supplied makes supply vertical at the quota. It is used when the goal is to restrict quantity (e.g. emissions).

3 · Step-by-step methods

M1 · Price ceiling / rent control on a grid
  1. Find P*, Q* (where D crosses S). If D shifts, use the new D.
  2. Ceiling below P* → binding/effective. At or above → non-binding/ineffective (answer: P*, Q*, no shortage).
  3. If binding: at the ceiling price read QS (on S) and QD (on D).
  4. Q traded = QS. Shortage = QD − QS.
  5. Black-market price = height of D at QS.
  6. DWL = ½ × (Pblack market − Pceiling) × (Q* − QS).
M2 · Price floor on a grid
  1. Floor above P* → binding. At or below → non-binding.
  2. If binding: Q traded = QD at the floor. Excess supply = QS − QD.
  3. DWL = ½ × (Pfloor − height of S at QD) × (Q* − QD).
  4. CS falls for sure. PS change is ambiguous (+ price rectangle, − lost-units triangle).
M3 · Per-unit tax (on sellers): PB, PS, burden, revenue, DWL
  1. Draw/identify S+tax = S shifted UP by the tax (the higher of two parallel supply lines is "Supply with tax").
  2. New equilibrium = D ∩ (S+tax) gives Qtax and PM = PB.
  3. PS = PB − tax = height of the original S at Qtax.
  4. Consumers' burden/unit = PB − P*. Sellers' burden/unit = P* − PS. Check: they sum to the tax.
  5. Revenue = tax × Qtax. DWL = ½ × tax × (Q* − Qtax).
  6. If given equations: add the tax to the supply equation's intercept (P = a + bQ becomes P = a + tax + bQ) and solve.
M4 · Per-unit subsidy (to sellers)
  1. S−sub = S shifted DOWN by the subsidy. D ∩ (S−sub) gives Qsub and PB = PM.
  2. PS = PM + subsidy = height of the original S at Qsub.
  3. Buyer gain = P* − PB. Seller gain = PS − P*. Govt cost = sub × Qsub. DWL = ½ × sub × (Qsub − Q*).
M5 · Point elasticity of demand at a point on a linear demand
  1. Read the point (Q, P).
  2. Slope of demand = ΔP/ΔQ between any two grid points on D (negative). Use the intercepts if visible: slope = −(P-intercept)/(Q-intercept).
  3. Slope of ray = P/Q.
  4. εD = (P/Q) ÷ slope. Keep the minus sign. Simplify to a fraction (exam options look like −3/2, −16/9, −1.25).
  5. Classify: |ε| > 1 elastic, = 1 unit, < 1 inelastic. Shortcut: above the midpoint of a linear D → elastic.
M6 · Using elasticity for predictions
  • %ΔQD = εD × %ΔP (e.g. ε = −0.5, P ↑10% gives QD ↓5%).
  • TR: elastic → move P and TR in opposite directions. Inelastic → same direction. %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ.
  • A supply shift with inelastic demand gives a big price change and small quantity change. Elastic demand gives the reverse.
  • A demand shift with inelastic supply gives a big price change and small quantity change (housing).
  • Tax/subsidy: the less elastic side bears more of the burden (gets more of the gain).
M7 · Surplus areas from a lettered figure
  1. Mark P* and Q*. Before the policy: CS = everything under D above P*. PS = everything above S below P*.
  2. After: CS = under D above PB up to new Q. PS = above S below PS up to new Q. Govt = rectangle tax × Q (tax) or −sub × Q (subsidy).
  3. DWL = the triangle(s) between D and S over the lost (or excess) units = old SS − new SS.

4 · How it's examined

Calibrated from the S2 2026 practice exam Q1 (rent ceiling on Robinson Island), S1 2026 final Q1 (bubble-tea tax, walked through in L13), S2 2025 final Q1–3 (cigarettes: elasticity, policy choice, two markets), and 2021 MCQs (DWL of ceilings/floors, tax revenue). The format is "Select alternative" dropdowns plus numeric fill-ins read off a grid drawn to scale.

QT1 · Rent ceiling with a demand shift (dropdowns). Grid: S passes (200, $200) and (800, $800), i.e. P = Q. D0: P = 1000 − Q. D1: P = 1400 − Q. Rent ceiling at $600.
• With D0: equilibrium is $500, Q 500. The ceiling of $600 is above, so the control is ineffective.
• With D1: equilibrium without control is $700, Q = 700. With the $600 ceiling, QS = 600 and QD = 1400 − 600 = 800, so quantity transacted = 600 and shortage = 200.
• Black-market rent = D1 at Q = 600 = 1400 − 600 = $800. DWL = ½ × (800 − 600) × (700 − 600) = $10,000.
Reasoning cue: quantity transacted is on the supply side, and black-market rent is on the demand curve at that quantity. The distractors will be QD and the ceiling itself.
QT2 · Price control MCQ (concept). "A price floor set above the equilibrium price will NOT result in a/an: (a) surplus of excess QS (b) DWL (c) efficient outcome (d) fall in CS." → (c). A binding control is never efficient.
"For a price floor to be effective it must be set ___ the equilibrium price" → above. For a ceiling → below.
"Which does NOT create a DWL in competitive markets: taxes / a shift in demand / quotas / tariffs" → a shift in demand. The market simply finds a new efficient equilibrium.
QT3 · Unit tax on a grid: label the curves, then PB, PS, Q, burden. D: P = 20 − Q/100. S: P = 2 + Q/50. Tax $6 per unit on sellers.
• Label: the upper parallel supply line is "Supply with tax" and the lower one is "Supply without tax".
• Before: 20 − Q/100 = 2 + Q/50 → Q* = 600, P* = $14.
• After: 20 − Q/100 = 8 + Q/50 → Q = 400, PB = PM = $16, PS = $10.
• Consumers' burden = 16 − 14 = $2/unit. Sellers' burden = 14 − 10 = $4/unit. Revenue = 6 × 400 = $2400. DWL = ½ × 6 × 200 = $600.
In L13's version the prices were labelled P1…P4: the price paid by consumers was the D ∩ S+tax height, the price received by producers was the original S at that Q, and the consumers' burden was PB − original P. Don't pick P1 (the S+tax height at the old Q). It's a distractor.
QT4 · Elasticity and tax burden (MCQ). "If demand becomes more elastic while supply is unchanged, will consumers bear a larger or smaller share of the tax burden?" → Smaller share. Buyers can switch away, so the price they pay rises less and sellers absorb more. Check with QT3: demand there is the more elastic side (εD = (14/600)/(−1/100) = −2.33 vs εS = (14/600)×50 = 1.17), and sellers bear $4 of $6 ✓.
QT5 · Compute εD at equilibrium (don't use the absolute value). Demand passes (200, $10) and (800, $4), and the equilibrium is at Q = 800, P = $4. Options: −2 / −0.5 / 0.5 / 2.
Slope = (4 − 10)/(800 − 200) = −6/600 = −1/100. Ray = 4/800 = 1/200. εD = (1/200) ÷ (−1/100) = −0.5, so demand is inelastic.
Follow-up: "a 10% price rise changes QD by…" → −0.5 × 10% = −5%, and total revenue rises.
QT6 · Pick the policy that achieves a goal. Goal: reduce consumption of cigarettes (P* = $5). Options: unit subsidy of $4 / price floor of $3 / price ceiling of $7 / price floor of $7.
→ Price floor of $7. It is binding (above $5) and Q traded = QD at $7 < Q*. It works by reducing the quantity of cigarettes demanded by smokers (a movement along D, not a shift of demand). A $3 floor and a $7 ceiling are non-binding, and a subsidy raises Q. A per-unit tax would also work (it reduces supply).
Goal "make housing affordable AND available": subsidy (PB ↓, Q ↑) or increase supply. A ceiling lowers P but reduces Q.
QT7 · Two markets with different elasticities. Premium and value cigarettes have identical supply, and demand for premium is much less elastic. A crop failure decreases supply in both by the same amount. Bigger price change? → Premium (inelastic demand: buyers keep buying, so price does the adjusting). Bigger quantity change? → Value (elastic demand).
QT8 · Subsidy incidence. "Rental demand is more elastic than rental supply. Who gets the larger share of the gain from a rental subsidy paid to landlords?" → Landlords (sellers), because they are the less elastic side. Who legally receives it doesn't matter. In the numbers: in the main market (demand less elastic) a $3 subsidy gave renters $2 and landlords $1.
QT9 · Combined shifts from policy (dropdowns). Negative gearing removed: in the rental market supply ↓ (investors sell) and demand ↓ (renters buy homes). Rent → uncertain. Quantity of rentals → decreases. (Practice exam 1(b) has the mirror case: sailors arrive (D ↑) and building materials arrive (S ↑), so rent is uncertain and quantity increases.)

5 · Traps & misconceptions

Binding direction. A ceiling binds when it is below P*, a floor when it is above P*. "A ceiling above equilibrium" does nothing. These are free marks.
Quantity under a binding control is the SHORT side: QS for a ceiling, QD for a floor. It is never QD under a ceiling, and the exam lists it as a distractor.
Black-market rent is read off the demand curve at QS, not off supply and not the old equilibrium price.
Re-check binding after a shift. In the practice exam the $500 ceiling is ineffective under D0 but effective once demand shifts to D1.
Tax shifts S UP (left); subsidy shifts S DOWN (right), by exactly the per-unit amount measured vertically, not horizontally.
PS is on the ORIGINAL supply curve at the new Q (= PB − tax). Don't read PS off S+tax, and don't use the S+tax height at the old Q*.
Burden ≠ who writes the cheque. Consumers' burden = PB − P* (original), even though sellers legally remit the tax.
Tax revenue uses the NEW quantity: tax × Qtax, not tax × Q*.
Elasticity sign. The lecturer's εD is negative. If the question says "do not use the absolute value", answer −1.25, not 1.25. "Inelastic" means |ε| < 1.
Elasticity ≠ slope. One straight demand line has every elasticity from −∞ to 0. Use (P/Q) ÷ slope at the specific point. Don't use the midpoint/arc formula from QCAA. The course uses the point formula.
More elastic demand → consumers bear LESS of a tax (not more). The less elastic side always bears more.
A subsidy also creates a DWL (over-production) even though quantity rises. A shift in demand or supply never creates a DWL by itself.
Lecture slide typo (L13 slide 30): the "more elastic demand" burden box reads PB,1Tax − PMNoTax. It should be PB,2. Trust the logic, not the label.
Floor and sellers: the impact on sellers of a binding floor is ambiguous (higher price, fewer sales). A binding ceiling is unambiguously bad for sellers (landlords).

6 · Quick self-check

Use the main market unless told otherwise: D: P = 10 − Q/100, S: P = 1 + Q/200 (P* = $4, Q* = 600).

1. A rent ceiling of $5 is imposed. What happens to P, Q?

Nothing. $5 is above P* = $4, so the ceiling is non-binding / ineffective. P = $4, Q = 600.

2. Rent ceiling of $2: quantity transacted, shortage, black-market rent, DWL?

QS: 2 = 1 + Q/200 → 200 (transacted). QD: 2 = 10 − Q/100 → 800, so shortage = 600. Black-market rent = D at 200 = $8. DWL = ½ × (8 − 2) × (600 − 200) = $1200.

3. A $1.50 per-unit tax on sellers: Q, PB, PS, burdens, revenue, DWL?

10 − Q/100 = 2.5 + Q/200 → 7.5 = 3Q/200 → Q = 500. PB = $5, PS = 5 − 1.5 = $3.50. Consumers' burden $1, sellers' $0.50. Revenue = 1.5 × 500 = $750. DWL = ½ × 1.5 × 100 = $75.

4. Price elasticity of demand at Q = 300? Elastic or inelastic?

P = 10 − 3 = 7. ε = (7/300) ÷ (−1/100) = −700/300 = −7/3 ≈ −2.33, so elastic (above the midpoint Q = 500).

5. εD = −2 and the price falls 3%. What happens to QD and total revenue?

%ΔQD = −2 × (−3%) = +6%. %ΔTR ≈ −3% + 6% = +3%, so TR rises (elastic: price and TR move in opposite directions).

6. Supply is perfectly inelastic (vertical). Who bears a per-unit tax?

Sellers bear all of it. Q can't change, so PB stays at P* and PS = P* − tax. The less elastic side bears more, and here supply is completely inelastic.

7. A $1.50 per-unit subsidy to landlords: Q, PB, PS, govt cost, DWL? Who gains more?

S−sub: P = −0.5 + Q/200. Solve: 10.5 = 3Q/200 → Q = 700. PB = $3, PS = $4.50. Renters gain $1, landlords $0.50, so renters gain more (demand is the less elastic side here). Cost = 1.5 × 700 = $1050. DWL = ½ × 1.5 × 100 = $75.

8. "A price ceiling set below equilibrium will result in: (a) surplus of excess QS (b) shortage due to excess QD (c) efficient outcome (d) increase in PS"

(b) shortage due to excess quantity demanded.

9. Using the lettered tax figure in 2.3: CS after the tax? Govt revenue? DWL?

CS after = A. Revenue = B + D. DWL = C + E. (CS before was A+B+C, PS before D+E+F, PS after F.)

10. Why does the lecturer think a rental subsidy mostly benefits landlords?

Rental demand is relatively elastic (housing is a big budget share), while rental supply is inelastic in the short run (it takes time to build). The less elastic side (landlords) captures the larger share of the subsidy's gain.

Definitions
  • Price control: legally binding restriction on price. Ceiling = max (rent cap/freeze). Floor = min (min wage $26.44/hr; employers = buyers of labour).
  • Binding = constrains the price ("effective"). Non-binding has no economic impact.
  • Unit tax: $ per unit (tobacco excise $1.53/cigarette). Analytically a negative subsidy.
  • Subsidy: govt payment to encourage production/consumption. Unit = $ per unit (course uses this). Ad-valorem = % of price. Paying it to sellers or buyers gives the same market outcome.
  • Quota: cap on quantity, so S becomes vertical at the quota.
  • CS = area under D, above PB, up to Q. PS = area above S, below PS, up to Q.
  • SS = CS + PS (+ tax rev − subsidy cost) = total benefit (area under D) − production cost (area under S).
  • DWL = SS lost vs competitive Q*. Comes from under- OR over-production.

Elasticity (lecturer's method)
  • εD = %ΔQD/%ΔP, negative. Use |εD| only if told.
  • Point formula: εD = (P/Q) ÷ (ΔP/ΔQ) = slope of ray ÷ slope of D = (1/slope)·(P/Q).
  • Linear D: slope = −(P-int)/(Q-int). Midpoint → ε = −1, MR = 0, TR max.
  • %ΔQD = εD × %ΔP. %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ.
  • Elastic: P ↓ gives TR ↑. Inelastic: P ↓ gives TR ↓.
  • Vertical = perfectly inelastic (0). Horizontal = perfectly elastic (∞).
  • More elastic D: more substitutes, longer time, luxury, big budget share.
  • εS = %ΔQS/%ΔP > 0 ("don't worry about calculating"). Housing S is inelastic in the short run.
  • Examples: (Q 6, P 8), slope −2 → (8/6)/(−2) = −2/3. (Q 8, P 12), slope −1 → −3/2. (Q 36, P 64), slope −1 → −16/9.
Incidence rule: the LESS elastic side bears MORE of a tax and gets MORE of a subsidy. Perfectly inelastic side bears it all. Check: buyers' share ≈ εS/(εS + |εD|) (textbook, not on slides).

Rent-control unintended consequences: black-market renting, illegal rent-bidding, non-price discrimination (no students/pets/kids/immigrants), lower maintenance and quality, landlords exit in the long run (S ↓ further). Subsidy (NRAS): lower rent + more units, but costs the budget (opportunity cost = other programs) and landlords capture most because rental S is inelastic and D more elastic. ↑ Supply: zoning/density, HAFF, Housing Accord. It fixes the root cause slowly; NIMBY, interest rates, materials and labour shortages get in the way. Neg. gearing removal: housing-market D ↓ so prices ↓; rental S ↓ and D ↓ so rent is ambiguous.

Mini procedures

Ceiling: 1. P*, Q* (current D). 2. Ceiling < P*? If no, ineffective. 3. QS, QD at the ceiling. 4. Q traded = QS. Shortage = QD − QS. 5. Black-market price = D(QS). 6. DWL = ½(D(QS) − ceiling)(Q* − QS).
Floor: 1. Floor > P*? 2. QD, QS at the floor. 3. Q traded = QD. Excess S = QS − QD. 4. DWL = ½(floor − S(QD))(Q* − QD).
Tax: 1. S+tax = S up by tax (equation: add tax to the intercept). 2. D ∩ S+tax gives Qt, PB = PM. 3. PS = PB − tax. 4. Burdens: PB − P*, P* − PS. 5. Rev = tax·Qt. 6. DWL = ½·tax·(Q* − Qt).
Subsidy: 1. S−sub = S down by sub. 2. D ∩ S−sub gives Qs, PB. 3. PS = PB + sub. 4. Gains: P* − PB, PS − P*. 5. Cost = sub·Qs. 6. DWL = ½·sub·(Qs − Q*).
Elasticity at a point: 1. Read (Q, P). 2. Slope of D = ΔP/ΔQ (negative). 3. ε = (P/Q)/slope. 4. Keep the sign and simplify to a fraction. 5. Classify by |ε|.
Equilibrium from equations: set PD(Q) = PS(Q) and solve for Q, then substitute back for P. Check on both curves.
Q under a ceiling = QS (not QD). Black market = demand height. PS is on the ORIGINAL S. Revenue uses NEW Q. εD is negative, uses the point formula (not midpoint), and is not the slope.

Section 1 · Bubble tea: demand, supply & a per-unit tax (14 marks)

Stem: This question concerns the market for bubble teas in City B. The demand-and-supply diagram of the market is given below (to scale; minor gridlines every 100 units and every $1).

Q1 · Equilibrium off the grid 2 marks

According to the demand-and-supply diagram, the competitive equilibrium price is $[ ] and the competitive equilibrium quantity is [ ].

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Price = $10, quantity = 700.

Method (T1 M1): find where D and S cross, then read across to the price axis and down to the quantity axis. Check with the endpoints: D falls $16 over 800 units ($0.02 per unit), so from (200, 20) to Q = 700 it falls 500 × 0.02 = $10, giving $10 ✓.

The quantity axis is labelled only at 0, 500 and 1000. Count the minor gridlines (100 each), not the major ones.

Q2 · Price of a substitute rises 5 marks

Suppose, due to an extreme weather event, there is a poor harvest of coffee beans, leading to an increase in the price of coffee. (The production cost of bubble teas is unaffected by the weather event.) Since coffees and bubble teas are [ ], the [ ] curve in the bubble tea market will [ ]. The equilibrium price of bubble teas will [ ] and the equilibrium quantity of bubble teas will [ ].

Options: [complements in consumption / substitutes in consumption / derived demand of each other] · [demand / supply] · [shift to the left / shift to the right / remain unchanged / be indeterminable] · price and quantity: [increase / decrease / remain unchanged / become uncertain]

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

substitutes in consumption · demand · shift to the right · price increase · quantity increase.

L13: coffee is dearer, so people buy less coffee and more bubble tea, and the demand for bubble tea rises (D1 → D2). Supply is unchanged, so P1 → P2 ↑ and Q1 → Q2 ↑ (T1 M4).

The bracket "(production cost of bubble teas is unaffected)" tells you supply does not move. Coffee beans are not an input to bubble tea, so don't pick "derived demand" and don't shift S.

Q3 · Label supply with and without tax 2 marks

Drag "Supply without tax" and "Supply with tax" onto the two red lines.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Upper line = Supply with tax. Lower line = Supply without tax.

Q4 · Read P_B, P_S, Q and the consumers' burden 4 marks

According to the diagram you have just labelled, after the tax is imposed, the price paid by consumers is [P1 / P2 / P3 / P4], while the price received by producers is [P1 / P2 / P3 / P4]. Quantity transacted is [Q1 / Q2 / Q3 / Q4]. The consumers' tax burden is equal to [P1 minus P3 / P2 minus P3 / P2 minus P4 / P3 minus P4] per unit of the good consumed.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Consumers pay P2 · producers receive P4 · quantity Q2 · consumers' burden = P2 minus P3.

  1. New equilibrium is where D meets the with-tax supply: (Q2, P2). Buyers pay PB = PM = P2.
  2. Sellers get PS = PM − tax: drop straight down from (Q2, P2) to the no-tax supply line, which gives P4.
  3. Original (no-tax) price was P3 (D meets the no-tax supply at Q3). Consumers' burden = increase in the buyers' price = P2 − P3. (Sellers' burden = P3 − P4; and P2 − P4 = the whole tax.)
P1 and Q4 are distractors. P1 = P3 + tax (the with-tax supply at Q3) and Q4 is the quantity demanded at P4. Neither is an equilibrium. "P2 minus P4" is the whole tax, not the consumers' share.

Method: T2 M3.

○ Consumers will bear a larger share of the tax burden. ○ Consumers will bear a smaller share of the tax burden. ○ The consumers will bear the same share of the tax burden. ○ There is not enough information to determine.

Consumers will bear a smaller share of the tax burden.

Section 2 · Mario's Fire Flower monopoly (11 marks)

Stem: Mario is the monopolist in the Fire Flower market. The demand, marginal revenue and marginal cost curves in this market are given by the diagram below.

Q6 · Monopoly Q, P, mark-up, under-production 5 marks

According to the diagram, the monopolist will choose a quantity of [ ] and charge a price of $[ ]. The monopolist enjoys a mark-up of [ ]. Comparing to a perfectly competitive market, the monopolist [overproduces / underproduces / differentiatedly produces / advantageously produces] by [ ] units.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Q = 36 · P = $64 · mark-up = 36 · underproduces by 24 units.

  1. Profit max: MR = MC (marginal profit MΠ = MR − MC = 0). MR crosses MC at Q = 36 (where MC = 28).
  2. Price: go up from Q = 36 to the demand curve: P = $64.
  3. Mark-up = P − MC = 64 − 28 = 36 (L13 slide 35).
  4. Competitive market supply = MC, so the competitive equilibrium is D = MC at Q = 60 (P = $40). Monopoly underproduces by 60 − 36 = 24.
Don't read the price off MR or MC (28 is the MC at Q = 36, not the price). Note that the mark-up 36 equals the quantity 36 here by coincidence, so don't let that throw you.

Method: T3 M1.

Q7 · Click the deadweight loss 1 mark

Click in the area representing the deadweight loss due to monopoly power. (Same diagram.)

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Click inside the triangle between the demand curve and MC, from Q = 36 to Q = 60, with corners (36, 64), (60, 40) and (36, 28). L13 labels it D+E.

L13 slide 40: monopoly social surplus = A+B. Competitive social surplus = A+B+D+E. So DWL = D+E, the lost units 36→60 for which the benefit (D) exceeds the cost (MC). Numerically ½ × (64 − 28) × (60 − 36) = ½ × 36 × 24 = 432 (not asked).

The DWL triangle is bounded by MC underneath, not MR. Don't click the region between MR and MC.

Q8 · Elasticity at the monopoly price 3 marks

Calculate the price elasticity of demand at the monopoly price. You can leave your answer in fractions.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

εD = −16/9 = −1 7/9 ≈ −1.78 (elastic).

L13 slide 41 (T3 M4): εD = (1/slope) × (P/QD).

  1. Slope of demand = ΔP/ΔQ = 100/(−100) = −1.
  2. Ray from origin at the monopoly point: P/Q = 64/36 = 16/9.
  3. εD = (1/−1) × 16/9 = −16/9.
Evaluate at the monopoly point (36, 64), not at the competitive point (60, 40), which would give −2/3. The lecturer keeps the minus sign.

Q9 · Luigi: is demand elastic at the monopoly price? 2 marks

Mario's brother, Luigi, is the monopolist of the Ice Flower market, a market that is different from and independent of the Fire Flower market. Luigi wonders if the demand for Ice Flower is elastic at the monopoly price he charges. Can you answer him with the information given? If yes, explain how you determine the answer. If no, state what further information you would need to determine your answer.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Yes: demand is elastic (|εD| > 1) at any profit-maximising monopoly price.

Model answer (L13 slide 42):

  • When demand is inelastic (|εD| < 1), marginal revenue is negative (MR < 0).
  • Firms, monopolies included, face non-negative marginal cost (MC ≥ 0).
  • A profit-maximising firm never produces where MR < MC, because then marginal profit is negative and it could raise profit by cutting output and raising price. So the monopolist always operates on the elastic part of demand.
Item 8 confirms it: −16/9 has absolute value greater than 1.

Q11 · Above or below potential; which monetary policy? 2 marks

At the equilibrium point you identified, Wonderland's real output is [above / exactly at / below / not comparable to] its potential GDP. Which of the following is a monetary policy that can return Wonderland's real output to its potential GDP level?

○ A reduction in the interest rate. ○ A reduction in income tax. ○ A reduction in money supply. ○ A reduction in government purchases.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Above potential · A reduction in money supply.

  • YNow is to the right of LRAS (YPot), so output is above potential. That calls for contractionary policy: AD must shift left.
  • Money supply ↓ ⇒ interest rate ↑ ⇒ C ↓ and I ↓ ⇒ AD ↓ (L13 slide 52–53). ✔ monetary and contractionary.
  • Income tax ↓ and government purchases ↓ are fiscal policy (lecturer circled them out). An interest rate cut is monetary but expansionary (AD ↑), the wrong direction.
Two filters: (1) the right type (monetary vs fiscal), (2) the right direction. "Reduction in government purchases" goes in the right direction but is fiscal.

Q12 · Income tax increase 4 marks

The government in Wonderland is contemplating an increase in income tax. If it implements this policy, Wonderland's [SRAS / AD / LRAS] will [shift to the left / shift to the right / remain unchanged / become uncertain], leading to [an increase in / a decrease in / no change in / an uncertain effect on] price level and [an increase in / a decrease in / no change in / an uncertain effect on] real output.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

AD · shift to the left · a decrease in price level · a decrease in real output.

L13 slide 55–56: net taxes T ↑ ⇒ disposable income ↓ ⇒ consumption C ↓ ⇒ AD shifts left. Along an upward-sloping SRAS, both P and Y fall.

Q13 · Middle East war: oil shock + confidence shock 8 marks

Recently, a war broke out in the Middle East. Wonderland imports most of its crude oil-derived products (e.g., diesel, petrol), and uses them in production activities. Meanwhile, consumers and investors in Wonderland are losing confidence in Wonderland's economic prospects due to the war.

As the price of crude oil spikes due to the war in the Middle East, Wonderland will experience a(n) [increase in AD / decrease in AD / increase in SRAS / decrease in SRAS]. Meanwhile, as consumers and investors delay their purchases, Wonderland will experience a(n) [same options]. Combining the two effects, the war in the Middle East will [lead to a higher / lead to a lower / have no effect on / have an uncertain effect on] real output and will [same options] price level in Wonderland.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Decrease in SRAS · decrease in AD · lead to a lower real output · have an uncertain effect on price level.

  • Oil is a production input, so input costs rise and SRAS shifts left (P ↑, Y ↓).
  • Delayed C and I mean AD shifts left (P ↓, Y ↓).
  • Both push Y down, so Y falls for sure. They push P in opposite directions, so ΔP is uncertain and depends on which shift is bigger (L13 slide 58).
Rule (T4 M2): with two shifts, the variable they move in the same direction is certain and the other is ambiguous. AD↓ + SRAS↓ ⇒ Y↓ certain, P ?

Q14 · Written: does the war cause inflation? 4 marks

Suppose Wonderland's government asks you to evaluate whether the war in the Middle East causes inflation. If you know that Wonderland relies heavily on petroleum products (i.e., products derived from crude oil) for domestic production, while the consumers' and investors' confidence were not severely impacted, what would your answer likely be? Would your answer be different if Wonderland has largely transited to renewable energy, but Wonderland's citizens were very worried about their exports being impacted by the war? Explain your answer briefly.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Scenario 1: likely inflation. Scenario 2: yes, the answer changes. The price level likely falls (deflation).

Model answer (L13 slide 59):

  • Heavy oil reliance, confidence not badly hit: the SRAS decrease is large (big upward pressure on prices) and the AD decrease is small (small downward pressure). The SRAS effect dominates, so the price level will likely rise (inflation).
  • Mostly renewables, confidence seriously damaged (worried about exports, so NX and C/I fall): the SRAS decrease is minimal and the AD decrease is large. The AD effect dominates, so the price level will likely fall (deflation).
Structure for full marks: name both shifts, say which is bigger in each scenario and why, then state the net effect on P.

Q15 · Absolute and comparative advantage 4 marks

Which two (2) of the following statements about absolute advantage are correct? ☐ Country A has an absolute advantage in producing Bread. ☐ Country A has an absolute advantage in producing Sausages. ☐ Country B has an absolute advantage in producing Bread. ☐ Country B has an absolute advantage in producing Sausages.

Which two (2) of the following statements about comparative advantage are correct? (Same four statements with "comparative".)

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Absolute: A in Bread ✔ and A in Sausages ✔. Comparative: A in Bread ✔ and B in Sausages ✔.

  • Absolute advantage = fewer inputs per unit. A needs 1 < 4 hours for bread and 4 < 6 hours for sausages, so A wins both.
  • Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost. Bread: A 0.25 < B 0.67 Sausages → A. Sausages: B 1.5 < A 4 Bread → B.
Absolute advantage is per-unit hours; it does not depend on total hours (100 vs 120). Each country always has a comparative advantage in exactly one good (unless OCs are equal).

Q18 · Bundles vs PPC and CPC 4 marks

Suppose the two countries agree to the terms of trade of 1 Sausage for 3 Bread. Suppose further that each country specialises completely in producing the good it has a comparative advantage on.

The consumption bundle (70 Bread, 10 Sausages) is [above / on / below / uncertain relative to] Country A's Production Possibility Curve (PPC) and [above / on / below / uncertain relative to] its Consumption Possibility Curve (CPC).

Meanwhile, the consumption bundle (30 Bread, 5 Sausages) is [above / on / below / uncertain relative to] Country B's PPC and [same options] its CPC.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

(70, 10): above A's PPC, on A's CPC. (30, 5): above B's PPC, below B's CPC.

  1. A's CPC: A specialises in Bread (100 Bread) and trades at 3 Bread per Sausage, so QBread = 100 − 3QSausage. At 10 Sausages: 100 − 30 = 70 Bread, so the bundle is exactly ON the CPC.
  2. A's PPC: Bread + 4 × Sausages ≤ 100 hours. 70 + 40 = 110 > 100, so the bundle is ABOVE the PPC (unreachable without trade).
  3. B's CPC: B specialises in Sausages (20) and trades at 3 Bread per Sausage, so QSausage = 20 − ⅓QBread. At 30 Bread: 20 − 10 = 10 Sausages > 5, so the bundle is BELOW the CPC.
  4. B's PPC: 4 × Bread + 6 × Sausages ≤ 120. 4(30) + 6(5) = 150 > 120, so the bundle is ABOVE the PPC.

L13 slides 73–77 give the CPC answers explicitly. The PPC answers come from the slide-71 plot (both bundles sit outside their PPCs), checked with the hours constraint above. Method: T5 M4.

Build the CPC from the specialised point (A: 100 Bread; B: 20 Sausages), not from the other axis. B's CPC hits the Bread axis at 20 × 3 = 60.

Section 6 · Flu vaccination externality & a pandemic game (21 marks)

Stem: Flu vaccination generates an externality. Not only does the vaccinated person gain some health benefits, people who come into contact with a vaccinated person also derive benefits through reduced chance of infection. The following diagram depicts the private marginal benefit, social marginal benefit, and marginal cost associated with flu vaccination. (Note: the diagram is not drawn to scale.)

Q21 · Type of externality and the two quantities 3 marks

According to the diagram, flu vaccination generates a [positive / negative] externality. The competitive equilibrium quantity in this market is [ ]. Meanwhile, the socially optimal quantity is [ ].

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Positive · 350 · 600.

  • SMB lies above PMB: third parties benefit, so the externality is positive.
  • The market uses private demand (PMB) and supply (MC): they cross at Q = 350 (at $12).
  • The socially optimal Q is where SMB = MC: Q = 600 (at $20).
225 is a distractor (where MC = $8, the PMB value at 600). Positive externality ⇒ the market under-produces.

Q22 · Tax or subsidy, and how much 4 marks

Suppose the government would like to correct the externality. Should it impose a per-unit tax or a per-unit subsidy? Calculate the per-unit tax or subsidy required. Show your working.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

A per-unit subsidy of $12 (= $20 − $8).

Model answer (L13 slide 89):

  • The government should provide a per-unit subsidy, because consumers need to internalise the external benefit.
  • A subsidy shifts the private demand (PMB) curve up by the size of the subsidy. We need the new private demand to cross MC at Q* = 600, where MC = SMB = $20.
  • At 600 consumers are willing to pay only PMB = $8, so subsidy = 20 − 8 = $12, the marginal external benefit at the socially optimal quantity.
MEB is not constant here: at Q = 350 the gap is 25 − 12 = $13. The subsidy is the gap at Q* = 600, so $12, not $13.

Q23 · DWL vs administrative cost 4 marks

It turns out that there are some administrative costs of correcting the externality. The government would like to implement the policy you identified in (b) only if the deadweight loss from the externality is bigger than the administrative cost of $1500. Calculate the deadweight loss associated with the externality of flu vaccination. Show your working. Would the government like to implement the policy?

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

DWL = ½ × (25 − 12) × (600 − 350) = ½ × 13 × 250 = $1625 > $1500 ⇒ yes, implement the subsidy.

  • At Q = 350 total surplus = Red (benefit − cost). At Q = 600 it is Red + Blue. The lost surplus is the Blue triangle between SMB and MC from 350 to 600 (L13 slide 90).
  • Height at Q = 350: SMB − MC = 25 − 12 = 13. Base = 600 − 350 = 250. DWL = ½ × 13 × 250 = 1625.
  • Since 1625 > 1500, the government would implement it.

L13 gives the formula (25 − 12) × (600 − 350) / 2; the arithmetic, 1625, was verified separately. Method: T6 DWL method.

The triangle's vertical side is at the competitive Q (350), using SMB − MC = 13. Using PMB or the gap at 600 (= 12) gives 1500, exactly the threshold, and the wrong conclusion.

Q24 · Does A have a dominant strategy? 4 marks

Does Country A have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

No.

  • If B chooses Contribute: A gets 3 (Contribute) vs 2 (Withdraw), so A's best response is Contribute.
  • If B chooses Withdraw: A gets 0 vs 1, so A's best response is Withdraw.
  • A's best response depends on B's action; no single action is always a best response, so A has no dominant strategy (L13 slide 92).
Show both comparisons with the actual payoff numbers. That is where the 4 marks are.

Q25 · Does B have a dominant strategy? 4 marks

Refer to the same payoff matrix. Does Country B have a dominant strategy? Explain your answer.

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

Yes: Withdraw.

  • If A chooses Contribute: B gets 3 (Contribute) vs 4 (Withdraw), so B's best response is Withdraw.
  • If A chooses Withdraw: B gets 0 vs 2, so B's best response is Withdraw.
  • Withdraw is a best response whatever A does, so it is B's dominant strategy (L13 slide 93).

Q26 · Nash equilibrium 2 marks

The Nash Equilibrium/Equilibria of this game is/are: ○ (Contribute, Contribute) ○ (Contribute, Withdraw) ○ (Withdraw, Withdraw) ○ (Contribute, Contribute) and (Withdraw, Withdraw)

Answer & working✅ official (L13)

(Withdraw, Withdraw), payoffs (1, 2).

B always withdraws (dominant strategy). A's best response to Withdraw is Withdraw (1 > 0). Mark best responses with stars: only the (Withdraw, Withdraw) cell has both. (Contribute, Contribute) is better for both players but is not a NE, because B would deviate to Withdraw (4 > 3).

Convention: (row player A's action, column player B's action). "(Contribute, Withdraw)" means A contributes and B withdraws.

Q27 · Click the steady state 2 marks

Click on the intersection that determines the steady-state capital-labour ratio.

Answer & working🟢 confident (not covered in L13)

Click where the saving line sf(k) crosses the maintenance line (n+δ)k.

At the steady state, investment per worker exactly covers depreciation plus equipping new workers, so Δk = sf(k) − (n+δ)k = 0.

Distractors: where f(k) crosses (n+δ)k (top right, irrelevant), and the tangent point (that is the Golden Rule, not the steady state).

Q28 · Saving vs the Golden Rule 2 marks

According to the diagram in (a) above, this country is: ○ saving too much relative to its Golden Rule saving rate. ○ saving too little relative to its Golden Rule saving rate. ○ saving exactly at its Golden Rule saving rate. ○ oscillating between saving above and saving below its Golden Rule saving rate.

Answer & working🟢 confident (not covered in L13)

Saving too much relative to its Golden Rule saving rate.

  • The Golden Rule kG is where the slope of f(k) equals n + δ, i.e. where the dashed tangent parallel to (n+δ)k touches f(k). That maximises steady-state consumption f(k) − (n+δ)k.
  • In the diagram the actual steady state k* (sf ∩ maintenance) lies to the right of kG (the dashed vertical line). k* > kG ⇒ s > sG ⇒ saving too much. Saving less would raise steady-state consumption.

🟢 rather than ✅ because L13 skipped it. The position of the dashed tangent (left of the sf ∩ (n+δ)k crossing) is clear on the exam image.

Q29 · Fertility falls (n ↓) 6 marks

Suppose the fertility rate in this country falls, that is, n decreases. This will shift the [Tangent to the output line / Per capita output line / Maintenance line / Saving line] [up / down / first up then down / first down then up]. Comparing to the original steady state, the new steady-state capital-labour ratio will be [higher / lower / the same as / uncertain].

Answer & working🟢 confident (not covered in L13)

Maintenance line · down · higher.

  • n appears only in (n+δ)k, so a lower n makes the maintenance line flatter, i.e. it pivots down (T7 convention: n ↓ ⇒ "down").
  • sf(k) is unchanged, so it now meets the flatter line further right ⇒ new k* is higher (see the green dot in the item 27 figure).
Not the "tangent to the output line": the Golden Rule tangent also gets flatter, but the question is about what n directly shifts. Neither f(k) nor sf(k) contains n.

What this paper teaches

  1. One section per topic, always in lecture order: D&S/tax → monopoly → AD-AS → comparative advantage → tariff → externality + game → Solow. Tab your binder in that order.
  2. Read grids by counting minor gridlines. Axes are labelled sparsely (0/500/1000). The equilibrium always lands on a gridline crossing.
  3. Tax diagrams with P1–P4 / Q1–Q4: new eqm = D ∩ Stax (P2, Q2); PS = drop to Sno tax (P4); consumers' burden = PB − old price (P2 − P3). P1 and Q4 are always distractors.
  4. Monopoly grid package: MR = MC → Q, go up to D → P, mark-up = P − MC, compare with D = MC, DWL triangle D+E (bounded by MC, not MR), elasticity = (1/slope) × P/Q at the monopoly point, plus the written "monopolist is always on the elastic part" argument (MR < 0 when inelastic, MC ≥ 0).
  5. Macro dropdowns: classify the event (input cost → SRAS; confidence/taxes/G/money → AD), and remember monetary ≠ fiscal. With two shocks, the variable both move the same way is certain and the other is "uncertain". The written follow-up wants "which shift is bigger and why" in each scenario.
  6. Comparative advantage chain: OC table (hours ÷ hours) → absolute (fewer hours) vs comparative (lower OC) → ToT window (between the two OCs of the same good, here 1.5–4 Bread per Sausage) → CPC from the specialised intercept at the ToT → bundle checks by plugging into the PPC hours constraint and the CPC equation.
  7. Tariff with a falling world price (large country): imports use P1+t; the welfare baseline is the old price P0; revenue = E+I; net = I − (D+F) ("gains iff the terms-of-trade gain beats the two DWL triangles").
  8. Positive externality: subsidy = SMB − PMB at Q* (here $12, not the $13 gap at Qc). DWL triangle has its vertical side at Qc. Compare it with any admin-cost threshold, which is built so that the wrong height lands exactly on the threshold.
  9. Payoff matrix written answers: state each best response with numbers, then conclude dominant or not. NE = cell where both best-respond. Write NE as (row, column).
  10. Solow: SS = sf(k) ∩ (n+δ)k. Golden Rule = the tangent parallel to (n+δ)k. k* right of kG ⇒ saving too much. n or δ changes → maintenance line; s → saving line; A → both f and sf.

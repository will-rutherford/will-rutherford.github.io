The big idea

Part 1 (L10): When you burn coal you don't pay for the damage your CO₂ does to everyone else. That unpaid cost is a negative externality. Because buyers and sellers only look at private benefits and costs, the competitive market produces too much (QMarket > QEff) and there is a deadweight loss. Fix: a Pigouvian tax t = MEC (measured at QEff) shifts supply up onto the MSC curve so the market lands on the efficient quantity.

Part 2 (L11): Cutting emissions is the flip side — a positive externality: the cutting country pays the cost, the whole world gets the benefit. So each country is tempted to free-ride. Game theory shows this as a Prisoner's Dilemma: both countries' dominant strategy is "Status quo", so the Nash equilibrium is (Status quo, Status quo) even though (Reduce, Reduce) is better for both. Changing payoffs (name & shame, domestic political pressure) can turn it into a coordination game or even make "Reduce" dominant.

Lecture story: IPCC AR6 — "we are cooked"; Stern Review (2006): "Climate change is the greatest market failure the world has ever seen"; Australia's carbon tax (2012–2014); Kyoto Protocol failed (no punishment for cheating) → Paris Agreement (pledge & review, name & shame, ratcheting NDCs).

1 · What is an externality?

An externality is a cost or benefit that affects a third party (someone not directly involved) in the production or consumption of a good. It arises when a decision-maker does not bear the full cost or enjoy the full benefit of their action.

Externalities can occur in production or consumption. A market failure = a competitive market over- or under-produces relative to the socially optimal quantity. Typical causes (L10): externalities, monopoly (price-searchers), asymmetric information. Note: "market fails, government fails too" — intervention isn't automatically the answer.

You met externalities/market failure in QCAA. Same ideas — but here you must read exact numbers off a graph (QMarket, QEff, tax size, DWL triangle) and use the lecturer's labels (MPC, MEC, MSC, MB/MPB, MSB, MEB).

2 · Private, external and social cost: MSC = MPC + MEC

Total Social Cost = Total Private Cost + Total External Cost: TSC = TPC + TEC. Per extra unit: MSC = MPC + MEC (ΔTSC/ΔQ = ΔTPC/ΔQ + ΔTEC/ΔQ). MPC is just the ordinary "MC" from earlier lectures, and the supply curve is the MPC curve. Totals build up from marginals: TC(Q) = TC(Q−1) + MC(Q).

4 · Pigouvian (corrective) tax — "make polluters internalise the external cost"

Arthur Pigou's idea (also spelt Pigovian): tax the decision-maker so private cost = social cost. "Sin taxes" (tobacco) are Pigouvian in nature; the Pigou Club backs a carbon tax.

Set t = MEC. Then the sellers' cost becomes MPC₁ = MPC₀ + t = MSC, so the supply curve shifts up onto the MSC curve ("decrease in supply"). New market equilibrium = Demand ∩ Supply₁ = MSC ∩ MSB → output falls to QEff, no deadweight loss.

When MEC changes with output (SMC and PMC not parallel): the right tax is the per-unit tax that shifts supply enough to cross demand at QEff — i.e. t = MEC(QEff) = MSC(QEff) − MPC(QEff). Not the gap at QMarket!

Real world (L10): Gillard government carbon tax 2012 — $20 per tonne CO₂; ANU 2014 study: cut emissions by 17 million tonnes; repealed by Abbott government 2014. Practical problems: (1) can MEC be measured accurately (what is the right tax)? (2) politically, how do you get the current generation to pay for future generations' benefit?

5 · Positive externality and the Pigouvian subsidy

L10 says "see next week's tutorial"; the S1 2026 final (flu vaccination, L13 Q6) examined it. Consumers ignore the benefit to others, so Demand = MPB sits below MSB = MPB + MEB. Supply = MC (no cost externality).

  • Market: MPB = MC → QMarket. Social optimum: MSB = MC → Q* (bigger). Under-production.
  • Subsidy = MEB at Q* = MSB(Q*) − MPB(Q*). The subsidy shifts the private demand curve up by the subsidy: MPBSub = MPB₀ + Sub. (L13: "if consumers were willing to pay $8 for the 600th unit, a $12 subsidy makes them willing to pay $20, since out-of-pocket stays $20 − $12 = $8".)
  • DWL = triangle between MSB and MC from QMarket to Q*: ½ × (MSB(QMarket) − MC(QMarket)) × (Q* − QMarket). L13 used exactly this: (25 − 12) × (600 − 350) / 2.

"Is it worth it?" add-on (L13 style): if the policy has an administrative cost, implement it only if DWL > admin cost. Here: implement if admin cost < $360.

6 · Cap-and-trade and Coase — not in the 2026 lectures

L10 and L11 cover only Pigouvian taxes/subsidies as the fix. Tradable permits (cap-and-trade) and the Coase theorem are not lectured this semester (Coase appears only in a pre-2024 past paper, which the lecturer says to disregard). Low priority — background only:
  • Cap-and-trade: government caps total emissions at QEff and issues that many permits; firms trade them. The permit price ends up = MEC at QEff — same outcome as the Pigouvian tax, but fixes quantity (tax fixes price).
  • Coase theorem: if property rights are clear and bargaining (transaction) costs are low, private parties can negotiate to the efficient outcome without government. Fails for climate change (billions of parties, future generations can't bargain).

8 · Game theory basics (L11)

Why a new tool? A competitive market assumes participants have no clout. Countries negotiating emissions clearly do (China + India + US ≈ 50% of CO₂ in 2020), so we need a model of strategic interaction: my payoff depends on my choice and others' choices.

Every game has: 1. Players  2. Actions  3. Payoffs (for every combination of actions = outcome). Goal: predict the outcome, assuming each player chooses their best option given others' choices. Course scope: simultaneous-move 2 × 2 games.

Payoff-matrix convention (memorise): Player 1 controls the rows; Player 2 controls the columns. Each cell is an outcome. First number = Row player's payoff, second number = Column player's payoff. An outcome is named by actions in the order (Row's action, Column's action) — never by the payoffs: say "(Status quo, Status quo)", not "(0, 0)".

The GHG abatement game (lecture): Reducing costs a country 5. Both reduce → each gets benefit 8 → 8 − 5 = 3. Only one reduces → both get benefit 4; reducer nets 4 − 5 = −1, free-rider gets 4. Neither → 0.

9 · Best response → dominant strategy

Best response: the action giving a player the highest payoff given the opponent's action. Mark it with an asterisk next to that player's payoff (lecture's tip).

Dominant strategy: an action that is always a best response, to every one of the opponent's actions — the highest payoff no matter what the opponent does. If a player has one, we expect them to play it.

In the abatement game: if B Reduces, A gets 3 (Reduce) vs 4 (Status quo) → SQ*; if B plays SQ, A gets −1 vs 0 → SQ*. Status quo is A's best response both times → A's dominant strategy = Status quo. Same for B (compare 3 vs 4, −1 vs 0). Prediction: (Status quo, Status quo).

10 · The Prisoner's Dilemma and free-riding

(SQ, SQ) gives 0 each, but (Reduce, Reduce) would give 3 each — not efficient. Dilemma: individual rationality (play your best response) vs joint rationality (pick the outcome best for all).

Prisoner's Dilemma structure (numbers can differ): (1) both players have a dominant strategy; (2) when both play it, the outcome is collectively inefficient (another cell makes both better off).

Source of the inefficiency here: free-riding — GHG abatement is a positive externality (you pay the cost, I enjoy the mitigation). Common whenever individuals pay to contribute to a public good (group assignments!). Criminals escape the PD with punishment (Omertà); Kyoto had no punishment for withdrawing, overshooting targets, or developing-country emission growth — so it stayed a PD.

11 · Nash equilibrium & multiple equilibria (name and shame)

Nash equilibrium = an outcome (combination of actions) in which all players are playing their best responses to the other players' chosen actions. Nobody wants to change their mind once they know what the other does → self-enforcing. In a matrix: a cell where BOTH payoffs have a star.

Paris "name and shame": cheating when the other reduces now pays only 2 (was 4). Everything else unchanged ("if everybody cheats, there's no shame").

Country B

Country AReduce3*, 3*−1, 2

A: vs Reduce → 3 > 2 Reduce*; vs Cheat → 0 > −1 Cheat*. Different answers → no dominant strategy (same for B). But two Nash equilibria: (Reduce, Reduce) and (Cheat, Cheat). This is a coordination problem: if countries trust each other they can coordinate on the good one; once there, it's self-enforcing. Paris ratcheting (NDCs updated every 5 years after observing others) is a coordination device.

12 · Domestic political pressure → dominant strategy → iterated elimination

Suppose only Country A's government is punished by voters for cheating (whatever B does): A's Cheat payoffs fall to 0 and −2. B's payoffs unchanged.

Country B
ReduceCheat
Country AReduce3*, 3*−1*, 2
Cheat0, −1−2, 0*

A: 3 > 0 and −1 > −2 → Reduce is now A's dominant strategy. B still has none. B expects A to Reduce → B's best response to Reduce is Reduce (3 > 2). Outcome (Reduce, Reduce), the unique Nash equilibrium. Contagion of political pressure: A's internal pressure changes B's choice.

Iterative elimination of dominated strategies (IEDS): delete a player's dominated action (here A's Cheat), then find the other player's best response to what's left. The lecture calls this a "special case" and notes it leads to a Nash equilibrium. Real-world reading: when A has a dominant Reduce, it stays in the agreement even if B withdraws (US left Kyoto → others followed; US left Paris → others stayed).

Step-by-step methods

M1 · Read an externality diagram (dropdown + numeric)

  1. Type: the "social" curve is on the cost side (SMC/MSC above PMC/MPC) → negative. On the benefit side (SMB/MSB above PMB/MPB) → positive.
  2. Competitive market Q: where the two private curves cross: MB(=MPB) ∩ PMC (negative), or MPB ∩ MC (positive). Ignore the social curve.
  3. Socially optimal Q: where the social curve crosses the other curve: MB ∩ SMC (negative), or SMB ∩ MC (positive).
  4. Check direction: negative ⇒ optimal < market. Positive ⇒ optimal > market.

M2 · Pigouvian tax / subsidy amount

  1. Go to the socially optimal Q (not the market Q).
  2. Read the two cost (or benefit) curves straight above it.
  3. Tax = SMC(QEff) − PMC(QEff)  |  Subsidy = SMB(Q*) − PMB(Q*).
  4. Dropdown choice: tax for negative, subsidy for positive. Never "enforce a price".
  5. If the curves are parallel the gap is the same everywhere — any Q gives the same answer. If not parallel, the gap at QMarket is a distractor.

M3 · Deadweight loss of an externality

  1. Locate QMarket and QEff (M1).
  2. Triangle's apex is the social optimum point; its vertical side is at QMarket.
  3. Height = vertical gap at QMarket between the social curve and the other curve: negative → SMC(QMkt) − MB(QMkt); positive → SMB(QMkt) − MC(QMkt).
  4. Base = |QMarket − QEff|.
  5. DWL = ½ × height × base. Show working (written questions say "no marks if working not shown").
  6. Optional add-on: correct only if DWL > administrative cost.

M4 · Equations instead of a graph

  1. Market: set MB = MPC, solve QMarket; PMarket = MB(QMarket).
  2. Build MSC = MPC + MEC (add MEC to the intercept if constant). Solve MB = MSC → QEff.
  3. Tax = MEC(QEff). With the tax: PB = MB(QEff), PS = PB − t.
  4. DWL = ½ × [MSC(QMarket) − MB(QMarket)] × (QMarket − QEff).
  5. TEC at a quantity Q (constant MEC) = MEC × Q.

M5 · Lettered-area questions (negative externality, L10 labels)

  1. TPC = area under MPC up to Q. TSC = area under MSC. TEC = between MSC and MPC.
  2. TSB (= private benefit) = area under demand up to Q.
  3. TSS = TSB − TSC. At QMarket: A − D. At QEff: A. DWL = D.
  4. If the exam relabels areas, rebuild with these four rules — don't memorise letters blindly.

M6 · BULLETPROOF: reading a payoff matrix (dominant strategy + Nash)

  1. Label who owns which number. Write above the table: "Row = Country A = 1st number; Column = Country B = 2nd number." (Check the question text — the row player isn't always called A.)
  2. Row player's best responses — work column by column. Put your finger on the first column (Column player's first action). Compare only the FIRST numbers in that column (moving up/down). Star the bigger one. Repeat for the second column.
    Say it: "If B plays X, A gets __ from Row1 and __ from Row2 → A's best response is ___."
  3. Column player's best responses — work row by row. Finger on the first row. Compare only the SECOND numbers in that row (moving left/right). Star the bigger one. Repeat for the second row.
  4. Dominant strategy? Row player has one if both of its stars are in the same row → that row's action is dominant. Column player has one if both of its stars are in the same column. Stars in different rows/columns → "No dominant strategy" (its best response depends on the other's action).
  5. Nash equilibria: every cell where both numbers are starred. There can be 1, 2, or (rarely) 0. Name each as (Row action, Column action).
  6. Sanity checks: (a) If both players have dominant strategies, the NE is the cell where those two actions meet — and it's the only one. (b) If one player has a dominant strategy, the NE is that action + the other's best response to it (= iterated elimination). (c) Is there another cell where both get more than at the NE? Then it's a Prisoner's-Dilemma-type inefficiency.
  7. Ties (not covered in lectures): if two payoffs are equal, star both — both are best responses.

Mnemonic: "Row looks down the column at the first number; Column looks across the row at the second number."

Written-answer template (copy of the model answer structure, 4 marks):
"[Yes/No]. If Country B plays X: R1 gives Country A a, R2 gives b; a > b, so R1 is A's best response to B's X. If Country B plays Y: R1 gives c, R2 gives d; … so ___ is A's best response to B's Y. [Since ___ is the best response against each and every one of B's actions, it is A's dominant strategy.] / [As no action is the best response to every one of B's actions, A has no dominant strategy.]"

Q-type 1 · Diagram dropdowns (negative externality, non-parallel curves) ≈10 marks

Use the non-parallel diagram in Learn §4 (MB, PMC, SMC with gridlines at 14, 20, 24, 36 and Q = 300, 500).

"The diagram illustrates a [positive / negative / potential / uncertain] externality. The competitive market output is [300 / 400 / 500 / indeterminable], the socially optimal output is [300 / 400 / 500 / indeterminable]. To correct it the government can [enforce a price / impose a tax / provide a subsidy] of [$24 − $14 = $10 / $36 − $20 = $16 / $24 − $20 = $4 / $20 − $14 = $6] per unit."

Reasoning for each choice
  • Social curve is on the cost side, above PMC → negative.
  • Market: MB ∩ PMC → 500 (price 20).
  • Optimum: MB ∩ SMC → 300 (24).
  • Tax at QEff = 300: SMC 24 − PMC 14 = $10. $16 is the gap at the market Q (distractor). $4 and $6 mix the wrong curves.

Q-type 2 · Calculate the DWL (written, show working) 3–4 marks

Same diagram: "Calculate the deadweight loss associated with the externality."

Model answer

Market output 500 exceeds the efficient 300. For units 300–500, SMC > MB. DWL is the triangle with vertices (300, 24), (500, 36), (500, 20).
Height at QMarket = SMC(500) − MB(500) = 36 − 20 = 16. Base = 500 − 300 = 200.
DWL = ½ × 16 × 200 = $1600.

Q-type 3 · Positive externality: tax or subsidy, and how much? (+ admin cost) 4 + 4 marks

Use the positive-externality diagram (MPB, MSB = MPB + $12, MC; QMarket = 200, Q* = 260). (a) Tax or subsidy? Amount? (b) The government will act only if DWL exceeds an administrative cost of $400. Will it?

Model answer

(a) Positive externality → under-production → per-unit subsidy = MEB at Q* = MSB(260) − MPB(260) = 36 − 24 = $12. It shifts private demand up by $12 so it meets supply at Q* = 260.
(b) DWL = ½ × (MSB(200) − MC(200)) × (260 − 200) = ½ × (42 − 30) × 60 = $360. Since $360 < $400 admin cost, the government would not implement it.

Q-type 5 · "Is this a Prisoner's Dilemma?" / how does a payoff change alter the game? MCQ / dropdown

Two nations choose Green or Pollute. (Green, Green) = 5, 5; (Green, Pollute) = 1, 7; (Pollute, Green) = 7, 1; (Pollute, Pollute) = 2, 2. Then a "name and shame" rule cuts the payoff from Pollute-when-the-other-is-Green from 7 to 4. What changes?

Answer

Before: A: vs Green 7 > 5 → Pollute; vs Pollute 2 > 1 → Pollute. Pollute dominant for both (symmetric). NE = (Pollute, Pollute) with 2 each, yet (Green, Green) gives 5 each → Prisoner's Dilemma.
After (7 → 4): A: vs Green 5 > 4 → Green; vs Pollute 2 > 1 → Pollute. No dominant strategy for either. NE = (Green, Green) and (Pollute, Pollute) — a coordination game, no longer a PD.

Q-type 6 · Concept dropdown / MCQ

e.g. "Kyoto failed mainly because [there was no punishment for cheating]", "GHG abatement generates a [positive] externality, creating an incentive to [free-ride]", "The Paris Agreement's core is [pledge and review] of [NDCs]", "Under a Pigouvian tax equal to MEC the supply curve shifts [up] to [MSC]", "A Nash equilibrium is an outcome in which [every player plays a best response to the others' actions]".

Traps & misconceptions

Tax measured at the wrong quantity. Tax = MEC at QEff (SMC − PMC directly above the social optimum). The gap at QMarket is the classic distractor (practice exam: $15 − $10 = $5 correct, $18 − $12 = $6 wrong).
DWL height at the wrong quantity. Height is the gap at QMarket between social curve and the other curve (SMC − MB, or SMB − MC). Not the tax, unless curves are parallel.
Market Q uses the social curve. The market ignores externalities: market = private ∩ private. Only the optimum uses the social curve.
Calling the market outcome P*/Q*. The star means socially optimal. With externalities they differ.
Subsidy vs tax. Positive externality → subsidy (shifts demand up). Negative → tax (shifts supply up). "Enforce a price" is never the Pigouvian answer.
Mixing up whose payoff is whose. First number = row player. When checking the column player, compare the SECOND numbers across a row.
Comparing the wrong pair. For A's best response you hold B's action fixed (one column) and compare A's two rows. Don't compare A's payoffs across a row — that's A comparing B's choices, which A can't control.
Answering with payoffs. The NE is "(Status quo, Status quo)", not "(0, 0)". And the order is (Row action, Column action) — "(Status quo, Regulate)" ≠ "(Regulate, Status quo)".
"No dominant strategy" ≠ "no Nash equilibrium". The name-and-shame game has no dominant strategies but two NE.
Assuming the NE is efficient. In a PD, the NE (both defect) is worse for both than (cooperate, cooperate).
Dominant strategy = biggest number in the matrix. No — it's about being best in every column (row player) / every row (column player).
TSS at the market = A, not A − D. At QMarket social surplus is A − D; D is subtracted because those units cost society more than they're worth.
"Select one or more" Nash questions. If there are two double-starred cells, tick both.

Quick self-check

1. MB: P = 90 − Q, MPC: P = 10 + Q, MEC = $20 (constant). Find QMarket, QEff, tax, DWL.

Market: 90 − Q = 10 + Q → Q = 40, P = 50. MSC = 30 + Q; Eff: 90 − Q = 30 + Q → Q = 30 (P = 60). Tax = $20. DWL = ½ × (MSC(40) − MB(40)) × (40 − 30) = ½ × (70 − 50) × 10 = 100.

2. In Q1, with the tax, what price do buyers pay and sellers keep? What's the tax revenue?

PB = MB(30) = 60; PS = 60 − 20 = 40 (= MPC(30) ✓). Revenue = 20 × 30 = 600 (= TEC at QEff).

3. A graph shows SMC = 18 and PMC = 12 at Q = 2000 (market), and SMC = 15, PMC = 10 at Q = 1200 (optimum). Tax per unit?

At QEff = 1200: 15 − 10 = $5. (The $6 gap at 2000 is the distractor.)

4. In Q3, if MB(2000) = 12, what is the DWL?

½ × (18 − 12) × (2000 − 1200) = ½ × 6 × 800 = 2400.

5. Matrix (A rows Restrict/Status quo; B cols Restrict/Status quo): (R,R)=2,2; (R,SQ)=4,0; (SQ,R)=1,0; (SQ,SQ)=3,2. Dominant strategies? NE?

A: vs R: 2 > 1 → R; vs SQ: 4 > 3 → R → A dominant = Restrict. B: vs A's R: 2 > 0 → R; vs A's SQ: 2 > 0 → SQ → B none. NE: B's best response to Restrict is Restrict → (Restrict, Restrict) only. (This is the practice-exam game.)

6. Which two features define a Prisoner's Dilemma?

Both players have a dominant strategy; when both play it the outcome is collectively inefficient (another outcome makes both better off).

7. Matrix: (Up, Left) = 1, 3; (Up, Right) = 4, 1; (Down, Left) = 2, 2; (Down, Right) = 3, 4. Find all NE.

Row: vs Left 2 > 1 → Down*; vs Right 4 > 3 → Up*. Column: vs Up 3 > 1 → Left*; vs Down 4 > 2 → Right*. Row stars sit in (Down, Left) and (Up, Right); column stars sit in (Up, Left) and (Down, Right). No cell has both. No Nash equilibrium (in these pure actions) — like Rock-Paper-Scissors.

8. Why is GHG abatement a positive externality, and what problem does that cause?

The abating country bears the private cost but the benefit (avoided climate damage) is shared globally → others can free-ride → too little abatement (PD).

9. Kyoto vs Paris: what changed in the game?

Kyoto: no punishment for cheating → PD (Status quo dominant). Paris: name and shame lowers the payoff from cheating → coordination game with a good NE (Reduce, Reduce); ratcheting NDCs help coordinate on it; domestic pressure can make Reduce dominant.

10. Stern Review: cost of inaction vs cost of action?

Inaction ≈ 5% of global GDP per year (up to 20%); action ≈ 1% of GDP per year.

Game theory — payoff matrix procedure (M6)

Definitions (L11)
Game = players, actions, payoffs. 2×2 simultaneous-move.
Convention: Player 1 = rows, Player 2 = columns; cell = outcome; 1st number → row player, 2nd → column player.
Best response: action with highest payoff given the opponent's action (mark with *).
Dominant strategy: action that is always a best response to all of the opponent's actions.
Nash equilibrium: outcome where all players play best responses to each other's chosen actions (self-enforcing; nobody wants to change). = cell with two stars. Can be more than one.
Prisoner's Dilemma: both have dominant strategy + resulting outcome collectively inefficient.
Coordination game: 2 NE (good & bad); need trust/coordination device.
IEDS: remove dominated action, best-respond to what's left → NE.
Steps
  1. Write: Row = __ = 1st no.; Col = __ = 2nd no.
  2. Row player: for each column, compare 1st numbers up/down; star larger.
  3. Column player: for each row, compare 2nd numbers left/right; star larger.
  4. Row's stars in the same row → dominant = that row. Col's stars in the same column → dominant. Else "no dominant strategy".
  5. NE = every cell with both numbers starred; name it (Row action, Col action), never by payoffs.
  6. Check: one dominant → NE = (that action, other's BR to it). Better cell for both? → PD inefficiency.
  7. Ties: star both.
"Row looks DOWN a column at 1st numbers; Column looks ACROSS a row at 2nd numbers."
Written answer (4 marks)
"Yes/No. If B plays X: R1 gives A __, R2 gives A __ → __ is A's best response to X. If B plays Y: R1 gives A __, R2 gives A __ → __ is A's best response to Y. Since __ is the best response to each and every one of B's actions, it is A's dominant strategy. / Since A's best response differs with B's action, A has no dominant strategy."

Climate facts & policy (dropdown fodder)

Market failure = competitive market over/under-produces vs social optimum. Causes: externalities, monopoly, asymmetric info. "Market fails, government fails too."
Externality: cost/benefit to a third party not involved in production/consumption; decision-maker doesn't bear full cost / get full benefit. Neg: smoking, coal power. Pos: vaccination, education, GHG abatement.
Climate = neg. externality of emissions (Stern: "greatest market failure the world has ever seen"); abatement = pos. externality → free-riding.
Pigou: corrective tax/subsidy; sin taxes; Pigou Club backs carbon tax. Problems: measuring MEC; getting current generation to pay for future.
Aus carbon tax: Gillard 2012, $20/t CO₂, cut 17 Mt (ANU 2014), repealed Abbott 2014.
Not lectured 2026: cap-and-trade (cap at QEff; permit price = MEC(QEff) ≡ tax), Coase (bargaining fixes it if property rights clear & transaction costs low).
IPCC 1988, 195 states, synthesises (AR6 2023). Target 1.5°C; net-zero by 2050 (2°C → 2070).
Stern 2006: inaction ≈ 5% world GDP/yr (to 20%); action ≈ 1%.
Kyoto: signed 1997, in force 2005; Annex I cut avg 5.2% vs 1990 by 2008–12; developing exempt; US out 2001, Canada 2011; round 2 lost US/Russia/Japan/Canada/NZ. No punishment for cheating (Barrett 2008) → PD.
Paris 2016; <2°C pref. 1.5°C; 194 countries/98% emissions; US out 2017, in 2021, out 2025. Pledge & review of NDCs (self-set, public, UNFCCC). Global Stocktake every 5 yrs (first 2023). Enforcement = name and shame. Ratcheting: NDCs updated every 5 yrs, more ambitious → coordination device.
US left Kyoto → others followed; US left Paris → others stayed (payoff structure matters).

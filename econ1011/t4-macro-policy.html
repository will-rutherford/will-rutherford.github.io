The big idea

Macroeconomics is just demand-and-supply for the whole economy: the vertical axis is the price level P (measured by the CPI) and the horizontal axis is real output Y (real GDP). Aggregate Demand (AD) slopes down, short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) slopes up because nominal wages are sticky, and long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) is vertical at potential GDP Ȳ.

Inflation comes from AD shifting right (demand-pull) or SRAS shifting left (cost-push). If the economy is left alone it drifts back to Ȳ as wages adjust and SRAS shifts, but that can take years. So the government (fiscal policy: G and net taxes T) and the RBA (monetary policy: the cash rate) shift AD to speed things up. Almost every exam question boils down to two steps: which curve shifts, and which way? Then read off P and Y.

The lecture's story: the 2022–23 cost-of-living crisis. Post-COVID "revenge spending", returning immigration and recovering exports pushed AD right. The Russia–Ukraine war (grain, energy, fertiliser) pushed SRAS left. Inflation went well above the RBA's 2–3% target, so the RBA raised the cash rate 13 times (0.1% → 4.35%, May 2022 – Dec 2023). "The pain is the point."

1 · Inflation: what it is and why it hurts

Inflation is an increase in the prices of goods and services. For example, 2% p.a. inflation means prices rose 2% over a year. The RBA targets 2–3% p.a., which is thought to be consistent with a healthy economy (inflation averaged 2.5% p.a. from 1993 to 2019). Deflation is a fall in the general price level, or negative inflation. Australia had deflation during COVID: prices fell 0.3% from June 2019 to June 2020.

The definition of inflation, the CPI, nominal vs real values and the RBA's 2–3% band.

Nominal vs real. Nominal variables are measured in money. Real variables are measured in amounts of goods and services, i.e. adjusted for price changes. The lecture's thought experiment: if every price, wage and bank balance were multiplied by 10 overnight, life would go on, because nothing real changed. Inflation is only a cost-of-living problem if it reduces purchasing power.

Real Income = Nominal Income ÷ Prices of goods and services
Lecture example: $50,000 salary, apples cost $2 each, so real income = 50,000/2 = 25,000 apples.
If prices rise faster than nominal income, real income falls.
Shortcut: %Δ real income ≈ %Δ nominal income − %Δ prices (inflation).

Worked example. Your wage rises 3% while the CPI rises 6%. Real income changes by about 3 − 6 = −3%. The exact figure is 1.03/1.06 − 1 = −2.83%. That is the cost-of-living crisis in one line: the lecture's chart shows the CPI outrunning the Wage Price Index (WPI) after 2021, giving a 4.4–10% fall in real wages depending on the measure.

Measuring the price level: the CPI. The Consumer Price Index measures the price of a fixed basket of goods and services that represents what a typical household buys. Changes in the CPI reflect changes in the cost of living for a typical household in an Australian capital city. Because not all prices rise at the same rate and households buy different baskets, the ABS also publishes Selected Living Cost Indices (SLCI) for employee, age-pensioner, other-transfer-recipient and self-funded-retiree households. Employee households' living costs rose faster than the CPI because mortgage interest is in the SLCI. The impact was uneven: less-educated people, single parents, women and people on low incomes reported the most financial stress.

Expected vs unexpected inflation. If inflation is expected, people build it into wages and prices. For example, if 4% is expected, workers negotiate 4% pay rises, so the real effects are limited. But this makes inflation a self-fulfilling prophecy (higher wages → higher prices), which is why the RBA cares about expectations. Inflation has real effects when it is unexpected: previously agreed pay rises (e.g. UQ's enterprise agreement gives about 4% p.a.) no longer protect real income.

When inflation is too high in absolute terms: hyperinflation (above 500% per month, or about 13,000% per year). It raises the transaction costs of doing business. Menu costs are sellers' costs of constantly updating prices. Shoe-leather costs are consumers' efforts to preserve purchasing power. Examples are Germany in the 1920s (29,500% p.a.), Zimbabwe in 2008 (98% per day) and Venezuela in 2018 (about 2 million % p.a.).

Deflation is also bad. Money gains purchasing power over time, so households delay consumption and firms hold cash instead of investing. The economy contracts.

2 · GDP: measuring output

GDP (expenditure method) = the market value of all final goods and services newly produced during a specified period in a specified country or region. Each piece of that definition decides what counts:

Whether a good is final depends on who buys it and what they do with it.Steel sold for $1,000 and used in a $10,000 car: GDP rises by $10,000, not $11,000. Bread for your breakfast is final; bread for a café's sandwiches is intermediate.
Newly producedSecond-hand or used goods are not counted. Only new services attached to the sale count.House built in 2014 and resold in 2015 for $1,210: only the agent's $10 fee counts in 2015 GDP (answer C). Peter's $200 capital gain is a transfer, not production.
In a specified regionProduction inside the borders counts, whoever owns the firmiPhones made in China count in China's GDP, not the US's. Imported tequila counts in Mexico's GDP.

Not examinable: financial assets, the informal sector, household production and externalities are excluded. The three methods (expenditure, production/value-added, income) give the same answer in principle. ECON1011 focuses on the expenditure method.

Expenditure identity: Y = C + I + G + (X − M), where NX = X − M
C = household consumption (education, food, shelter) · I = firms' spending on capital goods (trucks, railway tracks, CNC machines) · G = government purchases (bullets, roads, bridges, public schools) · X = foreigners' spending on domestic output (beef to Singapore, tourism) · M = residents' spending on foreign output.
Why subtract M? C + I + G includes spending on imports, and GDP counts only domestically produced output.

Nominal vs real GDP. Expenditure = P × Q, so %ΔExpenditure ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ. Nominal GDP can rise just because prices rose. Real GDP is the market value of all final goods and services measured at prices from a common base year. With prices held fixed (%ΔP = 0), %ΔReal GDP = %ΔQ. Economists focus on real GDP. If P doubles and Q halves, nominal GDP is unchanged but real GDP halves.

3 · Aggregate Demand (AD)

AD is the relationship between the price level P and the quantity of domestic output demanded YD = C + I + G + (X − M). It slopes downward for three reasons, all of which are movements along AD caused by a change in P:

EffectChainWhy
Wealth effectP↑ ⇒ C↓Much household wealth is held as nominal assets (cash and deposits: $1.7T of $15.3T in Sept 2023). Higher P cuts their real value, so households feel poorer and spend less.
Interest-rate effectP↑ ⇒ C↓ & I↓Higher prices mean households need to borrow more for cars, appliances and houses. Demand for loans rises, so interest rates rise, so borrowing-financed C and I fall.
International-trade effectP↑ ⇒ NX↓ (M↑, X↓)With foreign prices held constant, domestic goods become relatively dearer. Locals switch to imports and foreigners buy fewer exports.

Shifts of AD. Anything that changes C, I, G or NX at a given P shifts AD. Examples: consumer or business confidence, population growth, government spending, taxes and transfers, interest rates set by the RBA, and foreign demand for exports.

The AD = C + I + G + (X − M) components and the idea of AD shifts.

4 · Aggregate Supply: the short run vs the long run

AS is the relationship between P and the quantity of output firms supply, YS. It depends on the cost of inputs (wages, rents, intermediate inputs) and the availability of factors (labour, capital, technology).

  • Short run is a time frame in which nominal wages cannot adjust as quickly as prices, typically less than several years (weeks, quarters, a few years).
  • Long run is a period long enough for wages to "catch up" with prices, about a decade or more.

Why SRAS slopes up: sticky wages. Wages change slowly because of (1) long-term labour contracts, e.g. UQ's enterprise agreement locks in about 4% p.a. until 2026, and (2) wages are revised less often than prices (petrol prices change daily, wages yearly). When P rises, real wage = nominal wage ÷ P falls, so production becomes more profitable and firms supply more.

Why LRAS is vertical. In the long run wages catch up with prices. If P rises 10%, wages rise 10%, so real wages and real profits are unchanged and firms have no reason to change output. Output is set only by the economy's factors of production (labour force, capital stock, natural resources, technology). That output level is potential GDP Ȳ.

Potential GDP is the level of real output when the economy produces at full capacity, i.e. the output it can sustain in the long run, often estimated as long-run trend GDP. Full capacity ≠ maximum. A car plant normally runs 2 shifts (200 cars/day) and uses the 3rd shift for maintenance. It can run a 3rd shift (300 cars/day), but that is unsustainable because machines break down. So real GDP can be above (or below) Ȳ in the short run.
What shifts SRAS?What shifts LRAS?
Input costs and input scarcity: oil, energy, grain and fertiliser prices, nominal wages. Cost ↑ ⇒ SRAS shifts left (firms want a higher P for the same Y, or supply less at the same P). In the self-correction process, real-wage adjustments shift SRAS.The quantity or quality of factors of production: labour force, capital stock, technology, natural resources (growth topic, T7). Policy that only shifts AD does not move LRAS.

5 · Equilibrium: short run vs long run

Short-run equilibrium is where AD = SRAS. Long-run equilibrium is where AD = LRAS. Every LR equilibrium is also an SR equilibrium, so all three curves meet at one point. In the long run output must equal potential GDP, and AD only determines the price level.

If AD ∩ SRAS is not on LRAS, the economy is in a short-run but not long-run equilibrium. Market forces will slowly push output back to Ȳ, though this "may take a while without government interventions".

Exam Q3(a): "short-run but not long-run equilibrium, click the point". Click AD ∩ SRAS, which is off the LRAS line. Never click a point on LRAS.

6 · Self-correction: the movement back to long-run equilibrium

The lecture does not use the terms "recessionary gap" or "inflationary gap". It says "below full employment / economic contraction" and "above full employment / overheating". Recognise both sets of words. Self-correction works through SRAS (wages), not AD. AD and LRAS stay put.
Slide 26 of Lecture 7 says "Lowering Price Levels (P↑)", and slide 28 says "Decreasing Real GDP (Y↑)". Both arrows are typos. Below potential, self-correction lowers P and raises Y. Above potential, it raises P and lowers Y. The diagrams on slides 27 and 29 are correct.

So why intervene? Adjustment through market forces alone can take a long time, even decades. The Great Depression lasted from 1929 to 1939, and there is a strong argument that WWII government spending pulled the US out of it. Policy hastens the return to LR equilibrium.

8 · Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy means deliberate changes to the government's spending and tax policies aimed at influencing macroeconomic conditions (full employment, price stability, sustainable growth).

Net taxes: T = Tax revenue (TR) − Transfer payments (TP)
TR is paid by households to the government (income tax, company tax). TP is paid by the government to households (unemployment and disability benefits, pensions, childcare subsidies).
Disposable income = Y − TR + TP = Y − T. It is the income left for saving and consumption.
Government purchases G: new infrastructure, public servants' pay, military spending.
Expansionary (to stimulate output)Contractionary (to curb inflation)
ToolsG↑ and/or net taxes T↓ (cut taxes or raise transfers)G↓ and/or T↑ (raise taxes or cut transfers)
ChannelT↓ ⇒ disposable income↑ ⇒ C↑. G↑ ⇒ G itself ↑, plus household income ↑ ("a dollar spent is a dollar earned") ⇒ disposable income↑ ⇒ C↑T↑ ⇒ disposable income↓ ⇒ C↓. G↓ ⇒ G↓ and household income ↓ ⇒ C↓
ADShifts right: YD↑ = C↑ + I + G↑ + NXShifts left
Short-run resultP↑, Y↑P↓, Y↓
Not in these lectures: the numeric multiplier formula (1/(1−MPC)) and crowding out. The lecture only gives the qualitative point that G↑ raises income and therefore C as well. Don't compute multipliers unless a question gives you one. In ECON1011, fiscal policy simply shifts AD.

9 · Monetary policy and the RBA

Monetary policy means deliberate changes to interest rates and/or the money supply intended to influence macroeconomic conditions. In Australia it is run by the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA), whose remit is (1) stability of the currency, understood as keeping inflation within 2–3% p.a., (2) full employment and (3) the economic prosperity and welfare of Australians. Objectives 2 and 3 mean counteracting booms and contractions, i.e. keeping the economy at Ȳ.

The cash rate is the interest rate at which banks lend each other money overnight in the interbank money market. The RBA Board meets 8 times a year to set it. It is the benchmark for all other rates: it sets banks' funding costs (the risk-free opportunity cost of funds banks lend out), so it flows through to mortgage, business-loan and personal-loan rates.

Transmission mechanism (the lecture's version):
RBA raises the cash rate (or reduces the money supply) ⇒ banks' funding costs↑ ⇒ all interest rates↑ ⇒
• C↓: big-ticket durables and housing are financed by borrowing, which is now dearer
• I↓: investment is financed largely by borrowing or bonds. Even firms using their own funds face a higher opportunity cost, since they forgo the interest they could have earned.
⇒ AD shifts left: AD↓ = C↓ + I↓ + G + NX ⇒ P↓, Y↓ (short run).
Lower cash rate or more money supply: everything reverses, AD shifts right.
ExpansionaryContractionary
MonetaryReduce interest rates · increase money supplyRaise interest rates · reduce money supply
FiscalReduce net taxation · increase government spendingIncrease net taxation · reduce government spending
IntentionStimulate output (downturn, Y < Ȳ)Curb inflation (Y > Ȳ or high inflation)

How painful were the rate hikes? The average mortgage rate rose from 2.9% (Apr 2022) to 5.9% (Mar 2023). On a $600,000, 30-year loan, monthly repayments rose from $2,498 to $3,559 (+$1,061/month), against median gross household income of $7,691/month.

Evaluation (lecturer's view). Running expansionary fiscal policy during high inflation is "adding fuel to the fire": it raises AD and prices further. Why do it? The 2022 federal election. Contractionary policy calms inflation by reducing AD (and output), but it is unpopular: lower disposable income and higher loan repayments. Overall, monetary policy was contractionary and fiscal policy maybe expansionary, so the RBA's disinflationary effect was partly offset. Monetary policy worked: "the pain is the point". Financial stress makes households cut consumption, which lowers AD.

Key exam idea: whether contractionary policy is costly depends on the source of inflation. With demand-pull it just cools an overheated economy back to Ȳ. With cost-push it fights prices at the cost of deeper output losses and a higher risk of recession. Hence the question: should the RBA always hit 2–3%?

Why inflation targeting?

  1. Expectations are self-fulfilling. If everyone expects 5% inflation, everyone raises prices and wages by 5%, so inflation is 5%.
  2. Politicians are tempted. AD↑ raises GDP and lowers unemployment in the short run, so a government seeking re-election runs expansionary fiscal policy even at full employment, which causes inflation. People anticipate this, so expected inflation rises, and so does actual inflation.
  3. An inflation-targeting central bank counters this. It offsets self-interested fiscal expansion with contractionary monetary policy, which removes politicians' incentive. This only works if the central bank is politically independent (hence the controversy over Trump wanting more control of the US Fed).

Keep the promise? Abandoning the target when it is costly avoids pain, but then nobody believes future promises. If targets move "as needed" they lose their purpose. The RBA Review recommended a dual objective: price stability and full employment, with equal consideration given to each.

Step-by-step methods

M1 · Classify an event: which curve, which direction?
  1. Ask whether it changes spending (C, I, G, X, M) at a given P. If so, it moves AD. Spending up shifts AD right.
  2. Ask whether it changes firms' costs of production or input availability in the short run. If so, it moves SRAS. Costs up shift SRAS left.
  3. Ask whether it changes the economy's long-run productive capacity (labour force, capital, technology). If so, it moves LRAS (and usually SRAS too).
  4. Ask whether it is a change in P itself. If so, the economy moves along the curves.
  5. Policy: G, taxes, transfers, interest rates and the money supply all move AD, never SRAS (in this course).
M2 · Predict P and Y
  1. Draw the starting point where AD = SRAS.
  2. Shift the curve(s) from M1.
  3. One shift: AD right gives P↑ Y↑. AD left gives P↓ Y↓. SRAS left gives P↑ Y↓. SRAS right gives P↓ Y↑.
  4. Two shifts: write the effect of each on P and Y. Where the arrows agree, the answer is certain. Where they disagree, the answer is "uncertain", unless the question states which shift is bigger.
M3 · Short-run vs long-run position → choose the policy
  1. Find AD ∩ SRAS and drop a line to the Y axis. Compare it with the LRAS line (Ȳ).
  2. If Y* < Ȳ, output is below potential (contraction, high unemployment). You need AD right: expansionary policy, i.e. lower interest rates, more money supply, lower taxes, higher transfers or higher G.
  3. If Y* > Ȳ, output is above potential (overheating, inflation). You need AD left: contractionary policy, i.e. higher interest rates, less money supply, higher taxes or lower G.
  4. Check the label the question asks for. Monetary means interest rate or money supply. Fiscal means G, taxes or transfers. Eliminate options of the wrong type and of the wrong direction.
M4 · Policy → AD chain (say it in this order)
  • Income tax↑ ⇒ T↑ ⇒ disposable income (Y − T)↓ ⇒ C↓ ⇒ AD shifts left ⇒ P↓, Y↓.
  • G↑ ⇒ G↑ and household income↑ ⇒ C↑ ⇒ AD shifts right ⇒ P↑, Y↑.
  • Cash rate↑ (or money supply↓) ⇒ interest rates↑ ⇒ C↓ and I↓ ⇒ AD shifts left ⇒ P↓, Y↓.
  • Transfers↑ (e.g. a $250 payment) ⇒ T↓ ⇒ disposable income↑ ⇒ C↑ ⇒ AD shifts right.
M5 · Self-correction (no policy)
  1. Is Y* below Ȳ? Then unemployment is high, real wages fall, SRAS shifts right, P↓ and Y↑ back to Ȳ.
  2. Is Y* above Ȳ? Then unemployment is low, real wages rise, SRAS shifts left, P↑ and Y↓ back to Ȳ.
  3. The new LR equilibrium is at AD ∩ LRAS (AD has not moved). Read off its price level.
M6 · Numbers on a grid or with equations
  1. SR equilibrium: set the AD equation equal to the SRAS equation (or read their crossing on the grid) to get Y* and P*.
  2. Output gap = Ȳ − Y* (positive means below potential).
  3. Required policy shift: find where the current SRAS crosses LRAS. That point is where AD must pass. The horizontal distance between the old AD and that point (at the same P) is the AD shift needed.
  4. No-policy end point: find where the current AD crosses LRAS. That is the LR equilibrium after SRAS self-corrects.
M7 · Nominal and real GDP
  1. Nominal GDPt = Σ Pt × Qt (current prices).
  2. Real GDPt = Σ Pbase × Qt (base-year prices). In the base year, real = nominal.
  3. Growth = (new − old)/old × 100%. Nominal growth ≈ inflation + real growth.
M8 · Real income and real wages
  1. Real income = nominal income ÷ price (or ÷ index/100).
  2. %Δ real ≈ %Δ nominal − inflation. Exact: (1 + gnominal)/(1 + π) − 1.
  3. If wages grow more slowly than the CPI, real wages fall and the cost of living rises.
M9 · Does it count in this year's GDP?
  1. Is it a final good or service? If it is intermediate (used to make something else), it is already in the final value, so don't count it.
  2. Was it newly produced this period? A resale doesn't count, but the new service attached to it (agent's fee, dealer margin) does.
  3. Was it produced inside the country? If not (an import, or production by a domestic firm overseas), it doesn't count.
  4. Is it a market transaction at a market price? A financial asset, household production or the black market doesn't count.

How it's examined

Calibrated from the S2 2026 practice exam Q3 (Macroland, 12 marks) and the S1 2026 final Q3 (Wonderland, (a)–(e)) walked through in Lecture 13. Expect one macro section of about 4–5 parts: click-the-point, a below/above dropdown, a policy select-one/select-two, an event → curve → P and Y dropdown chain, a combined-shock question and a short written evaluation. The examples below are fresh.

Type 1 · "Click the point: short-run but not long-run equilibrium" ~2 marks

You see AD, SRAS and a vertical LRAS with no numbers. Click where AD crosses SRAS. If the question said "long-run equilibrium", you would click where AD crosses LRAS, which only works if SRAS also passes through it.

Example. Suppose LRAS sits to the right of the AD ∩ SRAS crossing. The answer is the AD ∩ SRAS point, and at that point output is below potential GDP. If LRAS were to the left of the crossing, output would be above potential.

Type 2 · "Output is [below/above] potential. Which policy returns it to potential?" 2–4 marks

Example. Freedonia's AD ∩ SRAS lies to the right of LRAS. Which two can move output to potential? (i) Raise income tax, (ii) cut the cash rate, (iii) increase government purchases, (iv) sell bonds to reduce the money supply.

Answer

Output is above Ȳ, so AD must shift left, which requires contractionary policy. (i) T↑ ⇒ C↓ ⇒ AD left ✔. (iv) Money supply↓ ⇒ interest↑ ⇒ C↓, I↓ ⇒ AD left ✔. (ii) and (iii) are expansionary ✘. If the question asked for "a monetary policy" only, the answer is (iv). Income tax is fiscal and cannot be chosen even though its direction is right.

Type 3 · "Event → [increase/reduce the AD/SRAS] → [higher/lower/same/uncertain] output and price" 4–6 marks

Example. The government of Macroland cuts personal income tax rates. This will ___ leading to a ___ output level and a ___ price level.

Answer

A tax cut ⇒ disposable income↑ ⇒ C↑, so it will increase the AD, leading to a higher output level and a higher price level (short run, upward-sloping SRAS). Treat tax and spending policy as AD shifts, not SRAS shifts. Building a stadium (G↑) works the same way: increase the AD, higher output, higher price.

Type 4 · Two simultaneous shocks ~8 marks

Example. A drought destroys half of Wonderland's wheat crop, which is an input for most food manufacturers. At the same time a mining boom in a trading partner sharply raises demand for Wonderland's iron ore exports. The drought causes a(n) ___. The export boom causes a(n) ___. Combined, real output will ___ and the price level will ___.

Answer

Drought: input scarcity, so a decrease in SRAS (P↑, Y↓). Export boom: X↑, so an increase in AD (P↑, Y↑). Combined: the price level increases (both push P up) and real output is uncertain (the two pushes conflict). This matches the 2022 Australian case. The mirror case from the S1 2026 final (an oil-price spike, SRAS↓, plus lost confidence, AD↓) gives output falls and price level uncertain.

Type 5 · Short written answer: which effect dominates? ~4 marks

Example. "Would a global gas-price spike cause inflation in Wonderland? Consider (a) Wonderland generates most electricity from gas and confidence is unaffected, and (b) Wonderland is mostly renewable but households are very worried about the spike's effect on their export jobs."

Model answer (lecturer's structure)

(a) Heavy gas reliance means a large decrease in SRAS (large upward pressure on P). Little confidence effect means only a small decrease in AD (small downward pressure on P). P likely rises, so there is inflation, while output falls.
(b) Little gas reliance means a minimal decrease in SRAS (small P↑). Worried households cut C and expected exports fall, so there is a large decrease in AD (large P↓). P likely falls, possibly into deflation. Output falls in both cases.
Mark-winning structure: name the curve, the size of the shift, the direction of P from each, then the net effect.

Type 6 · Use the grid (numbers read off a scaled diagram)

Example. (a) What are the SR equilibrium P and Y? (b) Is output above or below potential, and by how much? (c) By how much must AD shift horizontally for policy to restore Ȳ, and what is the new price level? (d) Without policy, what price level is the economy heading to?

Answer

(a) 160 − Y = 20 + Y ⇒ Y* = 70, P* = 90 (point A).
(b) 70 < 80, so output is below potential by 10. That calls for expansionary policy.
(c) SRAS₀ crosses LRAS at Y = 80, P = 20 + 80 = 100 (point B). AD must pass through B: P = a − Y ⇒ 100 = a − 80 ⇒ a = 180. So AD₁: P = 180 − Y, which is a shift right by 20 units (at P = 90, AD₀ gives Y = 70 and AD₁ gives Y = 90). The price level rises to 100.
(d) With no policy, SRAS shifts right until it meets AD₀ at Ȳ: P = 160 − 80 = 80. Self-correction gives the same Y but a lower P, more slowly.

Type 7 · Policy evaluation: demand-pull vs cost-push

Example. Inflation hits 7% because of an oil-supply shock. The central bank raises the cash rate. Compared with doing nothing, what happens to the price level and real output, and why is this more controversial than raising rates during a consumer-spending boom?

Answer

The rate rise shifts AD left: the price level is lower and real output is lower than with no policy. Because the original shock (SRAS left) had already cut output (stagflation), the policy pushes Y further below potential and risks a recession. With a demand boom, the same rate rise just brings an overheated Y back toward Ȳ, so prices fall with little cost to sustainable output.

Type 8 · GDP and real vs nominal (MCQ / numeric)

Example. An economy makes pizzas and haircuts. 2024: pizzas $5 × 100, haircuts $20 × 30. 2025: pizzas $6 × 110, haircuts $25 × 30. Using base year 2024, find nominal GDP in both years, 2025 real GDP, and real growth.

Answer

Nominal 2024 = 500 + 600 = $1,100. Nominal 2025 = 660 + 750 = $1,410 (+28.2%). Real 2025 (2024 prices) = 5(110) + 20(30) = 550 + 600 = $1,150. Real growth = 50/1,100 = 4.55%. The remaining ≈ 23.6 points of nominal growth is price change.
Counting MCQ: a used car resold this year for $8,000 with a $500 dealer commission adds $500 to GDP.

Traps & misconceptions

Movement vs shift. A change in the price level moves you along AD and SRAS. Only non-price changes (confidence, G, T, interest rates set by policy, input costs) shift the curves.
Fiscal ≠ monetary. Income tax, transfers and G are fiscal. The cash rate, interest rates and the money supply are monetary. "Select a monetary policy" options deliberately include a fiscal policy pointing the right way.
Direction of the money supply. Reducing the money supply raises interest rates, which shifts AD left (contractionary). Increasing it is expansionary.
Policy shifts AD, not SRAS. In this course, taxes, G and interest rates all work through C, I and G. Don't pick "increase the SRAS" for a stadium or a tax cut.
Self-correction shifts SRAS, not AD. The wage adjustment moves SRAS. AD and LRAS don't move unless there is a policy or a shock.
Two shifts means one answer is "uncertain". Unless the question gives relative sizes, never guess the direction of the variable the two shifts disagree on.
The long-run price level depends on AD. In the long run Y = Ȳ whatever AD is. AD only changes P.
Potential GDP ≠ maximum output. Y can exceed Ȳ temporarily (overheating, low unemployment).
GDP counting: don't add intermediate goods, resales, capital gains or imports. Do add the new service in a resale (agent fee).
Notation clash: the lecture uses YD for quantity of output demanded (L6) and for disposable income (L7, "YD"). Read the context.
Nominal ≠ real. Higher pay is not better off if prices rose more. Use real GDP, not nominal, to judge output growth.

Quick self-check

1. The RBA cuts the cash rate. What happens to AD, P and Y in the short run?

Interest rates fall, so C and I rise, so AD shifts right: P↑, Y↑.

2. Business confidence collapses. Which curve, which way, and what are the P and Y effects?

I falls, so AD shifts left: P↓, Y↓.

3. A global oil-price spike hits an oil-importing economy. Which curve, and what is the name for the outcome?

Input costs rise, so SRAS shifts left: P↑ and Y↓, i.e. stagflation (cost-push inflation).

4. The economy is at Y* > Ȳ and there is no policy. What shifts, and where do P and Y end up?

Low unemployment raises real wages, so SRAS shifts left until Y = Ȳ. P ends higher and Y falls back to Ȳ.

5. Which is a monetary policy to raise output toward potential: (a) cut income tax (b) raise the cash rate (c) increase the money supply (d) raise G?

(c). (a) and (d) are fiscal. (b) is contractionary.

6. Wages rise 2% while CPI inflation is 5%. What is the approximate change in real wages?

≈ 2 − 5 = −3% (exact 1.02/1.05 − 1 = −2.86%).

7. A café buys $3 of milk and sells $5 coffees. A family sells its 10-year-old house for $900k, paying a $20k agent fee. What is the contribution to GDP?

Coffee: $5 (the milk is intermediate and already included). House: only the $20k fee.

8. Why might contractionary policy be costlier against cost-push inflation than demand-pull?

Cost-push has already reduced Y below its starting level. Shifting AD left lowers Y further, so there is a bigger recession risk. With demand-pull, contraction just returns Y to Ȳ.

9. Give two reasons the lecture gives for SRAS sloping up.

Nominal wages are sticky because of (1) long-term labour contracts and (2) wages being updated less often than prices. So P↑ ⇒ real wage↓ ⇒ profits↑ ⇒ YS↑.

10. Nominal GDP grew 7%, and prices grew 4%. What is the approximate real GDP growth?

%ΔNominal ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ, so %ΔQ ≈ 7 − 4 = 3%.

Definitions
  • Inflation: an increase in the prices of goods and services (% p.a.). Deflation: a fall in the general price level.
  • RBA target: 2–3% p.a. (1993–2019 average 2.5%)
  • CPI: price of a fixed basket bought by a typical capital-city household. SLCI: by household type. WPI: wages.
  • Nominal = in $. Real = in goods (price-adjusted).
  • Hyperinflation: >500%/month (~13,000%/yr). Menu costs, shoe-leather costs.
  • GDP: market value of all final G&S newly produced in a period in a region
  • Real GDP: valued at base-year prices
  • AD: P vs output demanded, YD = C+I+G+(X−M)
  • SR: wages can't keep up with prices (< several years). LR: wages catch up (~decade+).
  • Potential GDP Ȳ: output at full capacity, sustainable in the LR. Not the maximum.
  • Fiscal policy: deliberate changes to G and taxes. Monetary: deliberate changes to interest rates or money supply.
  • Net tax T = TR − TP. Disposable income = Y − T.
  • Cash rate: overnight interbank lending rate, set by the RBA Board (8 meetings/yr). The benchmark for all rates.
  • RBA remit: currency stability (inflation 2–3%), full employment, prosperity and welfare. Review: dual objective (price stability + full employment).
Formulas
  • Real income = Nominal income ÷ Prices
  • %Δreal ≈ %Δnominal − inflation (exact: (1+g)/(1+π) − 1)
  • %ΔExpenditure ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ
  • Nominal GDP = Σ PtQt. Real GDP = Σ PbaseQt.
  • Real wage = nominal wage ÷ price level
  • Growth % = (new − old)/old × 100
  • Equations: set AD = SRAS → Y*, P*. Gap = Ȳ − Y*.

Why AD slopes down (P↑ ⇒ YD↓)
  • Wealth effect: real value of nominal assets ↓ ⇒ C↓
  • Interest-rate effect: more borrowing needed ⇒ r↑ ⇒ C↓, I↓
  • International-trade effect: domestic goods dearer ⇒ M↑, X↓ ⇒ NX↓
Why SRAS slopes up: sticky nominal wages (long-term contracts; wages revised less often than prices). P↑ ⇒ real wage↓ ⇒ profit↑ ⇒ YS↑.
Why LRAS is vertical: wages catch up, so the real wage is unchanged and so is output. Y set by labour, capital, resources and technology.

Self-correction (no policy)
  • Y<Ȳ (contraction): high unemployment, real wages↓, SRAS→, P↓, Y↑ to Ȳ
  • Y>Ȳ (overheating): low unemployment, real wages↑, SRAS←, P↑, Y↓ to Ȳ
  • Ends at AD ∩ LRAS. Slow (Great Depression 1929–39), which is why policy is used.
  • Slide typos: "Lowering P (P↑)" and "Decreasing Y (Y↑)". Trust the diagrams.
GDP counting rules
  • Final only (no intermediate goods, e.g. steel in a car)
  • New production only: resale ✘, agent/dealer fee ✔, capital gain ✘
  • Domestic only: made here ✔. Imports ✘. Our firms' overseas output ✘.
  • Not counted (non-examinable): financial assets, black market, household production, externalities
  • Y = C + I + G + (X − M)
Inflation: costs and context
  • Expected: built into wages and prices, so limited real effect, but self-fulfilling
  • Unexpected: fixed pay rises fall short, so real income falls
  • Deflation: households delay C, firms hoard cash, so the economy contracts
  • Uneven: less-educated, single parents, women and low-income people are hit hardest
  • 2022 AD↑: immigration, revenge spending, business I↑, export demand. SRAS↓: Ukraine war (grain, oil/LNG sanctions, fertiliser +153%).
  • Policy 2022: contractionary fiscal (JobKeeper end, LMITO "Lamington" expiry). Expansionary fiscal (fuel excise cut, tax offset, $250 payment) = "fuel to the fire" (election). RBA contractionary. "The pain is the point."
  • Inflation targeting: (1) expectations are self-fulfilling, (2) politicians are tempted to expand before elections, (3) an independent central bank counteracts this. Keep the promise or lose credibility.

Mini procedures

Policy question
  1. Find AD∩SRAS and compare it with LRAS.
  2. Below Ȳ: need AD→. Above Ȳ: need AD←.
  3. Filter the options by type (monetary/fiscal) and direction.
Event question
  1. Spending or costs? Spending means AD, costs mean SRAS.
  2. Direction?
  3. Read P and Y off the shift table. With two shifts, one variable is "uncertain".
Grid question
  1. Y*, P* at AD∩SRAS.
  2. Gap = Ȳ − Y*.
  3. Policy target: SRAS∩LRAS gives the new P. AD shift = horizontal gap at the same P.
  4. No policy: AD∩LRAS gives the LR P.
Real/nominal GDP
  1. Nominal = ΣPtQt.
  2. Real = ΣPbaseQt.
  3. % growth = (new−old)/old.
  4. Inflation ≈ nominal growth − real growth.

§1 Concepts in depth

The whole point: a model for randomness

A random experiment is any process whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty (a die roll, a person's height, whether a chip is faulty). We tame it with three ingredients (notes §1.4):

  • Sample space \(\Omega\) — the set of all possible outcomes.
  • Events — subsets of \(\Omega\) (e.g. "sum of two dice \(\ge 10\)"). Events are sets, so union \(\cup\), intersection \(\cap\), complement \(A^c\) all apply.
  • Probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) — assigns each event a number in \([0,1]\), obeying Kolmogorov's axioms: \(\mathbb{P}(A)\ge 0\), \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1\), and countable additivity for disjoint events.

Everything else in the course is built on these three objects. If you can name \(\Omega\), the event of interest, and how \(\mathbb{P}\) is assigned, you can answer any Chapter-1 question.

Consequences you will actually use

From the axioms (notes Thm 1.2) come the everyday rules:

  • Complement: \(\mathbb{P}(A^c)=1-\mathbb{P}(A)\) — the single most useful trick for "at least one" problems.
  • Addition rule: \(\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=\mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\). Subtract the overlap so you don't double-count.
  • Equally-likely principle: when \(\Omega\) is finite with all outcomes equally likely, \(\mathbb{P}(A)=|A|/|\Omega|\). Probability collapses to counting.

Counting — the four urn models

Drawing \(k\) balls from \(n\) reduces to two questions: does order matter, and is it with replacement? (notes §1.5)

Order?With replacementWithout replacement
Yes (ordered)\(n^k\)\({}^nP_k=\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}\)
No (unordered)— (not examinable)\({}^nC_k=\dbinom{n}{k}=\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!\,k!}\)

Choosing a lottery set, a poker hand, or which positions the "successes" occupy — all are \(\binom{n}{k}\). This combination is exactly what appears inside the binomial pmf, which is why counting is the bridge into Chapter 2.

Conditional probability, independence, and the two big rules

Conditional probability updates a probability once you know event \(B\) happened (notes Def 1.4): $$\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}.$$ Rearranged, this is the product rule \(\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(B)\,\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)\) — the engine for sequential "draw without replacement" problems.

Independence means knowing \(B\) tells you nothing about \(A\): \(\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\,\mathbb{P}(B)\). Do not confuse independent with disjoint — disjoint events with positive probability are the opposite of independent.

Law of Total Probability: if \(B_1,\dots,B_n\) partition \(\Omega\), $$\mathbb{P}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i)\,\mathbb{P}(B_i).$$ This is how you assemble an overall probability from scenario-by-scenario pieces (which factory made the chip, which test the patient took).

Bayes' Rule flips a conditional around: $$\mathbb{P}(B_j\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_j)\,\mathbb{P}(B_j)}{\sum_i \mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i)\,\mathbb{P}(B_i)}.$$ The classic wording is "given the test is positive, what is the probability the patient actually has the disease?" — you are given \(\mathbb{P}(\text{positive}\mid\text{disease})\) and asked for \(\mathbb{P}(\text{disease}\mid\text{positive})\).

Random variables: cdf, pmf, pdf

A random variable \(X\) is a numerical measurement of the experiment — a function from \(\Omega\) to \(\mathbb{R}\). We describe its behaviour with:

  • cdf \(F(x)=\mathbb{P}(X\le x)\) — defined for every RV, always non-decreasing from 0 to 1. Then \(\mathbb{P}(a\lt X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)\).
  • Discrete RV → pmf \(f(x)=\mathbb{P}(X=x)\), with \(\sum_x f(x)=1\). Probabilities are sums.
  • Continuous RV → pdf \(f(x)\), with \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\). Probabilities are areas: \(\mathbb{P}(a\lt X\lt b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). Crucially \(\mathbb{P}(X=c)=0\) for any single point.

The pdf and cdf are linked by calculus: \(F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(u)\,du\) and \(f(x)=F'(x)\). Integrate the density to get the cdf; differentiate the cdf to get the density. This single fact powers the flagship exam question.

Quantiles, median, mode

The \(p\)-th quantile \(q(p)\) solves \(F(q(p))=p\) (notes Def 1.9). Special cases:

  • Median = the 0.5-quantile: solve \(F(m)=0.5\).
  • Quartiles: \(Q_1=q(0.25)\), \(Q_3=q(0.75)\); the interquartile range is \(\text{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1\).
  • Mode = the value that maximises \(f\). For a continuous RV, differentiate \(f\) (or check the endpoints if \(f\) is monotone on a closed interval).

Median vs mode are different objects: median splits area in half (work with \(F\)); mode is the peak of the density (work with \(f\)).

Expectation and variance

Expectation (the long-run average) is a weighted average of the values \(X\) can take: $$\mathbb{E}(X)=\sum_x x\,f(x)\ \text{(discrete)},\qquad \mathbb{E}(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx\ \text{(continuous)}.$$ For any function \(g\), \(\mathbb{E}(g(X))=\sum_x g(x)f(x)\) or \(\int g(x)f(x)\,dx\) (Law of the Unconscious Statistician).

Variance measures spread: \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X-\mu)^2\), computed in practice via the shortcut $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-\big(\mathbb{E}(X)\big)^2.$$ The standard deviation is \(\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}\).

Linearity / scaling (notes Thm 1.10–1.11) — these are examined constantly: $$\mathbb{E}(aX+b)=a\,\mathbb{E}(X)+b,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X).$$ Note the shift \(b\) drops out of the variance, and the scale \(a\) is squared.

For a symmetric pdf/pmf, the expectation equals the point of symmetry — often no integration needed.

The four named distributions (Chapter 2)

These are the reusable "models" you fit to data. All parameters, pmfs/pdfs and moment formulas are on the provided formula sheet — your job is recognition and execution.

Distributionpmf / pdf\(\mathbb{E}(X)\)\(\operatorname{Var}(X)\)Use for…
Bernoulli \(\text{Ber}(p)\)\(\mathbb{P}(1)=p,\ \mathbb{P}(0)=1-p\)\(p\)\(p(1-p)\)a single yes/no trial
Binomial \(\text{Bin}(n,p)\)\(\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}\)\(np\)\(np(1-p)\)count of successes in \(n\) independent trials
Uniform \(\mathcal U[a,b]\)\(\dfrac{1}{b-a}\) on \([a,b]\)\(\dfrac{a+b}{2}\)\(\dfrac{(b-a)^2}{12}\)"equally likely" over an interval
Normal \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\)\(\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac12\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}\)\(\mu\)\(\sigma^2\)heights, measurement error, sample means (CLT)

R (and the formula sheet) parameterise the Normal by the standard deviation \(\sigma\), but the notation \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) uses the variance. If a question says "variance 36", then \(\sigma=6\). Mixing these up is the single most common Normal error.

Standardising the Normal

There is no closed form for Normal areas, so we convert every Normal question to the standard normal \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and read the table (provided): $$Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma},\qquad \mathbb{P}(X\le x)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right).$$ Use \(\mathbb{P}(Z\ge z)=1-\Phi(z)\) and symmetry \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\). Going backwards ("find the value with 10% above it") uses the quantile: \(x=\mu+z\,\sigma\) where \(\Phi(z)=1-\alpha\).

§2 Key formulas & when to use them

Normalisation of a continuous pdf $$\int_{\text{support}} f(x)\,dx = 1.$$
You are given \(f(x)=c\,x^a\) (or any pdf with unknown constant) and must find that constant. Integrate over the stated range and set equal to 1.
Probability from a pdf $$\mathbb{P}(a\lt X\lt b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a).$$
"Find \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt 4)\)", "\(\mathbb{P}(X\lt k)\)". For a "greater than", it is often fastest to use \(1-F(\text{lower limit})\).
cdf by integration $$F(x)=\int_{\text{lower}}^{x} f(u)\,du \quad(\text{on the support}).$$
"Determine the cumulative distribution function." Remember to state \(F=0\) below the support and \(F=1\) above it — full marks need the piecewise definition.
Median & general quantile $$F(m)=0.5;\qquad F(q_p)=p.$$
"Find the median / quartiles / IQR." Build \(F\), set it equal to the target probability, solve for \(x\).
Mode $$\text{maximise } f(x):\quad f'(x)=0 \text{, or check endpoints if } f \text{ is monotone.}$$
"Find the mode." If \(f\) is increasing on a closed interval (e.g. \(c x^{a}\) with \(a\gt 0\)), the mode is the right endpoint.
Expectation & variance (continuous) $$\mathbb{E}(X)=\int x f(x)\,dx,\quad \mathbb{E}(X^2)=\int x^2 f(x)\,dx,\quad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-\mathbb{E}(X)^2.$$
Any "determine \(\mathbb{E}(X)\) / \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) / \(\text{sd}(X)\)" on a pdf. Always compute \(\mathbb{E}(X^2)\) as its own integral, never \((\mathbb{E}X)^2\) by mistake.
Linearity & scaling $$\mathbb{E}(aX+b)=a\mathbb{E}(X)+b,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X).$$
Unit conversions, transformed variables \(Y=2+3X\), or deriving \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)\).
Binomial probabilities $$\mathbb{P}(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x},\quad \mathbb{E}(X)=np,\ \operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p).$$
A fixed number \(n\) of independent yes/no trials, constant success probability \(p\), counting successes. "At least/at most \(k\)" → sum terms, using the complement trick to shorten.
Complement trick for counts $$\mathbb{P}(X\ge 1)=1-\mathbb{P}(X=0),\qquad \mathbb{P}(X\ge k)=1-\mathbb{P}(X\le k-1).$$
"At least one", "at least \(k\)". Turns a long sum into a short one.
Standardising the Normal $$Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad \mathbb{P}(X\le x)=\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right).$$
"Heights are normally distributed…", "what proportion exceeds…". Convert to \(Z\), read the table.
Normal quantile (reverse lookup) $$x=\mu+z\,\sigma\ \text{ where }\ \Phi(z)=1-\alpha.$$
"Find the temperature exceeded only 10% of the time", "95th percentile", "what mean makes the probability 10%".
Uniform on \([a,b]\) $$f(x)=\frac{1}{b-a},\quad F(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a},\quad \mathbb{E}(X)=\frac{a+b}{2},\ \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}.$$
"Chosen at random / equally likely" over an interval.
Bayes' Rule & Law of Total Probability $$\mathbb{P}(A)=\sum_i \mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i)\mathbb{P}(B_i),\qquad \mathbb{P}(B_j\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_j)\mathbb{P}(B_j)}{\mathbb{P}(A)}.$$
"Given the test is positive…", "what fraction of faulty chips came from factory 1?" — a conditional whose direction is flipped from what you're told.

§3 Worked examples

Example 1 — THE FLAGSHIP: continuous pdf \(f(x)=c\,x^a\) (2025 exam Q1, 10 marks)
Let \(X\) have pdf \(f(x)=c\,x^{a}\) for \(0\le x\le 5\), where \(c\gt 0,\ a\gt 0\). Moreover \(\mathbb{P}\!\left(X\lt \tfrac54\right)=\tfrac{1}{32}\).
(a) Determine \(a\) and \(c\). (b) Determine \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt 4)\). (c) Determine the median and the mode.

(a) Find a and c — two equations

Equation 1 (normalisation): \(\displaystyle\int_0^5 c\,x^a\,dx = \frac{c\,5^{a+1}}{a+1}=1.\)

Equation 2 (probability condition): \(\displaystyle\int_0^{5/4} c\,x^a\,dx = \frac{c\,(5/4)^{a+1}}{a+1}=\frac{1}{32}.\)

Divide Eq 2 by Eq 1 to eliminate \(c\) and \(a+1\): $$\frac{(5/4)^{a+1}}{5^{a+1}}=\left(\frac14\right)^{a+1}=\frac{1}{32}.$$ So \(4^{a+1}=32\Rightarrow 2^{2(a+1)}=2^{5}\Rightarrow 2(a+1)=5\Rightarrow \boxed{a=\tfrac32}.\)

Substitute into Eq 1: \(c=\dfrac{a+1}{5^{a+1}}=\dfrac{2.5}{5^{2.5}}=\dfrac{2.5}{25\sqrt5}=\dfrac{\sqrt5}{50}\approx \boxed{0.0447}.\)

(b) P(X > 4)

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{P}(X\gt4)=\int_4^5 c\,x^{3/2}\,dx=\frac{c}{2.5}\big(5^{2.5}-4^{2.5}\big)=1-\frac{c\,4^{2.5}}{2.5}=1-\frac{32\sqrt5}{125}\approx \boxed{0.428}.\)

(c) Median and mode

Median: solve \(F(m)=\dfrac{c\,m^{2.5}}{2.5}=0.5\Rightarrow m^{2.5}=\dfrac{1.25}{c}=27.95\Rightarrow m=5\cdot 2^{-2/5}\approx \boxed{3.79}.\)

Mode: \(f(x)=c\,x^{3/2}\) is increasing on \([0,5]\) (since \(a\gt0\)), so the density peaks at the right endpoint: \(\text{mode}=\boxed{5}.\)

Recipe applies to any \(c x^a\): (1) integrate to 1; (2) use the given probability as a second equation; (3) divide to kill \(c\); (4) then median from \(F\), mode from the shape of \(f\).

Example 2 — pdf \(f(x)=k\,x^3\): find k, E, Var, cdf, median (2023 exam Q1, 15 marks)
\(X\) has pdf \(f(x)=k\,x^{3}\) for \(1\le x\le 2\), \(k\gt0\). Find (a) \(k\); (b) \(\mathbb{E}(X)\); (c) \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\); (d) the cdf; (e) the median.

(a) k

\(\displaystyle\int_1^2 k x^3\,dx = k\cdot\frac{2^4-1^4}{4}=\frac{15k}{4}=1\Rightarrow k=\frac{4}{15}\approx 0.2667.\)

(b) E(X)

\(\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X)=\int_1^2 x\cdot k x^3\,dx=k\cdot\frac{2^5-1}{5}=\frac{4}{15}\cdot\frac{31}{5}=\frac{124}{75}\approx 1.653.\)

(c) Var(X)

\(\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^2)=\int_1^2 x^2\cdot k x^3\,dx=k\cdot\frac{2^6-1}{6}=\frac{4}{15}\cdot\frac{63}{6}=\frac{252}{90}=2.8.\)

\(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-\mathbb{E}(X)^2=2.8-\left(\tfrac{124}{75}\right)^2\approx 2.8-2.7335=0.0665\) (sd \(\approx 0.258\)).

(d) cdf

For \(1\le x\le 2\): \(\displaystyle F(x)=\int_1^x k u^3\,du=\frac{k}{4}(x^4-1)=\frac{x^4-1}{15}.\) Full definition: $$F(x)=\begin{cases}0 & x\lt 1\\[2pt] \dfrac{x^4-1}{15} & 1\le x\le 2\\[4pt] 1 & x\gt 2.\end{cases}$$

(e) median

\(\dfrac{m^4-1}{15}=\tfrac12\Rightarrow m^4=8.5\Rightarrow m=8.5^{1/4}\approx 1.707.\)

Example 3 — Binomial "at least" with the complement trick + a Normal question
A fair-ish process: 10% of a large population are left-handed. We select \(n=100\) people. (a) What is \(\mathbb{P}(X=3)\) if instead \(X\sim\text{Bin}(10,\tfrac14)\)? (b) For the left-handers, find \(\mathbb{P}(\text{at least }15)\). (c) Female heights \(\sim\mathcal N(168,36)\) cm². What fraction of women are taller than 180 cm? (Notes Ch 2 exercises & Example 2.1.)

(a) Exact binomial term

\(X\sim\text{Bin}(10,0.25)\): \(\mathbb{P}(X=3)=\binom{10}{3}(0.25)^3(0.75)^7=120\cdot0.015625\cdot0.13348\approx 0.250.\) Also \(\mathbb{E}(X)=np=2.5\), \(\text{sd}=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{1.875}\approx1.369.\)

(b) At least 15 — complement

\(X\sim\text{Bin}(100,0.10)\), so \(\mathbb{P}(X\ge15)=1-\mathbb{P}(X\le14)\). Exact value \(\approx 0.0726.\) The notes intend a Normal approximation: \(\mu=np=10\), \(\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=3\), giving \(\mathbb{P}(X\ge15)\approx \mathbb{P}\!\left(Z\ge\tfrac{15-10}{3}\right)=1-\Phi(1.667)\approx 0.048\) (or \(0.067\) with a continuity correction at 14.5 — closest to exact).

(c) Standardise the Normal

Variance 36 \(\Rightarrow \sigma=6\). \(\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\gt180)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(Z\gt\frac{180-168}{6}\right)=\mathbb{P}(Z\gt2)=1-\Phi(2)\approx 0.0228.\)

"At least / at most" with a fixed count → binomial + complement. "Normally distributed … taller than" → standardise to \(Z\) and read the table.

Example 4 — Bayes / conditional probability (car faults, 2022 exam Q1, 11 marks)
For a car model in its first 5 years: \(\mathbb{P}(\text{engine fault } E)=0.05\), \(\mathbb{P}(\text{transmission fault } T)=0.02\), \(\mathbb{P}(E\cup T)=0.06\). (b) \(\mathbb{P}(E\mid T)\); (c) \(\mathbb{P}(E\mid T^c)\); (d) \(\mathbb{P}(T\mid E)\).

First get the overlap (addition rule)

\(\mathbb{P}(E\cap T)=\mathbb{P}(E)+\mathbb{P}(T)-\mathbb{P}(E\cup T)=0.05+0.02-0.06=0.01.\)

(b) faulty engine given faulty transmission

\(\mathbb{P}(E\mid T)=\dfrac{\mathbb{P}(E\cap T)}{\mathbb{P}(T)}=\dfrac{0.01}{0.02}=0.5.\)

(c) faulty engine given transmission passes

\(\mathbb{P}(E\cap T^c)=\mathbb{P}(E)-\mathbb{P}(E\cap T)=0.05-0.01=0.04\); \(\mathbb{P}(T^c)=0.98\).
\(\mathbb{P}(E\mid T^c)=\dfrac{0.04}{0.98}\approx 0.0408.\)

(d) faulty transmission given faulty engine

\(\mathbb{P}(T\mid E)=\dfrac{0.01}{0.05}=0.2.\)

Set up a \(2\times2\) grid of \(\{E,E^c\}\times\{T,T^c\}\) and every conditional is one cell over a margin. The classic full-Bayes version (notes Ex 1.7): three factories with faulty rates give \(\mathbb{P}(\text{factory 1}\mid\text{faulty})=\dfrac{0.15\cdot0.01}{0.15\cdot0.01+0.35\cdot0.05+0.5\cdot0.02}\approx 0.0517.\)

Example 5 — piecewise pdf: h, cdf, E, sd, IQR (2021 exam Q1)
\(f(x)=h\) for \(0\le x\lt1\), \(f(x)=h/2\) for \(1\le x\le2\), else 0. Find (a) \(h\); (b) cdf; (c) \(\mathbb{E}(X)\); (d) sd; (e) IQR.

(a) h

Area = \(h\cdot1 + \tfrac{h}{2}\cdot1=\tfrac{3h}{2}=1\Rightarrow h=\tfrac23.\)

(b) cdf (integrate piece by piece)

$$F(x)=\begin{cases}0 & x\lt0\\[2pt]\tfrac23 x & 0\le x\lt1\\[2pt]\tfrac23+\tfrac13(x-1) & 1\le x\le2\\[2pt]1 & x\gt2.\end{cases}$$

(c) E(X)

\(\mathbb{E}(X)=\int_0^1 \tfrac23 x\,dx+\int_1^2 \tfrac13 x\,dx=\tfrac13+\tfrac12=\tfrac56\approx0.833.\)

(d) variance & sd

\(\mathbb{E}(X^2)=\int_0^1\tfrac23 x^2+\int_1^2\tfrac13 x^2=\tfrac29+\tfrac79=1\); \(\operatorname{Var}=1-(\tfrac56)^2=\tfrac{11}{36}\approx0.306\), sd \(\approx0.553.\)

(e) IQR

\(Q_1\): \(\tfrac23 x=0.25\Rightarrow x=0.375.\) \(Q_3\): since \(F(1)=\tfrac23\lt0.75\), use the second piece: \(\tfrac23+\tfrac13(x-1)=0.75\Rightarrow x=1.25.\) So \(\text{IQR}=1.25-0.375=0.875.\)

Example 6 — pure counting → probability (notes §1.5)
(i) A 20-question, 3-choice exam: how many ways to complete it? (ii) In how many ways can 8 horses place 1st/2nd/3rd? (iii) Probability that 40 people all have different birthdays.

(i) Ordered, with replacement: \(3^{20}=3\,486\,784\,401.\)

(ii) Ordered, without replacement: \({}^8P_3=8\cdot7\cdot6=336.\)

(iii) \(\Omega=365^{40}\) equally-likely birthday vectors; favourable \(={}^{365}P_{40}\). So \(\mathbb{P}=\dfrac{{}^{365}P_{40}}{365^{40}}\approx 0.109\) — surprisingly small.

Decide order/replacement first, pick the box from the \(2\times2\) counting table, then apply \(\mathbb{P}(A)=|A|/|\Omega|\).

§4 Exam question types

Every Chapter 1–2 style seen across the 2021–2025 papers, with the recipe and a real citation.

Type A ★ Continuous RV with pdf \(f(x)=c\,x^a\) (the flagship)

Looks like: "\(X\) has pdf \(f(x)=c x^a\) on \([0,L]\), and \(\mathbb{P}(X\lt k)=\)(given). Find \(c,a\); then \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt\cdot)\), median, mode, \(\mathbb{E}\), \(\operatorname{Var}\), cdf."
Marks: 10–15 (a whole Question 1).

Recipe:
  1. Set \(\int_{\text{support}} f = 1\) (Equation 1).
  2. Use the given probability as Equation 2.
  3. Divide Eq 2 / Eq 1 to cancel \(c\) → solve for the exponent; back-substitute for \(c\).
  4. \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt k)=1-F(k)\); build \(F\) by integrating.
  5. Median: solve \(F(m)=0.5\). Mode: maximise \(f\) (check endpoints if monotone).
  6. \(\mathbb{E}=\int x f\), \(\mathbb{E}(X^2)=\int x^2 f\), \(\operatorname{Var}=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-\mathbb{E}(X)^2\).

Real examples: 2025 Q1 (\(cx^a\), find \(a,c\), \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt4)\), median & mode) — see Worked Ex 1. 2023 Q1 (\(kx^3\): \(k\), \(\mathbb{E}\), \(\operatorname{Var}\), cdf, median) — Worked Ex 2. 2021 Q1 (piecewise pdf: \(h\), cdf, \(\mathbb{E}\), sd, IQR) — Worked Ex 5. This type has appeared as Q1 in every recent paper.

Type B — Conditional probability / Bayes / probability rules

Looks like: word problem with given marginal and/or joint probabilities; asks for a conditional in the reversed direction, or "define events, sample space and probabilities."
Marks: ~11.

Recipe:
  1. Name events with letters; translate every sentence into \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot)\).
  2. Use addition rule to recover \(\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\) if only \(\mathbb{P}(A\cup B)\) is given.
  3. Apply \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)/\mathbb{P}(B)\), or Bayes / Law of Total Probability for reversed conditionals.

Real example: 2022 Q1 (engine/transmission faults) — Worked Ex 4. Template Bayes: notes Example 1.7 (three factories).

Type C — Binomial probabilities ("at least / at most")

Looks like: fixed number \(n\) of independent yes/no trials, constant \(p\); "probability of exactly / at least / at most \(k\) successes", or \(\mathbb{E}\)/sd of the count.
Marks: 2–4 within a larger question.

Recipe:
  1. Identify \(n\) and \(p\); confirm independence & constant \(p\).
  2. Exactly \(k\): \(\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\).
  3. "At least one" → \(1-\mathbb{P}(X=0)\); "at least \(k\)" → \(1-\mathbb{P}(X\le k-1)\).
  4. \(\mathbb{E}=np,\ \operatorname{Var}=np(1-p)\).

Real example: notes Ch 2 exercise 2 (\(\text{Bin}(10,\tfrac14)\): \(\mathbb{E}\), sd, \(\mathbb{P}(X=3)\), \(\mathbb{P}(X\le3)\)); coin-fairness \(\mathbb{P}(X\ge60)\) for \(\text{Bin}(100,0.5)\approx0.0284\) (Example 1.1). Left-hander count in Worked Ex 3.

Type D — Normal probability & quantiles ("heights are normally distributed")

Looks like: "\(X\) is normally distributed with mean … and variance/sd …; find \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt\cdot)\)" or "find the value exceeded 10% of the time / the 95th percentile / the mean that achieves a target probability."
Marks: 2–5.

Recipe:
  1. Read off \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) (take \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) if variance is given!).
  2. Forward: standardise \(Z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\), read \(\Phi\) from the table; use \(1-\Phi\) / symmetry as needed.
  3. Reverse: find \(z\) with \(\Phi(z)=\)target, then \(x=\mu+z\sigma\).

Real examples: notes Example 2.1 (female vs male heights, \(Z=2\) vs \(Z=3\)); Ch 2 exercise 5 (Brisbane temp \(\mathcal N(32,2^2)\): \(\mathbb{P}(\gt36)\approx0.0228\); \(\mu\) for a 10% chance \(\approx 33.44\)); exercise 3 (95% quantile of \(\mathcal N(3,4)\) \(=3+1.645\cdot2\approx6.29\)).

Type E — Normal approximation / CLT

Looks like: "use the central limit theorem to approximate the probability that fewer than … " a large count or sum; "expected number of outliers from a standard normal."
Marks: 6–8.

Recipe:
  1. Identify the sum/count and its mean and variance (for a binomial count, \(\mu=np,\ \sigma^2=np(1-p)\)).
  2. Approximate by \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), standardise, read the table.

Real examples: 2021 Q2(f) ("fewer than 130 go to the beach both days" via CLT); 2022 Q2(b) (expected outliers: \(\text{IQR}=1.349\), fence probability \(\approx0.00698\), so \(n=10/0.00698\approx1433\) to expect 10 outliers).

Type F — Counting → probability

Looks like: "how many ways…", "probability that … all different / a specific hand". Marks: 2–4 (sometimes embedded).

Recipe: choose order/replacement box → count \(|\Omega|\) and \(|A|\) → \(\mathbb{P}=|A|/|\Omega|\).

Real example: notes §1.5 problems (320, \({}^8P_3=336\), \(\binom{20}{3}=1140\); birthday problem \(\approx0.109\); 4 hearts & 3 diamonds \(\approx0.074\)) — Worked Ex 6.

§5 Wording triggers

The flagship feature: exam phrase → the exact method it demands.

When the exam says…Do this
"\(f(x)=c\,x^a\) … determine \(c\)" (or \(k\), \(h\))Integrate \(f\) over the support and set \(=1\). Solve for the constant.
"… and \(\mathbb{P}(X\lt k)=\)(value)" (a second condition)Write a second integral \(\int_0^k f = \)value. Divide the two equations to cancel the constant and solve for the exponent \(a\).
"Determine \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt k)\)"\(1-F(k)=1-\int_{\text{low}}^{k} f\). For "\(\lt k\)" just integrate up to \(k\).
"Determine the median"Build the cdf \(F\); solve \(F(m)=0.5\).
"Find \(Q_1\), \(Q_3\), the IQR / a percentile"Solve \(F(x)=0.25,\ 0.75\) (or \(p\)); IQR \(=Q_3-Q_1\). Watch which cdf piece the quantile lands in.
"Determine the mode"Maximise the density \(f\): set \(f'=0\), or take the endpoint if \(f\) is monotone (e.g. \(c x^a\), \(a\gt0\) ⇒ mode at right end).
"Determine the cumulative distribution function"Integrate the pdf from the lower support to \(x\); state the full piecewise \(F\) (0 below, 1 above).
"Determine \(\mathbb{E}(X)\) / \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) / standard deviation"\(\mathbb{E}=\int x f\); \(\operatorname{Var}=\int x^2 f-(\mathbb{E}X)^2\); sd \(=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}}\).
"\(Y=aX+b\) … find \(\mathbb{E}(Y)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)\)"\(\mathbb{E}(aX+b)=a\mathbb{E}X+b\); \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}X\) (shift disappears, scale squares).
"at least / at most … (a count of successes)"Binomial. "At least one" ⇒ \(1-\mathbb{P}(X=0)\); "at least \(k\)" ⇒ \(1-\mathbb{P}(X\le k-1)\) (complement trick).
"\(n\) independent trials, each with probability \(p\)"\(X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)\); \(\mathbb{E}=np\), \(\operatorname{Var}=np(1-p)\).
"heights / weights are normally distributed", "what proportion exceeds…"Standardise \(Z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) and read the \(\Phi\) table. If given variance, take \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) for \(\sigma\).
"the value exceeded only 10% of the time", "95th percentile", "what \(\mu\) gives probability 0.10"Reverse Normal: find \(z\) with \(\Phi(z)=1-\alpha\), then \(x=\mu+z\sigma\) (or solve for \(\mu\)).
"chosen at random / equally likely over an interval"Uniform \(\mathcal U[a,b]\): \(f=\frac{1}{b-a}\), \(\mathbb{E}=\frac{a+b}{2}\), \(\operatorname{Var}=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}\).
"given that the test is positive / the transmission failed…"Conditional probability; if the direction is reversed from what's given, use Bayes' Rule.
"produced by factory 1, 2, or 3…", "one of several scenarios"Law of Total Probability to combine scenarios; Bayes to invert.
"use the central limit theorem to approximate…"Sum/count ≈ \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\); standardise and read the table.
"how many ways / probability all different / a specific hand"Counting: pick order/replacement box, then \(\mathbb{P}=|A|/|\Omega|\).
"give your answer to at least 3 significant figures"Keep full precision through working; round only the final number to ≥3 s.f.

§6 Traps & marks lost

Variance vs standard deviation in \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). "Variance 36" means \(\sigma=6\), not 36. The notation carries the variance; R/tables want the sd. Halting here loses the whole Normal part.
Forgetting to divide the two equations. On \(f=cx^a\), students try to solve normalisation and the probability condition simultaneously and get stuck. Dividing instantly cancels \(c\) and \(a+1\), leaving one clean equation in \(a\).
Computing \((\mathbb{E}X)^2\) instead of \(\mathbb{E}(X^2)\). The variance shortcut needs the integral \(\int x^2 f\,dx\) as its own quantity. Squaring the mean is a different (wrong) number.
Median ≠ mode ≠ mean. Median solves \(F(m)=0.5\) (area); mode maximises \(f\) (height); mean is \(\int x f\). For a skewed \(cx^a\) density these three differ — using the wrong one loses easy marks.
Incomplete cdf. A cdf answer that only gives the formula on the support is incomplete. State \(F(x)=0\) for \(x\) below the support and \(F(x)=1\) above it.
Quartile in the wrong cdf piece. For piecewise densities, check whether \(F\) has already passed the target probability before the boundary (e.g. \(F(1)=\tfrac23\gt0.5\)) — otherwise you solve on the wrong segment.
Mode of a monotone density. For \(f=cx^a\) with \(a\gt0\), \(f'\) is never zero on \((0,L)\); the maximum is at the endpoint \(x=L\). Setting \(f'=0\) yields \(x=0\) (a minimum) — the opposite of the mode.
Independent vs mutually exclusive. Disjoint events (\(A\cap B=\varnothing\)) with positive probability are dependent. Independence is \(\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\), which is generally non-zero.
Order/replacement mix-up in counting. A committee is unordered (\(\binom{n}{k}\)); a ranked podium is ordered (\({}^nP_k\)). Choosing the wrong box changes the count by a factor of \(k!\).
"At least" without the complement. Summing \(\mathbb{P}(X=15)+\dots+\mathbb{P}(X=100)\) by hand is a Casio nightmare. Use \(1-\mathbb{P}(X\le14)\).
Premature rounding. The instruction asks for ≥3 s.f. Rounding \(c\) or \(z\) early and feeding it forward corrupts the final answer. Carry exact fractions/surds (e.g. \(c=\sqrt5/50\)) until the last step.
Continuity correction judgement (CLT of a discrete count). Approximating \(\mathbb{P}(X\ge15)\) by \(\Phi\) can be improved by using \(14.5\); know that the un-corrected answer (\(0.048\)) and the corrected one (\(0.067\)) differ, and the corrected value is closer to the exact \(0.073\).

§7 Self-check

Attempt before revealing. Numbers verified by computation.

Q1. \(X\) has pdf \(f(x)=c\,x^2\) on \([0,3]\). Find \(c\) and \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt2)\).

\(\int_0^3 c x^2 = 9c=1\Rightarrow c=\tfrac19\). \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt2)=\int_2^3 \tfrac19 x^2\,dx=\tfrac{1}{27}(27-8)=\tfrac{19}{27}\approx0.704.\)

Q2. For \(f(x)=kx^3\) on \([1,2]\) (Worked Ex 2), what is the mode?

\(f\) is increasing on \([1,2]\) (since \(x^3\uparrow\)), so the mode is the right endpoint \(x=2\).

Q3. \(X\sim\text{Bin}(10,\tfrac14)\). Find \(\mathbb{E}(X)\), sd, and \(\mathbb{P}(X\le3)\).

\(\mathbb{E}=np=2.5\); sd \(=\sqrt{10\cdot0.25\cdot0.75}=\sqrt{1.875}\approx1.369\); \(\mathbb{P}(X\le3)\approx0.776.\)

Q4. Adult IQ \(\sim\mathcal N(100,15^2)\). What proportion score above 130?

\(Z=(130-100)/15=2\); \(\mathbb{P}(Z\gt2)=1-\Phi(2)\approx0.0228\) (about 2.3%).

Q5. Brisbane December max temp \(\sim\mathcal N(32,2^2)\). Above which temperature do only 10% of days fall?

Need \(z\) with \(\Phi(z)=0.90\Rightarrow z\approx1.2816\). \(x=32+1.2816\cdot2\approx34.56\)°C.

Q6. \(\mathbb{P}(A)=0.6,\ \mathbb{P}(B)=0.5,\ \mathbb{P}(A\cup B)=0.8\). Find \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)\) and state whether \(A,B\) are independent.

\(\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=0.6+0.5-0.8=0.3\). \(\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=0.3/0.5=0.6=\mathbb{P}(A)\) ⇒ independent (also \(0.6\cdot0.5=0.3=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\)).

Q7. A disease affects 1% of people. A test is 99% sensitive and 95% specific. Given a positive test, what is the probability of disease? (Bayes)

\(\mathbb{P}(D\mid+)=\dfrac{0.99\cdot0.01}{0.99\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99}=\dfrac{0.0099}{0.0099+0.0495}=\dfrac{0.0099}{0.0594}\approx0.167.\) Only ~17% despite the "accurate" test — the base rate dominates.

Q8. \(X\sim\mathcal U[2,8]\). Find \(\mathbb{E}(X)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\), and \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt5)\).

\(\mathbb{E}=\tfrac{2+8}{2}=5\); \(\operatorname{Var}=\tfrac{(8-2)^2}{12}=\tfrac{36}{12}=3\); \(\mathbb{P}(X\gt5)=\tfrac{8-5}{8-2}=\tfrac36=0.5.\)

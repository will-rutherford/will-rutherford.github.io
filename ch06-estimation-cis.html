1 · Concepts in depth

New to \(t_{n-1}\), \(\chi^2\), or quantile notation? Read the Ground Floor first — it explains sampling distributions, standard error vs SD, and where every reference distribution below comes from.

The whole point of this chapter

You have data. Behind the data is a model with unknown parameters — a mean \(\mu\), a variance \(\sigma^2\), a proportion \(p\). You will never know the true value. The best you can do is (1) produce a single best guess (a point estimate), and (2) attach an honest statement of how accurate that guess is (a confidence interval). Chapter 6 is the machinery for both. Everything in Ch 7–11 (testing, ANOVA, regression) is built on these two ideas.

Estimate vs estimator — the distinction examiners love

This is the first thing the notes (§6.2) drill, and it underpins the whole idea of a standard error.

  • Estimate = a number computed from the data you actually have. Example: \(\bar x = 75.975\). Lower case, fixed, already observed.
  • Estimator = a random variable — the same formula but written in terms of the random data \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) you would get if you ran the experiment again tomorrow. Example: \(\bar X = \tfrac1n\sum X_i\). Upper case, random, has a distribution.

Notation: the estimate/estimator of a parameter \(\theta\) is written \(\widehat\theta\). So \(\widehat\mu = \bar X\) and \(\widehat{\sigma^2}=S^2\).

The estimator has a distribution, so we can talk about its mean and spread. That is exactly what lets us say how good an estimate is. A CI is just "estimate ± (a multiple of the estimator's spread)".

Bias — is the estimator right on average?

\[\text{Bias}(\widehat\theta)=\mathbb E(\widehat\theta)-\theta.\] An estimator is unbiased if \(\mathbb E(\widehat\theta)=\theta\) (bias \(=0\)): on average, across infinitely many repeats of the experiment, it lands on the truth.

The sample mean is unbiased: \(\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu\) (notes §6.2). The sample variance \(S^2\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\) — and this is why we divide by \(n-1\), not \(n\).

Why \(n-1\)? (Tutorial 6 Q1, and a classic exam-style derivation.) The "natural" estimator \(\widehat{\sigma^2}_{\text{MLE}}=\tfrac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\) has \(\mathbb E(\widehat{\sigma^2}_{\text{MLE}})=\tfrac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\), so its bias is \(-\sigma^2/n\) — it is systematically too small. Multiplying by \(\tfrac{n}{n-1}\) fixes it, giving \(S^2=\tfrac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\). Intuition: \(\bar X\) is fitted to the data, so deviations from \(\bar X\) are a touch smaller than deviations from the true \(\mu\); dividing by \(n-1\) compensates.

Standard error — how much does the estimator wobble?

The standard error is the standard deviation of the estimator. From \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\) we get \[\operatorname{s.e.}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\;\xrightarrow{\text{estimate }\sigma\text{ by }S}\;\frac{S}{\sqrt n}.\] Bigger \(n\) \(\Rightarrow\) smaller standard error \(\Rightarrow\) sharper estimate. The \(\sqrt n\) is why cutting the CI width in half costs four times the sample size — this is the engine of every sample-size question.

Where does \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\) come from? It is the variance of the sampling distribution of \(\bar X\) — see the Ground Floor derivation (averaging \(n\) independent numbers divides the variance by \(n\)). That shrinkage is the whole reason more data buys precision.

Standard error (spread of the estimator \(\bar X\), \(=S/\sqrt n\)) is NOT the sample standard deviation (spread of the data, \(=S\)). Confusing the two — forgetting to divide by \(\sqrt n\) — is the single most common CI mistake.

What a confidence interval actually means

A 95% CI is a random interval \((T_1,T_2)\) with the property \(\mathbb P(T_1\lt\theta\lt T_2)\ge 0.95\) (Definition 6.1). The endpoints are random (they depend on the data); the parameter \(\theta\) is a fixed unknown constant.

Once you plug in your data you get a numerical interval like \((-6.63,-5.35)\). It is wrong to say "there is a 95% probability \(\mu\) lies in \((-6.63,-5.35)\)" — \(\mu\) is either in it or not; there is no randomness left. The correct reading: if we repeated the whole study many times, about 95% of the intervals produced this way would capture the true \(\mu\). (Notes' urn analogy: 90 white, 10 black balls; you draw one without looking — you are 90% confident it is white.)

Exam-safe interpretation template: "We are 95% confident that the population [parameter, in words] lies between [lo] and [hi] [units]." Use "confident", never "probability".
A CI is a statement about the population mean \(\mu\), NOT about individual observations. "95% of low-carb dieters lost between 6.63 and 5.35 kg" is wrong — the interval is narrow only because \(\bar X\) wobbles little (\(S/\sqrt n\)); individual people vary far more (\(S\)). The interval that catches 95% of individuals is a prediction interval, which is much wider and does not shrink to 0 as \(n\to\infty\).

How every CI is built (the pivot)

Standardise the estimator into a quantity whose distribution has no unknown parameters — a pivot — then trap it between two quantiles and rearrange for \(\theta\). For the mean: \[\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbb P\!\left(-q\lt \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\lt q\right)=1-\alpha\quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar X\pm q\,\frac{S}{\sqrt n}.\] Every recipe below is the same three moves — only the pivot's distribution (\(t\), \(\chi^2\), or \(N(0,1)\)) changes. This is the universal CI machine.

The half-width \(q\cdot\operatorname{s.e.}\) is the margin of error. Larger confidence \(\Rightarrow\) larger \(q\) \(\Rightarrow\) wider interval. Common \(\alpha\): 0.10, 0.05, 0.01.

Two dials, and you can't cheat. Raise the confidence level (95%→99%) → bigger \(q\) → wider interval: being more sure costs precision. Raise \(n\) → smaller s.e. \(=S/\sqrt n\) → narrower interval. Only more data tightens the interval without sacrificing confidence — that is what every sample-size question is buying.
The mean CI derivation, one algebra step at a time (mirrors the variance derivation in the Ground Floor)
Start from the pivot — a quantity with a completely known distribution (no \(\mu\), no \(\sigma\) left inside it): \[\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}.\] Step 1 — trap it. Because \(t_{n-1}\) is symmetric about 0, the middle \(1-\alpha\) sits between \(\pm q\) with \(q=t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\): \[\mathbb P\!\left(-q\lt\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\lt q\right)=1-\alpha.\] Step 2 — multiply all three parts by \(S/\sqrt n\) (positive, so inequality directions stay): \[\mathbb P\!\left(-q\,\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\lt \bar X-\mu\lt q\,\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\right)=1-\alpha.\] Step 3 — subtract \(\bar X\) from all three parts: \[\mathbb P\!\left(-\bar X-q\,\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\lt -\mu\lt -\bar X+q\,\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\right)=1-\alpha.\] Step 4 — multiply by \(-1\), which flips both inequalities; reorder left-to-right: \[\mathbb P\!\left(\bar X-q\,\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\lt \mu\lt \bar X+q\,\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\right)=1-\alpha.\] That bracket is the interval: \(\;\bar X\pm q\,S/\sqrt n\).
Notice \(\mu\) never moved — we only rearranged to isolate it. The randomness lives entirely in \(\bar X\) and \(S\), so it is the endpoints that are random and \(\mu\) that is fixed. That is the algebra behind the "confidence, not probability" rule. Contrast the variance CI, where reciprocating the pivot flips the quantiles instead.

Approximate vs exact — and the CLT

Two engines produce CIs for a mean:

  • Exact — when the data are normal, \((\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\) is exactly \(t_{n-1}\), so the CI is exact for any \(n\).
  • Approximate — when the data are not normal but \(n\) is large, the Central Limit Theorem makes \(\bar X\) approximately normal anyway, so the same interval works approximately.

Crucially, the provided formula sheet (item 62) uses the \(t_{n-1}\) quantile for BOTH cases. For large \(n\), \(t_{n-1}\approx N(0,1)\) (e.g. \(t_{303}=1.97\approx1.96\); why the \(t\) fattens then converges), so it barely matters — but on this exam you can always reach for the \(t\) row. Proportion CIs are the exception (they use the normal quantile — see below).

① One-sample CI for a mean \(\mu\)  (item 62)

\[\bar X \pm q\,\frac{S}{\sqrt n},\qquad q = \text{the }1-\tfrac\alpha2\text{ quantile of } t_{n-1}.\]

One sample of measurements; you want a CI for its population mean. This is the workhorse — it is exact if the data are normal and a good approximation for large \(n\) by the CLT. \(\sigma\) is unknown (estimated by \(S\)), so use the \(t\) quantile.

② One-sample CI for a variance \(\sigma^2\)  (item 63)

\[\left(\frac{(n-1)S^2}{q_2},\;\frac{(n-1)S^2}{q_1}\right),\quad q_1=\tfrac\alpha2\text{ quantile},\;q_2=1-\tfrac\alpha2\text{ quantile of }\chi^2_{n-1}.\]

Normal data, one sample, and the question asks about variability — a CI for \(\sigma^2\) (variance) or \(\sigma\) (standard deviation). For a CI on \(\sigma\), take the square root of both endpoints. Uses \(\chi^2\) because \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\).
The small quantile \(q_1\) sits under the upper bound, and the large quantile \(q_2\) under the lower bound. Cross them and your interval comes out backwards.
Why this interval is not symmetric (unlike the mean CI, which is \(\bar X\pm\text{something}\)). The mean's pivot is symmetric \(t\), so its two quantiles are mirror images \(\pm q\). The variance pivot \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\) is right-skewed — a sum of squares can't dip below 0 but has a long upper tail — so \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are not mirror images, and \(s^2\) is not at the centre of the interval. The slow \(\chi^2\) derivation shows exactly why the reciprocal step lands the big quantile under the small bound.

③ Two-sample CI for \(\mu_X-\mu_Y\) — equal variances (pooled)  (items 64–65)

\[\bar X-\bar Y \pm q\,S_p\sqrt{\tfrac1m+\tfrac1n},\qquad S_p^2=\frac{(m-1)S_X^2+(n-1)S_Y^2}{m+n-2},\qquad q = t_{m+n-2}.\]

Two independent groups, comparing their means, and it is reasonable to assume the two populations have the same variance (similar sample SDs, similar boxplot spreads). Pool the two variances into \(S_p^2\); df \(=m+n-2\).

④ Two-sample CI for \(\mu_X-\mu_Y\) — unequal variances (Welch)  (item 66)

\[\bar X-\bar Y \pm q\,\sqrt{\tfrac{S_X^2}{m}+\tfrac{S_Y^2}{n}},\qquad q = t_{\text{df}}.\]

Welch df (item 66/75): \(\displaystyle \text{df}=\frac{\left(\tfrac{s_X^2}{m}+\tfrac{s_Y^2}{n}\right)^2}{\tfrac{1}{m-1}\left(\tfrac{s_X^2}{m}\right)^2+\tfrac{1}{n-1}\left(\tfrac{s_Y^2}{n}\right)^2}\).   Conservative shortcut: \(\text{df}=\min(m-1,\,n-1)\).

Two independent groups whose spreads look clearly different (unequal sample SDs). No pooling. If df isn't given, use the conservative \(\min(m-1,n-1)\) and say so.

⑤ One-sample CI for a proportion \(p\)  (item 67)

\[\widehat p \pm q\,\sqrt{\frac{\widehat p\,(1-\widehat p)}{n}},\qquad \widehat p=\frac{X}{n},\quad q = \text{the }1-\tfrac\alpha2\text{ quantile of }N(0,1).\]

Binary / yes-no data — a count \(X\) of "successes" out of \(n\). Uses the normal quantile (from the CLT for a binomial), not \(t\). Validity check: \(n\widehat p\ge5\) AND \(n(1-\widehat p)\ge5\) (at least 5 successes and 5 failures).

⑥ Two-sample CI for \(p_X-p_Y\)  (item 68)

\[\widehat p_X-\widehat p_Y \pm q\,\sqrt{\frac{\widehat p_X(1-\widehat p_X)}{m}+\frac{\widehat p_Y(1-\widehat p_Y)}{n}},\qquad q = 1-\tfrac\alpha2\text{ quantile of }N(0,1).\]

Comparing two proportions (two groups, each yes/no). If the CI contains 0, there is no evidence the two proportions differ.

⑦ Margin of error & sample size

The margin of error is the \(\pm\) half-width: \(\text{ME}=q\cdot\operatorname{s.e.}\) To guarantee \(\text{ME}\le E\), solve for \(n\):

  • Mean: \(q\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le E \;\Rightarrow\; n\ge\left(\dfrac{q\,\sigma}{E}\right)^2\)  (use \(q=z=1.96\) for 95%, and a supplied/estimated \(\sigma\)).
  • Proportion: \(n\ge\left(\dfrac{q}{2E}\right)^2\) using the worst case \(p(1-p)\le\tfrac14\) (max at \(p=0.5\)).
Always round UP to the next whole number — rounding down would fail the accuracy requirement.

⑧ (Rare) known-\(\sigma\) mean CI — uses \(z\)

\[\bar X \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.\]

Only when the problem explicitly hands you the true population \(\sigma\) (a theory/tutorial setup — Tutorial 6 Q3). Because \(\sigma\) is known you use the normal quantile instead of \(t\). If \(\sigma\) is estimated from the sample (the normal case), fall back to recipe ①.

WE1 — One-sample mean CI + interpret (2025 exam Q3(a), 4 marks) ★ the flagship
Gardner et al. (2018): 304 participants on a low-carb diet had a mean 12-month weight change of \(\bar x=-5.99\) kg with \(s=5.69\) kg. Calculate a 95% confidence interval for the low-carb population mean weight change (kg). Report to two decimal places and interpret in the context of the study.
Recognise: one sample, mean of measurements → recipe ① (\(t_{n-1}\), item 62).
  1. \(n=304\), df \(=303\). Quantile \(q=t_{0.975,303}\). Table: \(t_{200}=1.97\), \(t_\infty=1.96\) → use \(1.97\) (exact \(1.9679\)).
  2. Standard error: \(S/\sqrt n = 5.69/\sqrt{304}=0.3263\).
  3. Margin of error: \(q\cdot\operatorname{s.e.}=1.97\times0.3263=0.64\).
  4. CI: \(-5.99\pm0.64=(-6.63,\,-5.35)\) kg.

Answer (2 dp): \((-6.63,\;-5.35)\) kg.

Interpretation that scores full marks: "We are 95% confident that the true mean 12-month weight change for people on the low-carb diet lies between \(-6.63\) kg and \(-5.35\) kg. Because the entire interval is below 0, there is strong evidence the diet is associated with genuine average weight loss."
Do NOT write "there is a 95% probability the mean is in \((-6.63,-5.35)\)". No marks — the mean is fixed, the interval is what's random.
WE2 — Two-sample mean CI, pooled/equal variances (2023 exam Q4(b), 7 marks)
6 samples of 100g sheep paté: mean \(20.7\) g protein, \(s=0.17\). 6 samples of goat paté: mean \(19.2\) g, \(s=0.17\). Both normal. Construct a 95% CI for the mean difference in protein content. Report to 3 s.f., then give a statistical conclusion and a conclusion in context.
Recognise: two independent groups, comparing means, sample SDs identical → assume equal variances → pooled recipe ③ (\(t_{m+n-2}\)).
  1. Pooled variance: \(S_p^2=\dfrac{(6-1)0.17^2+(6-1)0.17^2}{6+6-2}=0.0289\), so \(S_p=0.17\).
  2. Standard error: \(S_p\sqrt{\tfrac16+\tfrac16}=0.17\times\sqrt{1/3}=0.0981\).
  3. df \(=m+n-2=10\); \(q=t_{0.975,10}=2.23\).
  4. Difference \(\bar x-\bar y=20.7-19.2=1.5\). ME \(=2.23\times0.0981=0.219\).
  5. CI: \(1.5\pm0.219=(1.28,\;1.72)\) g.

Answer (3 s.f.): \((1.28,\;1.72)\) g.

Conclusion: The interval does not contain 0 (it is entirely positive), so at the 5% level there is evidence of a real difference. In context: 100g sheep paté contains, on average, between 1.28 g and 1.72 g more protein than 100g goat paté.
WE3 — CI for a standard deviation \(\sigma\), chi-squared (2024 exam Q3(d), 4 marks)
The differences (forearm − upper-arm systolic BP) for 44 subjects have SD \(s=9.64\) mmHg. Construct a 95% CI for the standard deviation \(\sigma\) of the difference. State any assumptions.
Recognise: one sample, CI for the SD → recipe ② (\(\chi^2_{n-1}\), item 63). Build the CI for \(\sigma^2\) first, then square-root.
  1. \(n=44\), df \(=43\). Quantiles of \(\chi^2_{43}\): \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,43}=26.79\), \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,43}=62.99\). (df 43 isn't on the table — use the nearest row, df 40, or interpolate; nearest-row is accepted.)
  2. \((n-1)S^2 = 43\times9.64^2 = 3995.5\).
  3. CI for \(\sigma^2\): \(\left(\dfrac{3995.5}{62.99},\;\dfrac{3995.5}{26.79}\right)=(63.4,\;149.2)\).
  4. CI for \(\sigma\): square-root → \((\sqrt{63.4},\sqrt{149.2})=(7.97,\;12.21)\) mmHg.

Answer: \(\sigma\in(7.97,\;12.21)\) mmHg (using nearest table row df 40: \((8.21,\,12.80)\) — either is fine if you state which df you used).

Assumption: the differences are an iid sample from a normal distribution. The \(\chi^2\) result is exact only under normality (and, unlike the mean CI, it is NOT rescued by the CLT — so this assumption genuinely matters here).
Don't forget the final square root. The formula gives a CI for the variance; the question asked for the standard deviation.
WE4 — Two-sample proportion CI (notes §6.4 exercise 6: election poll)
In an election poll, 350 of 980 women and 420 of 1108 men said they would vote "Shady Shane". Give a 90% CI for the difference in the proportion of Shady voters among women and men.
Recognise: two groups, yes/no responses → two-sample proportion, recipe ⑥ (\(N(0,1)\)).
  1. \(\widehat p_X=350/980=0.3571\) (women), \(\widehat p_Y=420/1108=0.3791\) (men). Plenty of successes and failures in each → normal approx OK.
  2. 90% → \(\alpha=0.10\); \(q=z_{0.95}=1.645\) (0.95 quantile of \(N(0,1)\)).
  3. SE \(=\sqrt{\dfrac{0.3571(0.6429)}{980}+\dfrac{0.3791(0.6209)}{1108}}=0.0211\).
  4. Difference \(=0.3571-0.3791=-0.0219\). ME \(=1.645\times0.0211=0.0348\).
  5. CI: \(-0.0219\pm0.0348=(-0.057,\;0.013)\).

Answer: \((-0.057,\;0.013)\). The interval contains 0, so there is no evidence at the 10% level that women and men differ in their support for "Shady Shane".

WE5 — Sample-size / margin-of-error (2024 exam Q3(e), 3 marks + proportion version)
(a) A future study wants to estimate the mean forearm−upper-arm BP difference within a 95% margin of error of no more than 1 mmHg, taking \(\sigma\approx s=9.64\). What is the smallest number of subjects needed?
Recognise: "margin of error no more than …" → sample-size, recipe ⑦.
  1. Require \(z\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le 1\) with \(z=1.96\), \(\sigma=9.64\).
  2. Rearrange: \(n\ge\left(\dfrac{1.96\times9.64}{1}\right)^2=(18.894)^2=356.998\).
  3. Round UP: \(n=357\) subjects.

Answer: 357 subjects.

(b) Proportion version (Tutorial 6 Q4): a 95% CI for a proportion with margin of error \(\le 3\%\), no pilot data. How large must \(n\) be?
  1. Worst case: \(p(1-p)\le\tfrac14\) (maximum at \(p=0.5\)), so ME \(\le q\cdot\tfrac12/\sqrt n\).
  2. Set \(1.96\times\dfrac{0.5}{\sqrt n}=0.03 \Rightarrow n=\left(\dfrac{1.96\times0.5}{0.03}\right)^2=1067.1 \Rightarrow n=1068\).
  3. (The rougher \(q\approx2\) gives the neat \(1/\sqrt n=0.03\Rightarrow n=1111\).)

Answer: \(n=1068\) (or \(1111\) with the \(q\approx2\) approximation). You can answer before collecting data because the worst-case \(p(1-p)=\tfrac14\) removes the need for a pilot estimate.

WE6 — One-sample proportion CI + validity check (notes Ex 6.9, opinion poll)
In a poll of 1000 voters, 227 say they will vote Green. Construct a 95% CI for the population proportion \(p\).
Recognise: one sample, yes/no → recipe ⑤.
  1. \(\widehat p=227/1000=0.227\). Check: \(n\widehat p=227\ge5\) and \(n(1-\widehat p)=773\ge5\) ✓.
  2. \(q=z_{0.975}=1.96\). SE \(=\sqrt{0.227(0.773)/1000}=0.0132\).
  3. ME \(=1.96\times0.0132=0.026\). CI: \(0.227\pm0.026=(0.201,\;0.253)\).

Answer: \((0.20,\;0.25)\). We are 95% confident the true Green vote share lies between 20% and 25%.

4 · Exam question types

Every CI-flavoured question STAT1301 has generated, with the mark range, the step recipe, and a real past-paper reference.

Type A — One-sample mean CI + interpret in context

Looks like: summary stats (\(n,\bar x,s\)) given; "calculate a 95% CI for the mean … and interpret in the context of the study". Marks: 3–5.

Recipe:
  1. Recognise one-sample mean → \(t_{n-1}\) (item 62).
  2. \(q=t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\) from the table.
  3. \(\bar x\pm q\,s/\sqrt n\); compute to the requested precision.
  4. One context sentence using "we are 95% confident…"; state whether it excludes a benchmark value.

Seen in: 2025 Q3(a) [4 marks] — low-carb weight change, "report to two decimal places and interpret".

Type B — Two-sample mean CI (difference of means)

Looks like: two groups' summary stats; "CI for the mean difference"; often followed by "state a conclusion". Marks: 5–7.

Recipe:
  1. Two samples of measurements → recipe ③ (equal var, pooled) or ④ (unequal var, Welch). Decide from the sample SDs / plots.
  2. If pooled: compute \(S_p^2\), df \(=m+n-2\). If Welch: use \(\sqrt{S_X^2/m+S_Y^2/n}\), df \(=\) Welch or \(\min(m-1,n-1)\).
  3. \((\bar x-\bar y)\pm q\cdot\operatorname{s.e.}\)
  4. Conclusion: does the interval contain 0?

Seen in: 2023 Q4(b) [7 marks] — sheep vs goat paté protein (equal SDs → pooled).

Type C — CI for a variance \(\sigma^2\) or SD \(\sigma\)

Looks like: "CI for the variance / standard deviation"; the word variation or variability is the tell. Marks: 4.

Recipe:
  1. Normal data, one sample, variability asked → recipe ② (\(\chi^2_{n-1}\)).
  2. Read \(q_1=\chi^2_{\alpha/2}\), \(q_2=\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2}\); mind the ordering.
  3. \(\left((n-1)s^2/q_2,\;(n-1)s^2/q_1\right)\).
  4. If the SD is asked, square-root both ends. State the normality assumption.

Seen in: 2024 Q3(d) [4 marks] — SD of paired BP differences. Notes exercise 7 — juice-bottle variance (\(n=27,s=5.86\)).

Type D — Sample size / margin of error

Looks like: "smallest number of subjects to achieve a margin of error of …"; "what sample size gives a CI no wider than …". Marks: 3.

Recipe:
  1. Write ME \(=q\cdot\operatorname{s.e.}\le E\).
  2. Mean: \(n\ge(q\sigma/E)^2\). Proportion: \(n\ge(q/2E)^2\) via \(p(1-p)\le\tfrac14\).
  3. Round UP.

Seen in: 2024 Q3(e) [3 marks] — \(n=357\) for ME \(\le1\) mmHg. Tutorial 6 Q4 — proportion, ME \(\le3\%\). Notes exercises 3 & 5.

Type E — Proportion CI (one or two samples)

Looks like: counts out of totals ("227 of 1000", "350 of 980"). Marks: 3–5.

Recipe:
  1. Count data → proportion; use the normal quantile.
  2. Check \(\ge5\) successes and \(\ge5\) failures.
  3. \(\widehat p\pm z\sqrt{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)/n}\) (one sample) or the two-\(\widehat p\) version.
  4. Two-sample: contains 0? → no evidence of difference.

Seen in: Notes Ex 6.9 & 6.11; notes exercise 6 (election). Tutorial 6 Q4.

Type F — Estimator properties (bias, variance, distribution)

Looks like: "A suggested estimator of \(\theta\) is \(\widehat\theta=\dots\). Show it is unbiased / find \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta)\) / what is its approximate distribution for large \(n\)?" Marks: 8–12 (often a whole question).

Recipe:
  1. Unbiased? Compute \(\mathbb E(\widehat\theta)\) using linearity of expectation; compare to \(\theta\).
  2. \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta)\): use \(\operatorname{Var}(a+\sum b_iX_i)=\sum b_i^2\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\) for independent data.
  3. Large-\(n\) distribution: by the CLT it is approximately normal, centred at \(\theta\) with variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta)\).

Seen in: 2024 Q2 [12 marks] — unbiasedness & variance of \(\widehat\theta=-\tfrac1n\sum\log X_i\). 2023 Q2 [10 marks] — \(\widehat\theta=\tfrac1n\sum X_i/y_i\). Tutorial 6 Q1 — bias of the MLE variance.

Type G — CI theory / interpretation (words, not numbers)

Looks like: "Formally define the confidence level via probability statements; state what is fixed and what is random." Marks: 2–4.

Recipe: write \(\mathbb P(\bar X - q\,S/\sqrt n \lt \mu \lt \bar X + q\,S/\sqrt n)=1-\alpha\); say the endpoints are random and \(\mu\) is a fixed constant; distinguish \(\sigma\) known (\(z\)) vs unknown (\(t_{n-1}\)).

Seen in: Tutorial 6 Q2 & Q3. Frequently a 2-mark sub-part after a numeric CI (e.g. 2025 Q5(e) asks how CI width behaves as \(n\to\infty\)).

5 · Wording triggers  (the flagship — read this twice)

Exam phrasing maps to a method almost mechanically. Learn the left column and the right column follows.

When the exam says…→ do this

"interpret the interval" / "in context"Name the parameter in plain words + units + a study conclusion (does it exclude 0 / the claimed value?). Generic "the mean is in the interval" loses the context mark.

6 · Traps & marks lost

"Probability" instead of "confidence". "There's a 95% probability \(\mu\) is in the interval" is the classic zero-mark interpretation. Once the numbers are in, \(\mu\) is fixed — nothing is random. Say "we are 95% confident".
Using \(z\) when you need \(t\) (or vice-versa). Mean CI on this course → \(t_{n-1}\) (formula sheet item 62), even for large \(n\). Proportion CI → \(z\) (normal). Known-\(\sigma\) mean → \(z\). Getting this wrong is small numerically but flagged as a method error.
Forgetting the \(\sqrt n\). The margin of error uses the standard error \(s/\sqrt n\), not the sample SD \(s\). Dividing by \(n\) instead of \(\sqrt n\), or not dividing at all, is the most common arithmetic slip.
Chi-squared quantiles swapped. Lower bound uses the upper quantile \(q_2\); upper bound uses the lower quantile \(q_1\). If your "lower" bound is bigger than your "upper", you crossed them.
CI for \(\sigma^2\) vs \(\sigma\). The formula produces a variance interval. If the SD was asked, take square roots of both endpoints — and only at the end.
Two-sample SE errors. It is \(S_p\sqrt{\tfrac1m+\tfrac1n}\) (pooled) or \(\sqrt{\tfrac{S_X^2}{m}+\tfrac{S_Y^2}{n}}\) (Welch) — NOT \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{m+n}}\). And the pooled df is \(m+n-2\), not \(m+n\).
Proportion validity not checked. If asked to justify the method, state \(n\widehat p\ge5\) and \(n(1-\widehat p)\ge5\). Skipping the check when it's requested loses a mark.
Sample size rounded down. \(n=356.99\) means you need \(357\), not \(356\). Always round up — the requirement is an upper bound on the error.
Significant figures. "Give at least 3 significant figures" is on the front of every paper. If a part says "two decimal places", obey that exactly. Carry full precision through the working; round only the final answer.
Generic interpretation. "The mean lies between a and b" earns the number mark but not the context mark. Name the actual quantity (e.g. "mean 12-month weight change on the low-carb diet") with units, and state the study implication.
Pooled vs Welch not justified. When a question (like 2025 Q3(d)) asks you to state whether you pooled, give a one-line reason from the sample SDs or the plots — don't just pick one silently.

7 · Self-check

Attempt before revealing. Numbers verified with scipy.

Q1. Distinguish "estimate" and "estimator", and say which one has a standard error.

An estimate is a fixed number from your data (e.g. \(\bar x=75.98\)); an estimator is the random variable \(\bar X=\tfrac1n\sum X_i\) built from the random data. The estimator has a distribution, so it has a standard error \(=S/\sqrt n\). You can't attach a spread to a single observed number.

Q2. \(n=51\), \(\bar x=1136\), \(s=178\). Give a 95% CI for the mean, and say whether \(z\) or \(t\) is "correct" on the formula sheet.

Formula-sheet item 62 uses \(t_{n-1}=t_{50}\). \(t_{0.975,50}=2.01\), SE \(=178/\sqrt{51}=24.9\), ME \(=2.01\times24.9=50.1\) → CI \((1085.9,\,1186.1)\). (Using \(z=1.96\) instead — the notes' original version — gives \((1087,\,1185)\); the difference is tiny because \(t_{50}\approx z\).) On this exam, reach for the \(t\) row.

Q3. A juice company samples 27 bottles; \(s=5.86\) ml. Give a 95% CI for the variance \(\sigma^2\).

Recipe ② with df \(=26\): \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,26}=13.8\), \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,26}=41.9\). \((n-1)s^2=26\times5.86^2=892.6\). CI \(=(892.6/41.9,\;892.6/13.8)=(21.3,\;64.7)\) ml². (For the SD: \((4.62,\,8.04)\) ml.)

Q4. Nightmares: 55 of 160 men and 60 of 192 women report frequent nightmares. 95% CI for \(p_{\text{men}}-p_{\text{women}}\)?

Recipe ⑥. \(\widehat p_X=0.344\), \(\widehat p_Y=0.313\), diff \(=0.031\). SE \(=\sqrt{0.344(0.656)/160+0.313(0.687)/192}=0.0503\). ME \(=1.96\times0.0503=0.099\). CI \(=(-0.067,\;0.130)\). Contains 0 → no evidence men and women differ.

Q5. Why do we divide the sample variance by \(n-1\) and not \(n\)?

Because dividing by \(n\) gives a biased estimator: \(\mathbb E\big(\tfrac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\big)=\tfrac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\), systematically too small (deviations are taken from the fitted \(\bar X\), which hugs the data more tightly than the true \(\mu\)). Multiplying by \(\tfrac{n}{n-1}\) makes \(S^2\) unbiased: \(\mathbb E(S^2)=\sigma^2\).

Q6. A pilot 95% CI for a mean is \(16.75\pm7.39\) from \(n=4\). What \(n\) is needed for a margin of error \(\le 2\)?

Recover \(s\): half-width \(7.39=t_{0.975,3}\,s/\sqrt4=3.18\,s/2\Rightarrow s=4.64\). Then \(n\ge(1.96\times4.64/2)^2=20.7\Rightarrow n=21\). (Round up.)

Q7. A 95% CI for a difference in means comes out \((-2.1,\,4.8)\). What do you conclude?

The interval contains 0, so at the 5% level there is no evidence of a real difference between the two group means — the observed gap is consistent with sampling variability.

Q8. Which CIs require a normality assumption, and which are rescued by large \(n\)?

The mean CI (item 62) is exact under normality but the CLT rescues it for large \(n\), so normality is not essential when \(n\) is big. The proportion CIs rely on the CLT (need \(\ge5\) successes/failures). The chi-squared CI for \(\sigma^2\) genuinely needs normal data — the CLT does not save it — so always state that assumption there.

WE-A1 — Espresso extraction time (95% CI)

WE-B1 — Two keyboards, typing speed (pooled, 95%)
Ten typists test a mechanical keyboard \(X\) (\(\bar x=78\) wpm, \(s_X=6.0\)); ten different typists test a membrane keyboard \(Y\) (\(\bar y=71\) wpm, \(s_Y=6.4\)). The two sample SDs are almost identical, so treat the variances as equal. Give a 95% CI for \(\mu_X-\mu_Y\).

  4. \(\bar x-\bar y=78-71=7\). ME \(=2.10\times2.774=5.83\).

WE-B2 — Two fertilisers, tomato yield (Welch, unequal variances, 95%)

  1. \(\operatorname{s.e.}=\sqrt{\dfrac{0.560(0.440)}{300}+\dfrac{0.470(0.530)}{300}}=\sqrt{0.001652}=0.0406\).
  2. Diff \(=0.560-0.470=0.090\). \(q=1.96\). ME \(=1.96\times0.0406=0.0797\).
  3. CI: \(0.090\pm0.0797=(0.010,\;0.170)\).

Answer: \((0.010,\;0.170)\). Excludes 0 (just), so at the 5% level there is evidence app \(X\) has a higher binge rate than app \(Y\) — by roughly 1 to 17 percentage points.

YT-C3 STRETCH (3 marks) — when the method breaks
A rare-defect audit finds \(3\) faulty units in a batch of \(25\) (\(\widehat p=0.12\)). A colleague reports the "95% CI" as \((-0.007,\;0.247)\). What is wrong, and what should you do?
Show solution
Two red flags:
  1. Validity fails. The normal approximation needs \(\ge5\) successes and \(\ge5\) failures. Here \(n\widehat p=25\times0.12=3\lt5\), so the recipe-⑤ formula is not trustworthy.
  2. Impossible endpoint. The lower limit \(-0.007\) is negative, but a proportion cannot be below 0 — a tell-tale sign the symmetric normal interval has been pushed outside \([0,1]\).
What to do: report that the large-sample normal CI is invalid here, and collect more data (or use an exact/Clopper–Pearson-type binomial interval, beyond this course's formula sheet).

Answer: fewer than 5 successes ⇒ normal-approx assumption violated; the negative lower bound confirms it. Don't quote the interval.

④ CI for \(\sigma^2\) or \(\sigma\) — the chi-squared ambush

This is the one people forget to revise. The word variance, standard deviation, variation, or variability is your trigger for the \(\chi^2\) recipe ② — and the big quantile goes under the lower bound.

📖 Learn from these

WE-D1 — 3D-printer layer thickness (CI for variance, 95%)
A 3D printer's layer thickness (mm) is measured on \(n=20\) layers, giving \(s=0.08\) mm. Assume normality. Construct a 95% CI for the variance \(\sigma^2\).
Recognise: one sample, variability, normal → recipe ② (\(\chi^2_{n-1}\), item 63).
  1. df \(=19\). Quantiles of \(\chi^2_{19}\): \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,19}=8.91\) (lower), \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,19}=32.85\) (upper).
  2. \((n-1)s^2=19\times0.08^2=19\times0.0064=0.1216\).
  3. CI for \(\sigma^2\): \(\left(\dfrac{0.1216}{q_2},\;\dfrac{0.1216}{q_1}\right)=\left(\dfrac{0.1216}{32.85},\;\dfrac{0.1216}{8.91}\right)=(0.00370,\;0.01365)\) mm².

Answer: \(\sigma^2\in(0.00370,\;0.01365)\) mm².

The big quantile \(q_2=32.85\) divides to make the lower bound; the small quantile \(q_1=8.91\) makes the upper bound. Swap them and the interval comes out inside-out.
WE-D2 — Archery arrow spread (CI for SD, 95%)
An archer's arrow-to-centre distances (cm) for \(n=15\) shots have \(s=3.2\) cm. Assume normality. Give a 95% CI for the standard deviation \(\sigma\).
Recognise: SD asked → build the variance CI (recipe ②) then square-root.
  1. df \(=14\). \(\chi^2_{14}\): \(q_1=\chi^2_{0.025,14}=5.63\), \(q_2=\chi^2_{0.975,14}=26.12\).
  2. \((n-1)s^2=14\times3.2^2=14\times10.24=143.36\).
  3. CI for \(\sigma^2\): \(\left(\dfrac{143.36}{26.12},\;\dfrac{143.36}{5.63}\right)=(5.49,\;25.47)\) cm².
  4. CI for \(\sigma\): \(\left(\sqrt{5.49},\;\sqrt{25.47}\right)=(2.34,\;5.05)\) cm.

Answer: \(\sigma\in(2.34,\;5.05)\) cm.

Assumption: the distances are an iid sample from a normal distribution. Unlike the mean CI, the CLT does not rescue this one — normality genuinely matters.

⑤ Margin of error & sample size

📖 Learn from these

WE-E1 — E-scooter range (mean sample size)
An e-scooter maker wants to estimate the mean range (km) on a full charge to within a 95% margin of error of \(1\) km. A pilot suggests \(\sigma\approx4.5\) km. What is the smallest number of scooters to test?
Recognise: "margin of error no more than …" → sample-size recipe ⑦ (mean).
  1. Require \(z\,\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le E\) with \(z=1.96\), \(\sigma=4.5\), \(E=1\).
  2. \(n\ge\left(\dfrac{z\sigma}{E}\right)^2=\left(\dfrac{1.96\times4.5}{1}\right)^2=(8.82)^2=77.79\).
  3. Round up: \(n=78\).

Answer: 78 scooters.

WE-E2 — Campus survey (proportion sample size, no pilot)
A student union wants a 95% CI for the proportion who commute by bike, with margin of error \(\le 2.5\%\). No prior estimate of \(p\) is available. How large must the sample be?
Recognise: proportion, worst-case → recipe ⑦ with \(p(1-p)\le\tfrac14\).
  1. \(n\ge\left(\dfrac{z}{2E}\right)^2\) with \(z=1.96\), \(E=0.025\).
  2. \(n\ge\left(\dfrac{1.96}{2\times0.025}\right)^2=\left(\dfrac{1.96}{0.05}\right)^2=(39.2)^2=1536.6\).
  3. Round up: \(n=1537\).

Answer: 1537 students. Using worst-case \(p=0.5\) means you can size the survey before collecting any data.

YT-E3 STRETCH (4 marks) — recover \(\sigma\) from a pilot, then size up
A pilot 95% CI for a mean, from \(n=9\) normal observations, came out as \((11.4,\;14.6)\). A full study wants margin of error \(\le0.5\) at 95%. How many observations are needed?
Show solution
Step 1 — recover \(s\) from the pilot. Half-width \(=\tfrac12(14.6-11.4)=1.6\); pilot df \(=8\), \(q=t_{0.975,8}=2.31\). \[1.6=2.31\cdot\frac{s}{\sqrt9}=2.31\cdot\frac{s}{3}\ \Rightarrow\ s=\frac{1.6\times3}{2.31}=2.08.\] Step 2 — size the full study (use \(z=1.96\), \(\sigma\approx s=2.08\)): \[n\ge\left(\frac{1.96\times2.08}{0.5}\right)^2=(8.15)^2=66.6\ \Rightarrow\ n=67.\]

Answer: 67 observations. The pilot's job was just to supply a working \(\sigma\); everything after is recipe ⑦.

⑥ Estimator properties & CI theory (concepts)

📖 Learn from these

WE-F1 — Which interpretation is correct? (MCQ)
A 95% CI for a population mean download speed is computed as \((48.2,\;53.6)\) Mbps. Which statement is correct?
  A. There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) lies in \((48.2,\;53.6)\).
  B. 95% of individual users have a download speed between 48.2 and 53.6 Mbps.
  C. If the study were repeated many times, about 95% of the intervals produced this way would contain \(\mu\).
  D. 95% of sample means fall in \((48.2,\;53.6)\).
Answer: C. Why the others fail:
  • A — once the numbers are in, \(\mu\) is a fixed constant and the interval is fixed; nothing is random, so "probability" is wrong. Say "confident".
  • B — that describes a prediction interval for individuals (far wider, \(\propto S\)), not a CI for the mean (\(\propto S/\sqrt n\)).
  • D — the CI is built around this \(\bar x\); it isn't a statement about where other sample means land.
WE-F2 — Comparing two unbiased estimators (efficiency)
\(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Consider two estimators of \(\mu\): the full mean \(\widehat\mu_A=\bar X=\tfrac1n\sum X_i\), and the "endpoints-only" average \(\widehat\mu_B=\tfrac12(X_1+X_n)\). Show both are unbiased and say which you'd prefer for \(n\gt2\).
Unbiasedness (linearity of expectation): \[\mathbb E(\widehat\mu_A)=\tfrac1n\sum\mathbb E(X_i)=\tfrac1n(n\mu)=\mu,\qquad \mathbb E(\widehat\mu_B)=\tfrac12(\mu+\mu)=\mu.\] Both unbiased. Variance (independence ⇒ variances add, and \(\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}X\)): \[\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu_A)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu_B)=\tfrac14(\sigma^2+\sigma^2)=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}.\] For \(n\gt2\), \(\sigma^2/n\lt\sigma^2/2\), so \(\widehat\mu_A\) has the smaller variance.

Prefer \(\widehat\mu_A=\bar X\): it is unbiased and more efficient (smaller variance) for every \(n\gt2\), because it uses all the data, not just two points.

✏️ Your turn

YT-F1 WARM-UP (2 marks) — confidence vs probability
In one sentence, explain why it is wrong to say "there is a 95% probability the population mean lies in my computed interval \((7.1,\;9.3)\)".
Show solution

Once the data are observed, both the interval endpoints and the parameter \(\mu\) are fixed numbers — \(\mu\) is either in \((7.1,9.3)\) or it isn't, with no randomness left. The 95% refers to the long-run capture rate of the procedure across many repeated samples, so we say we are "95% confident", not that there is a 95% probability.

YT-F2 STANDARD (3 marks) — width: two dials at once
A 95% CI for a mean has half-width (margin of error) \(2.0\). Holding everything else fixed, what happens to the half-width if you (a) switch to 99% confidence, and (b) instead quadruple the sample size? Which dial can shrink the interval without a trade-off?
Show solution
  1. (a) 95%→99%: \(q\) grows (\(z\) roughly \(1.96\to2.58\)), so the half-width grows by a factor \(\approx2.58/1.96\approx1.32\) → about \(2.0\times1.32=2.6\). More confidence costs precision.
  2. (b) \(n\to4n\): the s.e. \(=S/\sqrt n\) is divided by \(\sqrt4=2\), so the half-width halves → \(1.0\). This is the \(1/\sqrt n\) law: quartering the width needs 4× the data.

Answer: raising confidence widens (a trade-off); raising \(n\) is the only lever that tightens the interval without sacrificing confidence. Only more data buys precision for free.

YT-F3 EXAM-LEVEL (4 marks) — is this estimator any good?
\(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). A student proposes estimating \(\mu\) by \(\widehat\mu=\dfrac{X_1+2X_2}{3}\). (a) Is it unbiased? (b) Find \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu)\). (c) Compare it to \(\bar X\) for the same \(n\) (take \(n=2\)) and comment.
Show solution
(a) Unbiased? \(\mathbb E(\widehat\mu)=\tfrac13\big(\mathbb E X_1+2\mathbb E X_2\big)=\tfrac13(\mu+2\mu)=\mu\). Yes — unbiased (the weights \(\tfrac13,\tfrac23\) sum to 1).
(b) Variance (independence ⇒ add; pull out squared coefficients): \[\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu)=\left(\tfrac13\right)^2\sigma^2+\left(\tfrac23\right)^2\sigma^2=\tfrac19\sigma^2+\tfrac49\sigma^2=\tfrac59\sigma^2\approx0.556\,\sigma^2.\]
(c) Versus \(\bar X\). With \(n=2\), \(\bar X=\tfrac12(X_1+X_2)\) has \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\tfrac12\sigma^2=0.5\,\sigma^2\). Since \(0.5\lt0.556\), \(\bar X\) is more efficient — both are unbiased, but the equal-weights mean has the smaller variance.

Answer: (a) unbiased; (b) \(\tfrac59\sigma^2\); (c) \(\bar X\) beats it (variance \(\tfrac12\sigma^2\lt\tfrac59\sigma^2\)) — unequal weights waste information. In fact, among unbiased linear estimators, equal weights (\(\bar X\)) always minimise the variance.
